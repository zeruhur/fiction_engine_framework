# Fiction Engine Framework
Roberto Bisceglie
2026-05-01

# The Bet

*A note on terms: this collection of essays uses several concepts
precisely enough that imprecision creates confusion. Fiction means the
shared diegetic situation, not narrative craft. Fictional state means a
condition inside the fiction visible to the characters within it, not a
note in the game record. Overhead means procedural steps that produce
neither fictional change nor the conditions for it — a narrower category
than it might appear. These are not terms in their everyday senses. A
glossary at the back defines each one; the essays will earn the terms as
they use them.*

“Narrative game” is too broad to be analytically useful.

Games grouped under that label differ in where they place authority, how
they resolve uncertainty, and how much interpretive labor they require.
A game where a referee adjudicates outcomes freely against the situation
is doing something structurally different from one where codified move
triggers govern what the fiction can produce. A game where players hold
authorial leverage over their story’s shape through a metacurrency is
doing something structurally different again from one where all
fictional changes emerge from in-world cause and effect. None of these
differences is captured by “narrative.” The label names a loose family
resemblance — these games care about story in some sense — without
describing what any of them actually does or how it does it.

A fiction engine is the design family defined by one structural
commitment: every rule exists to serve the fiction, and any rule that
fails that test is a candidate for removal. That commitment is not
stylistic. It applies to every design decision — resolution procedures,
character traits, pacing tools, uncertainty mechanisms — and the test
applies to each one individually and to the system as a whole. The label
is a tool for precision, not a claim that this family is superior to
others. It names one design approach, with a specific logic and specific
costs, in order to describe that approach clearly enough to reason about
it.

The rest of this collection is what the commitment means in practice.
But two words in it — rules and fiction — are doing more specific work
than they appear to, and the argument depends on keeping them precise.

## What fiction means

In what follows, fiction does not mean literary craft, narrative
quality, or storytelling in the writerly sense. It means the shared
situation: the characters, places, events, and conditions that all
participants hold in common during play and treat as real within the
game. It is the living, mutable situation that play produces and
transforms, moment to moment. When a character forces a door and it
gives way noisily, alerting the guard upstairs — that is a change to the
fiction. When the player marks off two hit points and the scene
continues exactly as it would have without that mark — that is not.

This distinction is the load-bearing one. A fictional state is any
condition inside the shared situation that the characters can see,
respond to, and reason about. A character’s exhaustion is a fictional
state when it makes her slow, when the other characters notice, when it
shapes what she can plausibly attempt. It stops being a fictional state
when it is only a number on a sheet that modifies future rolls without
changing how the situation reads inside the world. The test is simple:
remove the game record and ask whether the condition still exists inside
the fiction as something participants can respond to. Fictional states
survive that removal. Administrative records do not.

Everything in this collection follows from that test.

## The single structural commitment

A fiction engine takes that test seriously and applies it to every rule.

The design family is defined by one commitment: every rule exists to
serve the fiction, and any rule that fails that test is a candidate for
removal. This does not mean the game must be simple, or spare, or
without texture. A rule can be intricate and still pass the test,
provided it produces fictional change or supports the conditions under
which fictional change can occur. What the commitment refuses is a third
category: rules that produce neither, that exist as administrative
overhead between fictional intent and fictional consequence.

The core operation a fiction engine runs is: the player states what
their character attempts within the fiction; the game produces an
outcome; that outcome modifies the fiction; play continues. The
procedural layer in the middle should be as thin as the situation
allows. Not absent — uncertainty and consequence both require procedure.
Thin. The rules handle the friction between intent and outcome, then get
out of the way.

Three examples show where the distinction does its most useful work.

**A rule that belongs.** When you force a locked door, roll. On a strong
success, it opens cleanly. On a partial success, it opens but alerts
someone nearby. On a failure, the lock breaks and something approaches.
Each result is a new fictional state. The player knows immediately what
the situation has become and how play continues.

**A rule that doesn’t.** Track door integrity across five damage boxes.
Once all boxes are filled, make a secondary roll to determine whether
the door opens or jams. The procedure produces a count, then another
roll, before the fiction changes. Neither step is a fictional event.
Both are administrative steps interposed between fictional intent and
fictional consequence.

**The borderline case — where the framework does its most useful work.**
A stress track with no direct expression inside the fiction: no
condition the other characters can observe. But as it fills, the
character’s actions become constrained in ways that match a
deteriorating state. The track is non-diegetic in form — it exists on
the sheet, not in the world — but fiction-supporting in function. It is
preserving the coherence of a condition the fiction needs. Whether it
belongs in a fiction engine depends on whether its administrative cost
is proportionate to the fictional coherence it maintains. That is a
design judgment, not a rule violation.

The borderline case matters because it prevents the test from becoming a
blunt instrument. The question is never whether a rule looks mechanical.
It is whether the rule is carrying its weight, and in what currency.

## How outcomes work

A fiction engine treats outcomes as continuations, not endpoints.

The productive question after any resolution is not simply whether an
action succeeded. It is: what new fictional state does this result
create, and how does play proceed from there? The full range of outcomes
— yes and, yes but, no but, no and — each forces the fiction to keep
changing. Each opens the next move rather than closing the last one.

A result that leaves the fiction suspended rather than transformed is a
design problem, not a neutral state. A resolution chain that produces a
number, then requires another roll to interpret the number, then
requires the referee to translate the interpretation into fictional
terms, has three suspension points where one would be enough. Each step
is a moment where the fiction waits on the procedure. The procedure
should be doing the waiting instead.

## What can go wrong

A fiction engine fails in characteristic ways.

**Overhead accumulation.** Rules that generate tracking obligations
without producing fictional output are overhead. Individually each may
seem minor. Cumulatively, they shift the table’s attention from the
situation to the system. The engine stalls not through a single bad rule
but through the weight of many marginal ones.

**Suspended outcomes.** Resolution that produces a result requiring
further resolution before play can continue breaks the fundamental
cycle. The fiction stops while the procedure runs. Run enough of those
stops in a session and the procedure has become the primary object of
attention.

**Authority drift.** Any mechanism that handles narrative authority — a
referee, an oracle, a randomized prompt system — can drift from
producing fictional events toward producing administrative prompts that
require significant interpretive work before the fiction can advance.
The oracle output that reads as a task (“find a way to resolve the
tension”) rather than an event (“the contact is already gone when you
arrive”) is the clearest case. The failure mode is the same across
configurations: the authority mechanism has become an obstacle between
fictional intent and fictional change rather than a conduit for it.

**Orientational divergence.** When participants hold different models of
what the game is doing — one treating it as a tactical puzzle, another
as character exploration, another as improvised theater — the engine
loses coherence even when the rules are functioning correctly. The
fiction fragments because participants are not holding it in common. No
rule can fix this, because no rule caused it.

## The social dimension

A fiction engine is a social agreement before it is a mechanical system.

It functions only when all participants are oriented toward the fiction
as the primary object of play. When that orientation holds, the rules
recede: participants engage with the situation, the situation
transforms, and the mechanics become infrastructure they are aware of
without attending to. When it breaks — when one player is running a
different game in their head — the procedures cannot compensate. The
rules formalize the agreement. They cannot substitute for it.

Designers reduce this risk by making the game’s orientation legible from
the first page: what kind of object this is, what it asks of the people
playing it, what it will produce. Players reduce it by stating their
expectations before play begins and by treating the fiction as a common
object. The diagnostic when it breaks is not “which rule is wrong” but
“which participant is somewhere else, and why.”

## One paradigm among several

Nothing in this argument implies that other design approaches are
deficient.

Some games intentionally foreground procedure because procedure itself
creates the experience: tension under managed risk, strategic identity,
the ritual weight of dramatic grammar. These are coherent design
achievements, optimized for things a fiction engine is not designed to
produce. Evaluating them using criteria borrowed from a different
paradigm produces bad analysis. Evaluating a fiction engine against
criteria built for a pressure economy produces equally bad analysis in
the other direction.

This is not a claim that all good tabletop RPG design should converge on
thin mediation. It is a precise description of one design commitment,
its structural logic, and the costs it accepts.

A fiction engine accepts, as the direct consequence of minimal overhead,
that it provides less structural scaffolding than paradigms built around
dramatic prescription or managed pressure. It provides no move list to
generate consistent dramatic rhythm. It provides no metacurrency to give
players authorial leverage over story shape. It generates no pressure
economy. Those are costs, and they are real. Tables that need the system
to provide momentum and structure will find a fiction engine sparse.

What it offers in return is a procedural layer thin enough to disappear
into the fiction — thin enough that the story appears to emerge through
play rather than being processed through procedure. That is the bet this
book is built around. The chapters that follow are what the bet costs,
what it produces, and how far it travels.

# The Transparency Threshold

*A companion essay to “Fiction Engine: A Design Framework for Tabletop
RPGs.” Also readable as a standalone piece following “Fiction Engines,
Pressure Engines, and World Engines.”*

## Abstraction, Mediation, and the Limits of Fiction Engine Design

The fiction engine framework makes a deliberate choice not to place
specific games on a map. It describes a paradigm’s commitments, failure
modes, and criteria without naming which systems belong inside it and
which do not. That restraint is principled. A framework that generates
taxonomy risks becoming a ranking, and a ranking is not what the
framework is trying to produce.

But the framework does raise a question it does not answer: how much
abstraction can exist in a design before the system stops being
fiction-transparent? The framework says that a rule can be non-diegetic
in form while still being fiction-supporting in function. It says that
the distinction between dead overhead and load-bearing infrastructure is
one of function rather than form. What it does not say is where the
boundary lies: at what point the accumulation of non-diegetic elements,
however individually justifiable, crosses into a different kind of
design.

This essay is an attempt to answer that question. It is comparative in
ways the framework is not, and it names specific paradigms and their
characteristic behaviors. The goal is not to rank those paradigms but to
locate the boundary more precisely: to describe what happens when a
design crosses it, and why that matters for both designers and players.

## Two Kinds of Abstraction

Not all abstraction works the same way in play, and the distinction
matters before anything else can be said clearly.

The first kind is abstraction of form. A hit point total is an
abstraction of form: it represents something that exists inside the
fiction (a character’s physical capacity to keep functioning) in a
notation that exists outside the fiction. The character’s condition is
diegetic. The number that tracks it is not. But in many contexts this
abstraction stays invisible during play. The player does not think “my
character has 14 hit points.” They think “she is badly hurt and one more
hit might finish her.” The number is infrastructure for that thought,
not an object of attention in itself.

The second kind is abstraction of procedure. A move trigger in a Powered
by the Apocalypse game is an abstraction of procedure: it takes
fictional action and classifies it into a predefined taxonomic category
before the fiction can advance. When a player “acts under fire” or “goes
aggro,” they are not simply describing what their character does. They
are identifying which procedural category that action falls into, and
that identification is a step the fiction must pass through before
producing an outcome. The procedure is not notation for a diegetic
thing. It is a required translation layer between fictional intent and
fictional consequence.

These two kinds of abstraction are different in kind, not just degree.
Abstraction of form can operate in the background of play; it does its
bookkeeping without demanding the player’s attention. Abstraction of
procedure cannot stay in the background: the player must engage it
consciously to move play forward. This distinction is where the
transparency threshold begins.

## The Threshold Defined

A system crosses the transparency threshold when the player’s attention
must shift from the fiction to the procedure in order to advance play.

This is not the same as being aware of mechanics. Every player of every
game is aware that mechanics exist. It is not the same as engaging with
rules: players engage with rules constantly without those rules becoming
objects of attention in themselves. The threshold is specifically
attentional and cognitive: it marks the point where the player’s primary
focus must redirect from the fiction (the situation, the characters, the
conditions inside the diegetic layer) to the system: the
classifications, the resource states, the procedural steps that govern
what the system will accept as valid input.

Below the threshold, procedure operates as background infrastructure.
The player stays inside the fiction and the rules respond. The rules are
the pipes; the fiction is what flows through them.

Above the threshold, procedure moves to the foreground. The player must
engage the system as an object in order to produce any fictional output
at all. The fiction and the system are both objects of attention, and
the player must manage their relationship.

Neither position is wrong. They produce different kinds of play. But
they are genuinely different, and the difference is not simply about how
many rules a game has.

## Where Paradigms Sit

With the threshold defined, it becomes possible to place different
design paradigms relative to it, not as a ranking but as a map of where
each paradigm’s primary attention demands fall.

*A note on what follows: if you are arriving at this essay through the
Zotiquest newsletter article “Fiction Engines, Pressure Engines, and
World Engines,” the paradigm descriptions below will be familiar
territory. Read them as anchors for the threshold argument rather than
as new material. The placement of each paradigm relative to the
threshold is the point, not the paradigm descriptions themselves. If you
are arriving through the design framework, what follows is a brief map
of the design landscape the framework sits inside.*

**FU and Loner** sit clearly below the threshold. The procedure is:
describe what you attempt, roll, interpret the result as a fictional
outcome, continue. There is no classification step. There is no taxonomy
of action types. The resolution procedure is thin enough to become
habitual almost immediately, which means it operates in the background
after very little play. The player’s attention stays on the fiction
because the rules do not require it elsewhere.

**PbtA** sits at the threshold, and this is worth being precise about.
PbtA games are fiction-first in their input structure: the fiction
triggers mechanics rather than mechanics triggering fiction. That is an
important design commitment. But the move structure introduces a
procedural foreground at the moment of resolution. The player must
identify which move applies, or whether any move applies, before the
fiction can advance. That identification is a classification step, and
it requires the player to hold the system’s taxonomy in mind while
reading the fiction. Experienced PbtA players internalize this quickly
and the step becomes nearly invisible. But it is structurally always
there, which is why PbtA sits at the threshold rather than clearly below
it. Fiction-first in input. Procedurally mediated in output.

**FitD** sits above the threshold by design, and Blades in the Dark is
the clearest case. Position and effect judgments, stress economy,
resistance rolls, clock management: these are not background
infrastructure. They are persistent objects of attention throughout
play. The system is designed to make risk legible, costly, and
strategically interesting, and it achieves this by keeping the procedure
visible. A player running Blades well is simultaneously inside the
fiction and actively managing a pressure economy. That dual attention is
not a flaw. It is the experience the game is designed to produce. But it
means Blades is not attempting fiction-transparency and should not be
evaluated as if it were.

**Fate** sits above the threshold more completely than FitD, because in
Fate the procedure is the explicit object of authorial attention.
Aspects and fate points are the primary design surface. Invoking an
aspect is not a background action: it is a deliberate act of narrative
leverage, a conscious claim about the fictional world’s relevance to a
mechanical outcome. Fate externalizes dramatic control and makes
authorship visible. This is internally coherent and serves players who
want explicit authorial agency over their story’s shape. But the fiction
does not flow through Fate’s mechanics; it flows through its authors’
decisions about when and how to engage those mechanics.

**OSR** presents an interesting case because it often sits below the
threshold without being a fiction engine in the sense the framework
describes. OSR games typically have thin procedures in service of world
interaction and player problem-solving rather than fictional flow. The
mechanics (reaction rolls, resource checks, save-or-die) are often
background infrastructure. But the primary object of attention is the
world as a puzzle and a danger, not the fiction as a mutable situation.
OSR and fiction engine designs share a value for procedural thinness
without sharing a goal for what that thinness is supposed to serve.

## The Player-Variable Threshold

The threshold is not fixed, and this is where the analysis becomes most
interesting.

The same game can sit on different sides of the threshold depending on
who is playing it and how long they have been playing it. A player
encountering PbtA move structure for the first time experiences it as
foreground: they are actively consulting the move list, identifying
categories, checking trigger conditions. A player who has run Apocalypse
World for two years has internalized that structure to the point where
it operates nearly invisibly. The move identification step has become
habitual, and habitual steps do not demand conscious attention in the
same way that novel steps do.

This means the transparency threshold is partly a function of
familiarity, and that has real consequences.

The first consequence is for game design. A game that places a high
attentional demand on new players but a low demand on experienced ones
has a significant learning threshold rather than a permanent
transparency problem. Many PbtA games are like this: the procedural
foreground is most visible during the first few sessions and fades as
the move structure is internalized. This is a design choice, and it is
worth naming as such. The learning cost is real, but it is amortized
over time.

The second consequence is for how games are introduced. If the
transparency threshold is player-variable, then the experience of a game
depends heavily on how it is taught. A game introduced through its
fiction (here is the situation, here is what your character might
attempt, here is what happened) will feel different at the table than
the same game introduced through its mechanics: here are the six stats,
here are the move categories, here are the roll triggers. Both
approaches eventually arrive at the same system, but they cross the
threshold in opposite directions. The first approach keeps the fiction
primary and allows the mechanics to emerge from play. The second
approach foregrounds the procedure from the start and asks the fiction
to fill in around it.

This has a practical implication that goes beyond advice about running
games. It suggests that the transparency threshold is a property of the
design-in-use, not of the design alone. A fiction engine that is poorly
introduced can feel procedurally heavy. A PbtA game that is masterfully
run can feel nearly frictionless. The threshold is real, but it is not
solely determined by the rules.

The third consequence is for solo play specifically, and it is one the
framework document acknowledges indirectly. A solo player has no
external facilitator to absorb procedural weight. In group play, a
skilled referee or experienced player can smooth the attentional
transitions: can hold the taxonomy in mind so others do not have to, can
manage the pressure economy in the background while other players stay
in the fiction. In solo play, every procedural step is the player’s
responsibility, which means the threshold is effectively lower for solo
design. A procedural foreground that a group can distribute becomes a
full cognitive load for one person. This is one reason why fiction
engine designs, with their thin procedural layers, are particularly
well-suited to solo play: they minimize the load that the solo player
has to carry alone.

## Load-Bearing Abstraction and Its Limits

The framework distinguishes dead overhead from fiction-supporting
infrastructure, and that distinction does real analytical work. But the
essay needs to press it further, because the category of
fiction-supporting infrastructure can expand to absorb almost anything
if it is not bounded.

Consider Blades in the Dark clocks. A clock is a non-diegetic element:
it is a pie chart divided into segments that tracks progress toward an
event. It is not something characters can see or touch inside the
fiction. But clocks reliably produce fictional pressure. When the clock
for the city watch response fills another segment, something changes
inside the fiction even if no character has noticed it yet. The clock is
doing fictional work while remaining non-diegetic in form. By the
framework’s definition of fiction-supporting infrastructure, it
qualifies.

But clocks in Blades are also persistent objects of attention. A player
managing multiple clocks simultaneously is doing real cognitive work
that exists outside the fiction: tracking segment counts, estimating
trigger distances, evaluating which clocks to prioritize. That cognitive
work pushes the design above the threshold even though the clocks are
producing fictional output. The abstraction is load-bearing and it is
also foregrounded.

This reveals the limit of the fiction-supporting infrastructure
category: a rule can be simultaneously fiction-supporting in function
and procedurally foregrounded in use. These are not mutually exclusive.
The distinction between dead overhead and load-bearing infrastructure
tells you whether a rule is worth having. The transparency threshold
tells you what kind of experience it produces while you have it.

Both questions matter. The fiction engine framework answers the first.
The threshold answers the second. A design that sits below the threshold
will tend to contain only background infrastructure: mechanics that do
their work without demanding conscious attention. A design that sits
above the threshold may contain deeply fiction-supporting mechanics that
nevertheless require active management. Blades is a system full of the
latter. It is not dead overhead. It is high-cost fiction-supporting
infrastructure, and whether that cost is worth paying is a question each
designer and each table answers for themselves.

## What Lives Above the Threshold

The essay would be dishonest if it implied that fiction-transparency is
the only legitimate design goal, or that everything above the threshold
is a lesser achievement.

Foregrounded procedure produces experiences that a fiction engine
cannot. Blades in the Dark generates the tension of managed risk,
calibrated expenditure, and strategic decision-making under pressure, a
tension that emerges precisely because the pressure economy is visible
and actively managed. If the stress track became invisible
infrastructure, the experience of spending stress would lose its weight.
The foreground is the point.

PbtA’s move structure produces dramatic rhythm. The moment of move
identification is the moment where the fiction is sorted into meaning,
where the game says: this is the kind of thing that is happening, and
here is what that kind of thing costs and produces. That sorting is a
form of interpretation, and the game performs it structurally rather
than leaving it entirely to the table. For players who want consistent
dramatic coherence, that structure is not overhead. It is the engine.

Fate produces authorial satisfaction. The explicit negotiation over
aspects and fate points is a form of collaborative dramatic authorship
that cannot happen without the metacurrency layer. Removing it in favor
of thin mediation would not produce a better Fate. It would produce a
different game that no longer does what Fate is trying to do.

These are genuine design achievements. The transparency threshold marks
a boundary, not a hierarchy. Below the threshold, fiction engines
produce a specific kind of play: responsive, situational, lightly
framed, with the mechanics receding into the background and the fiction
remaining primary. Above the threshold, other paradigms produce other
kinds of play: structured, rhythmic, pressurized, explicitly authored.
Neither set of properties is superior. They serve different aims, appeal
to different players, and reward different kinds of engagement.

What matters is knowing which side of the threshold your design
inhabits, and inhabiting it deliberately.

## The Threshold as a Design Decision

The most common way to cross the transparency threshold is not through a
deliberate design choice but through accumulation. Each individual rule
seems justified. Each abstraction earns its place in isolation. The move
trigger provides clarity. The stress track provides pacing. The clock
provides tension. The metacurrency provides authorial control. None of
these additions is obviously wrong. But together they shift the table’s
primary attention from the fiction to the system, and at some point the
design has crossed the threshold without anyone having decided to cross
it.

This is the design failure that the fiction engine framework’s overhead
accumulation failure mode is pointing at. But overhead accumulation as
named in the framework refers to dead overhead: rules that produce no
fictional output. The more insidious version is fiction-supporting
overhead accumulation: rules that are each producing fictional value
while collectively driving the design above the threshold.

The diagnostic for this is not whether each rule is load-bearing (they
may all be). It is “what is the combined attentional cost of all the
load-bearing rules together, and is that cost consistent with the kind
of play this design is trying to produce?” A design aiming for
fiction-transparency cannot answer that question rule by rule. It has to
answer it for the system as a whole.

Conversely, a design that deliberately aims to sit above the threshold
should own that position fully rather than claiming fiction-first virtue
while building procedural foreground into every resolution step. The
most coherent designs in any paradigm are the ones that know what they
are and build toward it without apology.

The transparency threshold is not a test that designs pass or fail. It
is a location on a continuum, and knowing where your design sits, and
why, is the beginning of designing deliberately rather than by
accumulation.

# Four Designs, One Framework

*A follow-up to “The Transparency Threshold: Abstraction, Mediation, and
the Limits of Fiction Engine Design”*

## Applying the Fiction Engine Analysis in Practice

The previous essay defined the transparency threshold and placed design
paradigms relative to it in broad strokes. This essay works at a finer
resolution. It takes four specific games (*Freeform Universal*, *24XX*,
*Breathless*, and *Hints and Hijinx*) and applies the fiction engine
framework’s tools to each one in turn.

The four were not chosen arbitrarily. They share enough surface
properties to be grouped together under any conventional label: all four
are rules-light, three are solo-capable, and all four sit closer to the
fiction end of the design spectrum than PbtA, FitD, or Fate. A reader
encountering all four for the first time might reasonably treat them as
a family.

They are not a family. They make different tradeoffs, sit at different
positions relative to the transparency threshold, and serve different
design goals. Demonstrating precisely how they differ is what the
framework is for. They also arrive from different design genealogies:
*Freeform Universal* from the narrative indie tradition; *24XX* from the
OSR and FKR lineage, pushed to the point where it touches the fiction
engine paradigm from the other direction; *Breathless* as a direct hack
of *24XX*; and *Hints and Hijinx* building on the Breathless SRD. That
three separate traditions produce designs that cluster in similar
territory is itself worth examining.

## What “Rules-Light” Conceals

Before examining the four games individually, it is worth naming the
analytical problem they collectively illustrate.

“Rules-light” is as blunt a label as “narrative.” It describes a
quantity (fewer rules) without describing what the remaining rules do,
where authority sits, what procedural demands they place on the player,
or what kind of experience they are optimized to produce. A design can
be rules-light and still sit above the transparency threshold. A design
can be mechanically spare and still generate significant attentional
overhead through the combined weight of what it does track.

The fiction engine framework replaces the quantity question with a
function question. Not how many rules does this game have, but what does
each rule do: does it produce a new fictional state, support the
conditions for fictional play, or generate dead overhead? And the
transparency threshold adds a second question: even if a rule is
load-bearing, does it operate in the background or does it demand the
player’s conscious attention in order to advance play?

These two questions, applied consistently, produce meaningfully
different assessments of games that a quantity-based approach would
group together.

## 24XX: The Baseline Case

*24XX* sits closer to a pure fiction engine than any other design in
this comparison, and it is worth establishing why before the other three
introduce complications. It is also worth noting where it comes from:
not from the narrative indie tradition but from the OSR and FKR lineage,
where thin procedures serve world interaction and player problem-solving
rather than fictional flow. *24XX* takes that tradition’s commitment to
minimal mediation and pushes it until the design touches the fiction
engine paradigm from the other direction. The result is a design that
looks like a fiction engine and functions like one, while arriving from
a different set of values.

The core cycle is as thin as resolution gets without abandoning
uncertainty entirely. Players describe what their characters do. The GM
determines whether a roll is needed, and specifically, whether what they
are doing is risky. Only risk triggers resolution. The roll produces one
of three outcomes, each of which is immediately a fictional state:
something goes wrong, something partially works, something succeeds.
Play continues.

There is no classification step. There is no taxonomy of action types.
There is no required translation layer between fictional intent and
fictional consequence. The procedure is thin enough to become habitual
almost immediately, which means it operates in the background from very
early in play.

The design’s most elegant single rule is the defense mechanic: say how
one of your items breaks to turn a hit into a brief hindrance. The
mechanical action and the fictional event are the same action. There is
no administrative step between them. The player does not spend a
resource and then describe a consequence; they describe a consequence
and the mechanical effect follows automatically from the description.
This is fiction engine design at its most precise.

The GM instructions reinforce the orientation throughout. “Describe
characters in terms of behaviors, risks, and obstacles, not skill dice”
is a design constraint that keeps the referee’s output fictional rather
than procedural. The bad luck test, where the GM rolls to see if trouble
arrives, is a compact authority-distribution mechanism that produces
fictional events rather than administrative prompts.

One friction point exists: advancement. After a job, each character
increases a skill. The trigger (“after a job”) is an administrative
boundary rather than a fictional event. The advancement itself, a die
step increase, is non-diegetic tracking. This is minor, and common to
almost every design with any progression system, but it is the clearest
point where *24XX* generates a small amount of overhead that does not
immediately produce a fictional state.

What *24XX* gives up to stay below the threshold is worth naming. It
provides minimal genre texture beyond what the setting supplement adds.
It offers no pacing structure beyond the fictional situation itself. It
generates no pressure economy. Tables that need the system to provide
momentum and structure will find it sparse. These are not flaws. They
are the costs of the tradeoff, and the design accepts them fully and
without apology.

## Freeform Universal: The Ancestral Case

*Freeform Universal* is the design from which much of this lineage
descends, and examining it against the framework reveals something
interesting: the ancestor is not always the purest example of the
paradigm it established. FU is an excellent fiction engine, but it
carries one element that introduces a modest procedural foreground that
its descendants often remove.

The core cycle is clean. Describe what you attempt, roll a d6, interpret
the even/odd result as a fictional outcome on a six-point scale from
yes-and to no-and, continue. The result table is so simple it fits on
the character sheet and becomes habitual within minutes. No action
taxonomy, no classification step, no required translation between
fictional intent and fictional consequence.

The character system is as close to pure fictional description as a
structured game gets. Body, Mind, Edge, and Flaw are not stat blocks;
they are fictional descriptors, adjectives and short phrases that grant
narrative permission and modify dice by adding bonus or penalty dice to
the pool. Gear operates identically: an adjective and a noun, chosen for
fictional legibility, functioning as a modifier when relevant.
Conditions produced by outcomes are themselves fictional states (angry,
tired, injured, trapped) that apply mechanically by adding penalty dice
and fictionally by describing the character’s actual situation. The
mechanical state and the diegetic state are the same state, stated once.
This is exemplary fiction engine design.

The Narrator guidance reinforces the orientation throughout. “Make rolls
meaningful”: every roll should matter regardless of result, and failure
should never stop story momentum. “Say yes”: players introducing
fictional elements is encouraged rather than adjudicated. The explicit
instruction that only players ever roll dice keeps the Narrator’s
attention on the fiction rather than the procedure.

FU Points are where the analysis becomes more complex. FU Points are a
metacurrency: earned for good roleplaying and spectacular moments, spent
for bonus dice before a roll or to reroll after. This introduces a
non-diegetic resource pool that the player tracks and manages. It is not
Fate-style authorial leverage (FU Points do not shape narrative
directly) but they are tokens that exist outside the fiction as a
mechanical buffer. The intermediate states of a FU Point pool have no
mandatory diegetic expression. Three points versus one point is
mechanically significant but fictionally silent until spent.

The saving grace is the earning mechanism. FU Points are generated by
fictional behavior: doing something cool, making everyone laugh, playing
your character authentically. The resource is non-diegetic in form but
fiction-driven in generation. This is meaningfully different from a
metacurrency earned by administrative milestones. It keeps the FU Point
economy oriented toward the fiction even if the pool itself sits outside
it.

The design explicitly acknowledges this as a variable. Starting FU
Points, earning conditions, and spending options are all presented as
adjustable parameters. Groups can run FU with no FU Points at all, in
which case the design sits as clearly below the threshold as any game in
this comparison. Groups that use FU Points heavily introduce a light
metacurrency foreground. The threshold position is, by design, a dial
rather than a fixed point.

What FU demonstrates as the ancestral case is that the fiction engine
paradigm was established not by eliminating all non-diegetic elements
but by keeping them minimal, optional, and fiction-oriented in their
generation. Its descendants, including 24XX and the Breathless lineage,
each made different choices about which elements to preserve, remove, or
replace. Reading FU is reading the design space before those choices
were made.

## Breathless: The Pressure Engine with Fiction Engine DNA

*Breathless* is the most complex case in this comparison because it is
two designs in one: a fiction engine core carrying a pressure engine
structure. Understanding where one ends and the other begins is where
the analysis does its most useful work.

The check procedure inherits the fiction engine orientation cleanly.
Narrate what your character does, roll only when things are risky,
interpret the result as a fictional outcome. The GM telegraphs risk
before the roll. The outcome categories (fail with complication, succeed
with complication, succeed) all produce fictional states directly. No
classification step. No action taxonomy. In isolation, the check
procedure is a fiction engine procedure.

The die degradation mechanic is where Breathless makes its most original
contribution to this design space, and it is a contribution the
framework should acknowledge directly. Every roll steps the used die
down one rating. The degradation is simultaneously a mechanical state
and a fictional condition: the character is tiring, their resources are
depleting, their capacity is diminishing. The player does not track a
separate fatigue resource alongside the die rating; the die rating is
the fatigue resource, expressed in the same element that drives
resolution. Form and function are unified.

The catch your breath mechanic is equally precise. To reset all skill
dice to their original ratings, the player asks to catch their breath.
The GM immediately introduces a new complication. The administrative
action, resetting die ratings, is structurally coupled to a fictional
consequence: the situation changes. The player cannot restore their
resources without the fiction changing. This is the cleanest expression
of fiction-supporting infrastructure in this set of four designs:
non-diegetic in form, fiction-producing in function, and impossible to
engage without generating fictional output.

The stress track is where the friction begins. Four stress points lead
to vulnerability, at which point failure on a dangerous action risks
being taken out or sudden death. The threshold consequence is
fictionally legible. But the intermediate stress states (one point, two
points, three points) have no mandatory diegetic expression. A character
at three stress is mechanically closer to danger, but the rules do not
require that proximity to manifest as anything observable inside the
fiction. The player must perform their own interpretive translation,
which is exactly the kind of work the framework identifies as overhead
accumulation: a non-diegetic state that demands the player bridge it to
the fiction themselves.

The loot die adds a third degradation track alongside skill dice and the
stress count. Each element is individually justifiable: skill dice
represent capacity, the loot die represents scavenging fortune, stress
represents accumulated damage. But the combined attentional cost of
tracking all three simultaneously, plus backpack contents, which are
capped at three items plus one med kit, pushes the design above the
transparency threshold even though no single rule crosses it on its own.
This is the fiction-supporting overhead accumulation failure mode: rules
that are each load-bearing, whose combined weight shifts the player’s
primary attention from the situation to the resource economy.

The result is a design that sits just above the threshold by design. The
tension-and-release loop (skills depleting under pressure, the risky
decision of when to catch your breath, the GM complication that follows)
is the experience Breathless is optimized to produce. The tracking load
is the cost of that loop, and it is a cost the design pays deliberately.
This does not make Breathless a poorly designed fiction engine; it makes
it a well-designed pressure engine with fiction engine DNA in its core.
The distinction is worth maintaining precisely because it explains both
what Breathless does well and what it costs to do it.

For designers using the Breathless SRD as a base, including for solo
adaptations, this analysis has a practical implication. The core check
procedure and the die degradation and reset mechanics are the elements
most consistent with fiction engine principles and most worth
preserving. The stress track is the element most worth reconsidering or
replacing, depending on whether the target design wants to maintain the
pressure engine experience or move closer to the fiction engine side of
the threshold.

## Hints and Hijinx: When Genre Requires the Crossing

*Hints and Hijinx* is the most instructive case in this comparison
because its departure from fiction engine principles is not a design
failure. It is a genre requirement, deliberately implemented, and
understanding why it is necessary clarifies something the framework
cannot say on its own: some genres resist fiction engine treatment at
the structural level.

The Breathless core that the design inherits functions cleanly. The
two-skill system, Introvert and Extrovert, is minimal and fictionally
legible. The check procedure follows the established pattern: roll the
skill die, interpret the result as a fictional outcome, the die
degrades. The Refill Your Cup reset is the catch your breath equivalent,
triggering a narrative event rather than a free administrative action.
This section of the design sits below the transparency threshold.

The clue economy is where the design changes paradigm. Clues are
accumulated as a count. They are carried between locations as a resource
pool. They are spent in the Deduction phase to determine a die rating.
This is a metacurrency system. A clue is not a fictional state in the
framework’s sense; it does not exist inside the fiction as a condition
that characters can observe, respond to, or reason about. It is a token
that exists outside the fiction as a mechanical resource, and the player
tracks it, accumulates it, and spends it as such.

The Deduction Roll makes this fully explicit. The player writes a
hypothesis (a fiction engine act, a narrative statement about what
happened inside the fiction) but the die rating that determines whether
the hypothesis is correct is calculated from clue count rather than from
anything inside the fiction. The more clues used, the larger the die.
The hypothesis is assessed not by its internal fictional coherence but
by the size of the resource pool backing it. The fiction engine act of
writing the hypothesis is coupled to a metacurrency act of spending
tokens.

This is not a design flaw. Mystery investigation as a genre has
structural properties that resist pure fiction engine treatment. A
mystery requires accumulation: the gradual assembly of information
toward a revelation. It requires deduction: a moment where the
accumulated information is synthesized into a conclusion. And it
requires stakes at that moment of synthesis: the deduction must feel
consequential, and consequence requires that something is at risk. These
structural requirements push toward a progress-tracking economy almost
inevitably, because the fiction alone cannot reliably produce the sense
that enough information has been gathered to justify a conclusion.

The design solves this elegantly within its constraints: the
investigation phase uses fiction engine mechanics, and the deduction
phase uses metacurrency mechanics. The threshold is crossed not
gradually through accumulated overhead but at a specific structural
moment: the transition from investigation to deduction. Below the
threshold during investigation, above it during deduction. This is a
deliberate architectural decision, and it works for the genre.

The design raises a question for the framework: can mystery
investigation be served by a fiction engine without a clue economy? What
would a pure fiction engine mystery look like? The answer is probably
that it would look like a different kind of mystery, one where the
revelation emerges from play rather than being unlocked by accumulated
evidence. *Where the Wolves Are*, for instance, uses sealed events and
observational uncertainty to produce a mystery structure without a clue
economy. But that design produces a different mystery experience than
*Hints and Hijinx* is trying to produce. Genre requirements and design
goals are not separable.

## What the Comparison Reveals

Placing all four designs on the map the framework provides:

*Freeform Universal* sits below the threshold, with a variable margin
determined by how the table uses FU Points. Without FU Points the design
is as clean a fiction engine as this comparison contains. With FU Points
active, a light metacurrency foreground is introduced (manageable,
fiction-oriented in its generation, but present). The design presents
this as a dial rather than a fixed position, which is itself a design
philosophy worth naming.

*24XX* sits clearly below the threshold. Its procedural layer is thin
enough to operate as background infrastructure almost immediately, and
it achieves this partly by removing the FU Points economy entirely in
favor of a pure risk-resolution cycle. It pays for this with minimal
structural support and genre texture.

*Breathless* sits just above the threshold by design. Its die
degradation and reset mechanics are exemplary fiction engine design. Its
stress track and combined tracking load produce the pressure engine
experience the game is optimized for. The crossing is deliberate and
serves the design’s goals.

*Hints and Hijinx* crosses the threshold at a specific structural
moment, the transition to the deduction phase, because the mystery genre
requires it. Below the threshold during investigation, above it during
deduction. The crossing is architecturally contained rather than
diffuse.

The genealogy reading adds something the paradigm reading alone cannot.
*FU* arrives from the narrative indie tradition and establishes the
fiction engine paradigm through fictional descriptors, fiction-driven
outcomes, and an optional metacurrency. *24XX* arrives from a different
direction entirely (OSR and FKR design values) and crosses into fiction
engine territory by stripping mediation to its minimum in service of
situational consequence. *Breathless* is a direct hack of *24XX*, taking
its resolution core and adding a degradation economy that shifts the
design toward pressure engine territory. *Hints and Hijinx* inherits
that Breathless core and adds a clue economy on top, crossing the
threshold at a specific structural moment. Three different genealogies,
converging in the same design neighborhood while making meaningfully
different choices about what stays and what goes.

That convergence is worth naming. It suggests the fiction engine
paradigm is not the property of any single design tradition. It can be
arrived at from narrative indie roots, from OSR minimalism, or from
genre-specific adaptation. What the framework provides is a way to
describe where each arrival lands and what it costs to land there,
regardless of the route taken.

The comparison also reveals something the framework states abstractly:
fiction engine and rules-light are not the same thing. *Breathless* is
rules-light by any conventional measure, and it sits above the
threshold. *FU* is more mechanically specified than *24XX* in several
respects, and the FU Points economy introduces a variable the
OSR-derived design deliberately shed. The quantity of rules is a poor
predictor of transparency threshold position. The function and combined
attentional cost of the rules is what matters.

## The Solo Dimension

Three of the four designs in this comparison are solo-capable; *Freeform
Universal* is the exception, being designed for a group with a Narrator.
This asymmetry is itself analytically useful.

A solo player has no facilitation distribution available. In group play,
an experienced referee absorbs procedural weight: holds the result table
in mind, manages the fiction’s continuity, smooths attentional
transitions. Other players contribute to this distribution: someone at
the table who understands the system intuitively can carry load that
allows others to stay inside the fiction. None of this is available to a
solo player. Every procedural step is the solo player’s sole
responsibility.

This means the transparency threshold is effectively lower for solo
design. A procedural foreground that a group of four distributes across
multiple participants becomes a full cognitive load for one person. A
tracking system that feels lightweight in a group game can feel heavy in
solo play simply because there is no one to share it with.

FU’s group design context is worth noting here precisely because its
descendants were often adapted for solo play. The FU Points economy,
already a light metacurrency in group play, becomes a different kind of
cognitive demand when managed alone. This is one reason why *24XX*,
which removes that economy, handles solo play more cleanly than FU does
without adaptation. *Breathless*’s combined tracking load is more
significant in solo play than the design’s group context might suggest;
most of its 200+ derivatives are solo designs, which makes the stress
track’s overhead more consequential than it might appear. *Hints and
Hijinx* partially solves the problem by concentrating its
above-threshold mechanics in a single structural phase rather than
distributing them across the whole session.

The design question for solo fiction engines is whether the design is
thin enough for one player to carry alone, without the experience
becoming procedurally dominant. FU answers that question for groups. Its
descendants answer it, differently, for one player at a time.

## Conclusion: The Framework as a Diagnostic Tool

The fiction engine framework offers three uses: as a design checklist,
as an evaluative lens, and as a shared vocabulary. This comparison has
demonstrated the second most directly.

The evaluative lens does not produce rankings. *24XX* is not better than
*Breathless* because it sits further below the threshold. *Breathless*
is not better than *Hints and Hijinx* because its departure from fiction
engine principles is more gradual. Each design is better or worse at
producing the specific experience it is optimized for, and the
framework’s job is to describe what each design is optimized for and
what it costs to be optimized for that thing.

What the comparison reveals most clearly is that the fiction engine
paradigm is a specific design commitment with specific costs, not a
generic virtue that all good design shares. A design that stays below
the transparency threshold accepts structural looseness, minimal genre
texture, and limited pacing support in exchange for a fiction that
remains primary throughout play. A design that crosses the threshold
gains structural support, genre texture, and pacing economy, but at the
cost of a procedural layer that demands the player’s conscious
attention.

Knowing which side of the threshold your design inhabits, and why, is
more useful than claiming a generic fiction-forward identity. The four
designs in this comparison each know what they are. *Freeform Universal*
knows it is a flexible, group-oriented fiction engine with an optional
metacurrency dial. *24XX* knows it is the thinnest possible version of
that core. *Breathless* knows it is a pressure engine built on fiction
engine foundations. *Hints and Hijinx* knows it crosses the threshold at
a specific moment because its genre requires it.

# The Faction and the Fiction

*Part of the Fiction Engine companion essay series.*

## What Open Strategy Games Reveal at the Fiction Engine’s Edge

The fiction engine framework describes a player making fictional choices
about what their character attempts inside the diegetic layer. The
player asks what their character would do, the procedure produces an
outcome, the fiction changes, play continues. Everything orbits a
subject: a character, a protagonist, a perspective located inside the
shared imagined situation.

Open Strategy Games introduce a player making strategic choices about
what their faction does inside a geopolitical fiction. The player asks
what their faction should do given its leverage and objectives, the
Referee adjudicates, the Report changes what everyone knows, and play
continues. The fiction is present, shared, and primary. But nobody
inhabits it the way a character inhabits their situation. The subject of
play is a collective entity (a republic, a military force, a banking
consortium) that acts in the world without anyone living inside it.

Both are fiction-primary designs. The fiction they are primary toward is
not the same kind of fiction.

This essay is not asking whether OSG is a fiction engine. It is asking
something more interesting: what does the fiction engine framework
reveal when applied to OSG, and what does OSG reveal that the framework
does not currently address? Both questions have substantive answers, and
the second one has practical implications for how the framework handles
referee adjudication.

## What OSG Shares with the Fiction Engine Paradigm

Before locating the divergences, the shared ground deserves precise
description. OSG is not a fiction-adjacent design that happens to
involve a fictional world. Its core procedures are fiction engine
procedures operating at a different scale.

The action format (Action / Outcome / Leverage) requires every
submission to be grounded in established fiction. Leverage is not a
stat, a modifier, or a resource pool. It is a claim about the fictional
world: resources, relationships, positions, and prior actions that have
been established as facts in the fiction. The Referee assesses leverage
as a fiction reader asking whether the established fiction supports the
claim, not as a calculator running a resolution table. A player who
submits an action with strong leverage has done fictional work to
establish that leverage in prior turns. A player who submits an action
with weak leverage is asking the fiction to support something it has not
yet established. The distinction is entirely diegetic.

The RAT checklist (Reasonable, Actionable, Traceable) is quality control
for fictional output, stated in wargame language. A Reasonable outcome
is one proportionate to what the fiction has established. A Traceable
outcome is one that emerges from prior fictional events: you can point
to the decisions and conditions that led here. An Actionable outcome is
one that keeps the fiction moving rather than suspending it, seeding
hooks for other Actors to respond to. All three criteria are fiction
engine criteria. The framework’s insistence that outcomes must be
continuations rather than endpoints is the same requirement Traceable
and Actionable enforce together. The framework’s test (does this rule
produce a new fictional state?) is what Reasonable is checking.

“No Action Without Friction” maps directly onto the framework’s
prohibition on suspended outcomes. The explicit replacement of Hurdles
with complications (replace “no” with “yes, but”) is the yes-and,
yes-but, no-but, no-and structure of fiction engine resolution expressed
as a referee instruction rather than a dice table. Unopposed actions
succeed, with the Referee determining how: the fiction advances, the
Referee does not block it.

The Force of Nature mechanic (a chaotic external event introduced when
both dice show the same number) is structurally equivalent to a twist
mechanic: a fiction-producing event originating outside any player’s
control, emerging from threads already present in the fiction, creating
new conditions rather than resolving old ones. The Contested Ground SRD
is explicit that this is a prompt, not a requirement, and should be used
only when a good Force of Nature is available. That is exactly the
criterion a solo oracle should apply to a twist result.

What OSG shares with the fiction engine paradigm is the foundational
commitment: procedures exist to serve the fiction, and any procedure
that fails that test is a candidate for removal. The RAT checklist is,
among other things, a per-outcome application of the fiction engine’s
central design test.

## The Transparency Threshold in OSG

OSG sits above the transparency threshold. The question worth asking is
whether that position is deliberate, load-bearing, or incidental.

The action format requires conscious engagement with a three-part
structure before the fiction can advance. This is a classification step:
the player must identify an Action, separate it from an Outcome, and
provide Leverage grounded in the fiction before the Referee can
adjudicate. That step is not dead overhead; it is doing real fictional
work by forcing the player to think about why their plan should work
before asking the Referee to assess it. But it is procedurally
foregrounded. The player is not simply stating their fictional intent;
they are translating it into a structured submission format.

Private submission, Report reading, objective tracking, Turn Zero, and
the management of information asymmetry across turns are all persistent
attentional demands. A player in an OSG is simultaneously inside the
strategic fiction, reading positions, evaluating leverage, forming
alliances, and managing a procedural architecture. The dual attention is
the experience the form produces.

This is closest to the FitD case described in the previous essays:
procedure persistently foregrounded, not as a failure but as a design
commitment that produces something fiction-transparent designs cannot.
In FitD, the pressure economy creates tension. In OSG, the procedural
architecture creates the information asymmetry and strategic depth that
distinguish it from freeform diplomatic roleplay. Remove the action
format and private submission, and you remove the mechanism that makes
OSG a game rather than a collaborative fiction exercise.

The overhead is almost entirely load-bearing. The transparency threshold
is crossed deliberately, and the crossing serves the design’s goals. The
framework holds that a rule can be intricate and still belong to a
fiction engine, provided it produces fictional output rather than
administrative obligation. By that logic, OSG’s procedures qualify as
fiction-supporting infrastructure even while they push the design above
the threshold. What they produce is not dead overhead. What they produce
is a layered geopolitical fiction in which position, leverage, and
consequence interact over time.

## The Collective Subject Problem

The fiction engine framework assumes a diegetic layer populated by
individual agents: characters, protagonists, referees, oracle outputs
that describe what individual characters experience. Its player-facing
guidance is built around this assumption. The relevant question is what
the character would do. The diagnostic for fiction drift is whether the
player is thinking about what the rules allow rather than what the
character would do. The shared imagined space is a situation in which
individual agents with individual perspectives are embedded.

OSG has no such agents. The subject of play is a faction: a collective
entity with interests, leverage, and objectives, but no interiority in
the fictional sense. The Republic of Saivorn controls deep-water ports
and has no standing army. The Kingdom of Caldrath has the largest
standing army and significant debt to House Meranto. These are fictional
facts about collective entities, not descriptions of experiences or
intentions that any individual holds. No one is inside the fiction the
way a character is inside their situation. The fiction is experienced
from the outside, as a map of forces and consequences, a geopolitical
surface that the Actors operate across rather than inhabit.

This is a gap in the framework’s vocabulary. The framework describes
fiction as “the living, mutable situation that play produces and
transforms moment to moment.” That description fits OSG’s geopolitical
fiction; the situation is living, mutable, and produced by play. But the
framework’s description of what players do inside that situation (making
informed choices about what their character attempts) does not fit. OSG
players make informed choices about what their faction should do
strategically given what the fiction has established. The unit of agency
is collective, the register is strategic rather than experiential, and
the fiction is a contested surface rather than an inhabited world.

The framework’s focus on individual agents — characters with
perspectives, intentions, and embedded situations — is not inadvertent
narrowness. It is part of what defines the paradigm. OSG demonstrates
something different: that fiction primacy as a principle extends beyond
the fiction engine paradigm as a design family. A faction’s leverage is
as diegetic as a character’s skill. A treaty obligation is as fictional
as an injury. But the principle holding across both cases does not
require the fiction engine framework to extend its vocabulary to cover
collective agency. It requires recognizing that there is more than one
design family that can hold the principle, and that the framework
describes one of them with deliberate specificity.

## The Competitive Objective Structure

The fiction engine framework has no winning condition. Fiction engine
design produces uncertainty and consequence as generative forces. The
fiction moves because things keep changing, not because players are
working toward measurable outcomes. Fictional momentum is its own
justification: the situation develops, the oracle or referee or dice
produce a result, and play continues into whatever new situation
emerges. There is no external criterion against which that situation is
assessed.

OSG introduces objectives: paired short-term and long-term goals,
written into the Brief, self-assessed at game end. The Expectations
Formula holds two orientations in explicit tension: “play to win, play
to find out, both at once.” This is not a contradiction but a design
instruction. Players pursuing objectives produce the fictional pressure
that makes OSG’s fiction interesting. Without competitive stakes, the
fiction has no structural reason to escalate, complicate, or surprise.
Without fictional commitment, the competitive stakes become a resource
optimization problem that collapses the fiction into a calculation.

The tension is generative. Saivorn’s objective (secure a successor
treaty that guarantees commercial freedom) shapes every action Saivorn
takes, and those actions produce fictional consequences that other
factions must respond to. The objectives are not a metagame layer on top
of the fiction; they are the motivational structure that makes the
fiction a contest rather than a chronicle. The fiction would still be
produced without objectives, but it would be shapeless. Objectives give
the fictional situation stakes, and stakes give outcomes consequence.

This reveals something the framework does not address: competitive
structure can be a fiction-producing mechanism rather than a
fiction-distorting one, provided the fiction remains the medium through
which competition is resolved. OSG’s adjudication keeps competition
inside the fiction by requiring every action to be grounded in
established fictional facts and every outcome to pass the RAT test. The
competition is real; the resolution is always fictional. That
combination is what distinguishes OSG from a wargame, where competition
is resolved through calculation rather than fiction, and from pure
collaborative roleplay, where there is no competition to resolve.

A useful distinction emerges from this: objective-driven play and
fiction-driven resolution are not the same thing and do not conflict.
OSG keeps the first while requiring the second for everything that
matters. The fiction engine framework excludes winning conditions by
design; that exclusion is part of the paradigm’s definition, not a gap.
What this comparison clarifies is that fiction-driven resolution is
compatible with objective-driven play — which means designs outside the
fiction engine paradigm can share its resolution logic while making
different choices about goal structure. The boundary between the two
runs through the question of whether play is producing situations or
pursuing outcomes.

## The Layered Information Architecture

The fiction engine framework assumes a shared real-time fiction. All
participants hold the same diegetic layer in common at any given moment.
When the oracle produces a result, everyone hears it. When the referee
describes a consequence, everyone knows it. When a character acts, the
action is public to the table even if it is private within the fiction.
The shared imagined space is, by assumption, shared.

OSG introduces information asymmetry as a design feature. Each Actor
receives a Brief with private information that no other Actor sees.
Actions are submitted privately. The Report reveals what the Referee
chooses to reveal, which is not necessarily everything that happened.
Different Actors hold different versions of the same fictional world at
the same time. The Republic of Saivorn knows things about its own debt
situation that House Meranto does not know it knows. The Conclave holds
treaty clauses that have never been made public. The fiction is
partially opaque, and that opacity is load-bearing.

This is a radically different relationship to the shared imagined space.
In OSG, the fiction is never fully shared. It exists in layers: the
public fiction established by the Report, and multiple private fictions
held by individual Actors that interact with it. Intelligence,
deception, and strategic uncertainty are all produced by information
asymmetry built into the architecture, not improvised at the table.

The Report is the mechanism that periodically synchronizes the
partially-shared fiction without collapsing the asymmetry. It is written
in news-roundup style: outcomes chronicled as facts, hooks seeded for
future play, private outcomes accompanied by traceable crumbs that let
other Actors infer something happened without knowing what. The Report
is the most sophisticated authority distribution mechanism examined in
this essay series. It is simultaneously the public fiction, the shared
layer all Actors hold in common, and a carefully managed partial reveal
that preserves the strategic value of private information.

The fiction engine framework’s concept of the shared imagined space is a
design commitment, not an omission. Its oracle produces a shared output.
Its referee produces a shared description. The assumption that all
participants hold the same fiction in common is load-bearing: shared
real-time fiction is what makes fictional consequence legible to
everyone at the table simultaneously. OSG demonstrates that fiction can
be primary, load-bearing, and shared while also being layered,
asymmetric, and partially opaque. That is a different structural choice,
not a correction. The public Report and the private Brief are both parts
of the same fiction, and the game’s strategic depth depends on the gap
between them — a gap fiction engine play does not create because fiction
engine play is not trying to produce strategic depth of that kind.

## The RAT Checklist as a General Fiction Engine Tool

Of all the elements in OSG’s design, the RAT checklist deserves
particular attention from fiction engine designers. It is the most
exportable element in the form: a quality control mechanism for referee
adjudication that applies to any fiction engine game where a referee
mediates outcomes, not only to wargame-adjacent designs.

The framework currently offers the following guidance on outcomes: treat
them as continuations rather than endpoints; use yes-and, yes-but,
no-but, no-and to keep the fiction moving; avoid results that leave the
fiction suspended rather than transformed. This guidance is sound, but
it operates at the level of principle rather than practice. A referee in
a fiction engine game knows they should produce fictional continuations.
They do not always have a tool for checking whether a specific outcome
they have just written actually does that.

The RAT checklist provides exactly that tool. Adapted from OSG’s wargame
context into the broader fiction engine register:

**Reasonable.** Is this outcome proportionate to what the fiction has
established? Does it feel like something that would actually happen
given the situation, the characters, and the conditions in play? An
outcome that contradicts established fictional facts, assigns
consequences out of proportion to the action, or introduces elements the
fiction has not prepared the ground for fails this test. The fix is to
find an outcome that the fiction already supports: one that the
established situation makes plausible rather than requiring the fiction
to be bent to accommodate it.

**Actionable.** Does this outcome advance the fiction rather than
suspend it? Does it leave at least one participant something to respond
to, investigate, exploit, or contend with? An outcome that resolves
everything cleanly, eliminates a thread without replacing it, or
produces a state in which nothing is at stake fails this test. The fix
is not to make outcomes worse for the players but to ensure every
outcome opens at least one new question, pressure, or possibility. A
character who succeeds completely at their goal should find that success
complicates something they had not anticipated. A character who fails
should find that failure produces a new condition rather than a dead
end.

**Traceable.** Can you point to earlier events in the fiction that led
here? Does this outcome feel like a consequence of prior decisions
rather than something that arrived from outside the fiction? An outcome
that cannot be traced to established fictional facts, that depends on
information or conditions not yet established in the shared fiction,
fails this test. The fix is to either root the outcome in something that
has already been established, or to establish the relevant conditions
before producing the outcome.

The three tests work together in a specific way. Reasonable ensures the
outcome is supported by the fiction that exists. Traceable ensures the
outcome emerges from the fiction’s history rather than arriving from
outside it. Actionable ensures the outcome serves the fiction’s future
rather than closing it off. A referee who passes all three tests has
produced an outcome that is simultaneously grounded in what the fiction
is, what it has been, and what it needs to become.

What the RAT checklist adds to the framework’s existing guidance is the
Traceable criterion. The framework’s yes-and / no-but structure ensures
Actionable outcomes. The framework’s Reasonable test (is this outcome
proportionate and plausible?) is implicit in the concept of fictional
states. But Traceable is not currently named anywhere in the framework,
and it is doing distinct work. An outcome can be plausible and
actionable while still feeling arbitrary if it cannot be traced to prior
fictional events. Traceability is what makes an outcome feel like a
consequence rather than a contrivance. It is the difference between a
fiction that accumulates, where earlier decisions have weight and later
events feel earned, and a fiction that resets, where each turn’s
outcomes are disconnected from what came before.

The practical form of the RAT checklist for a fiction engine referee or
oracle interpreter might read:

Before accepting any resolution outcome, ask:

- Is this what the fiction supports? (Reasonable)
- Does this create something to respond to? (Actionable)
- Can I trace this to something that already happened? (Traceable)

If any test fails, rewrite the outcome before continuing. The fiction
engine equivalent of the OSG Referee’s mandate (do not publish a Report
that fails the RAT check) is a referee or player who does not accept an
oracle interpretation or GM ruling that fails these tests.

## Where OSG Sits

OSG is not a fiction engine. The transparency threshold is crossed
structurally, the subject of play is collective rather than individual,
and the competitive objective structure introduces a goal-directedness
that fiction engine design does not have. A player in an OSG is managing
a procedural architecture alongside the fiction in a way that the
fiction engine paradigm specifically works to eliminate.

But OSG is not a pressure engine or a metacurrency engine either. It
does not use codified move triggers, persistent resource economies, or
explicit authorial leverage. Its fiction is primary in a way that aligns
it with the fiction engine family of values while placing it outside the
fiction engine paradigm as the framework defines it.

The design occupies a region adjacent to the fiction engine paradigm,
sharing its foundational commitment to fiction primacy and fictional
consequence while operating on a collective subject, a competitive
objective structure, and a layered information architecture that the
framework was not built to describe. It is neither inside the map the
framework draws nor simply outside it. It is at the edge: recognizable
as sharing the same values, operating through different means.

The Expectations Formula names this position as precisely as any design
document can: play to win, play to find out, both at once. A fiction
engine asks only the second. OSG asks both, and finds that asking both
produces a different kind of fiction (a fiction of strategic forces,
contested positions, and layered information) that fiction engine
designs, in their commitment to individual agency and real-time shared
fiction, are not designed to produce.

## What Each Reveals About the Other

The fiction engine framework applied to OSG reveals that fiction primacy
is a broader principle than the framework’s current language captures. A
design can keep the fiction primary without keeping the subject of play
individual, the fiction fully shared, or the resolution oriented toward
individual consequence. The collective, the asymmetric, and the
strategic are all compatible with fiction primacy, provided every
procedure is tested against whether it produces fictional output rather
than administrative obligation.

OSG applied to the fiction engine framework marks three edges of the
paradigm. The framework’s commitment to individual character agency —
what the character does, experiences, and intends — is a defining
feature, not an oversight; designs whose subject of play is a collective
entity operate outside it by design. The framework’s exclusion of
winning conditions is equally deliberate; OSG demonstrates that
fiction-driven resolution is compatible with objective-driven play, but
the fiction engine paradigm does not combine them, and that is the
point. And the framework’s shared imagined space — its structural
assumption that all participants hold the same fiction in common — is a
load-bearing commitment, not a limitation; OSG’s layered, asymmetric
architecture makes different choices and produces different affordances.

These edges are not deficiencies. They are places where the framework’s
precision about one design space leaves adjacent design spaces
undescribed — deliberately, because describing them all would dissolve
the paradigm into a general theory of fiction-primary games. The fiction
engine framework is a map of one region. OSG is a landmark just outside
that region whose position helps clarify where the region ends.

The most practically useful exchange between the two is the RAT
checklist. Developed inside OSG’s wargame-adjacent context, it is a
general-purpose tool for fiction engine referee adjudication that is
more operationally precise than the framework’s current guidance on
outcomes. Reasonable, Actionable, Traceable: three tests that any
referee, oracle interpreter, or player adjudicating their own solo
outcomes can apply to check whether a resolution is genuinely serving
the fiction. The framework’s guidance says outcomes should be
continuations rather than endpoints. The RAT checklist tells you how to
verify that a specific outcome is.

## Conclusion: Knowing the Edge of the Map

The fiction engine essay series has moved progressively outward: from
the framework itself, to the transparency threshold that marks its
boundary, to specific designs within and adjacent to the paradigm, and
now to a form that shares its foundational values while operating in a
different register entirely.

OSG demonstrates that fiction primacy, the commitment to keeping every
procedure in service of the fiction, is a principle that travels further
than any single design paradigm can contain. Fiction engine designs are
one family of implementations. OSG is another, working through
collective subjects, layered information, and competitive stakes toward
a fiction of a different kind: strategic, contested, and revealed turn
by turn through a public Report that tells you what happened while
preserving the asymmetries that make the next turn matter.

Knowing where the fiction engine paradigm ends and this adjacent
territory begins is part of knowing what the fiction engine paradigm is.
The edge of the map is not a failure of the map. It is where the map
becomes useful as a boundary rather than a guide.

# The Same Thinness, Different Purposes

*This essay is a standalone comparative piece in the Fiction Engine
companion essay series. It is readable after the framework and “Four
Designs, One Framework,” and assumes familiarity with the transparency
threshold concept.*

## Why OSR/FKR and Fiction Engine Designs Converge Procedurally and Diverge Structurally

Two traditions arrive at nearly identical procedural profiles — thin
rules, situational rulings, minimal overhead, high referee discretion —
from different starting points and in service of different things. At
the level of play transcript, a 24XX session and an FKR session can be
indistinguishable: quick resolutions, a foregrounded situation, a
referee making calls rather than consulting tables. A reader
encountering both for the first time has reasonable grounds to treat
them as the same tradition.

They are not. This essay says precisely why, and why that matters for
designers and players who operate in both neighborhoods.

## What Each Tradition Is Minimizing Toward

The convergence is real and not accidental. Both traditions are
responding to the same diagnosis: heavy procedure gets between the
player and what the game is trying to produce. What they disagree about
is what the game is trying to produce.

OSR/FKR thinness is in service of world interaction. The procedure gets
out of the way so the player can engage with the world as a coherent
problem space: what is actually here, what can I actually do, what are
the real consequences of this action inside a world that operates
according to its own logic. The fiction is the medium through which that
engagement happens. The world’s logic — its physical and social
properties, its established facts and causal structures — is the object
toward which the player is oriented. A thin procedure is valuable
because it does not obscure the world. The world remains the primary
object of attention.

Fiction engine thinness is in service of fictional flow. The procedure
gets out of the way so the fiction can keep moving: what changes, what
opens, how does the situation transform into the next situation. The
world’s logic is a constraint on plausibility — outcomes must be
consistent with what the fiction has established — but it is not the
primary object. Fictional momentum is the object. A thin procedure is
valuable because it does not interrupt the fiction. The fiction remains
the primary object of attention.

These are not the same minimization target. Both traditions are
minimizing overhead. The overhead they are minimizing is getting between
the player and different things.

## Where They Look Identical

The procedural surface is genuinely shared. Both traditions favor
rulings over rules: the referee applies judgment to the specific
situation rather than consulting a codified procedure. Both favor
minimal tracking: the fiction carries the relevant states without
extensive administrative record. Both treat risk as the primary trigger
for resolution: unopposed actions succeed, and the mechanics activate
only where something genuinely hangs in the balance. Both value high
referee discretion: the person mediating the fiction has authority to
make calls that a design document cannot anticipate.

These are not superficial resemblances. They are structural commitments
that both traditions share because they are responding to a shared
problem. Codified procedure accumulates weight. That weight draws the
player’s attention away from the game’s primary object — whether that
object is the world or the fiction — and toward the system itself. Both
traditions refuse that accumulation for the same reason, even if the
object being protected differs.

The result is that both traditions sit below, or near, the transparency
threshold. In each case the procedural layer is thin enough that the
player’s attention passes through it rather than pausing at it. The
system does not demand conscious engagement in order for play to
advance. The shared commitment to thinness produces a shared threshold
position.

## Where They Diverge Under Pressure

The divergence becomes visible at the moment of ruling under ambiguity —
where the situation is genuinely unclear and the referee must decide
what happens. The question each tradition asks is different.

OSR/FKR: what would actually happen here, given the world’s established
logic? The ruling is an inference about the world. Getting it right
means being consistent with what the world is — its physical properties,
its causal history, the behavioral logic of its inhabitants. Fictional
momentum is a secondary consideration; world-coherence is primary. A
ruling that halts play because the world’s logic requires it is a
correct ruling. The player has encountered the world operating
correctly.

Fiction engine: what new fictional state does this produce, and does
play continue? The ruling is a transformation of the situation. Getting
it right means the fiction keeps moving and the outcome is a
continuation rather than a suspension. World-coherence is a constraint
on plausibility — an outcome that contradicts established fictional
facts fails the Reasonable criterion — but it is not a trump card. A
ruling that halts play is a failed ruling regardless of its world-logic
justification. The fiction has stalled, and a stalled fiction is a
design problem.

Two designs with identical procedural thinness produce different rulings
under the same ambiguous circumstances because they are applying
different criteria for what makes a ruling correct. The thinness is the
same. What it is optimizing for is not.

## The Locked Door

A concrete case that does not require knowledge of any specific game.

A locked door in an OSR design: the player engages with the door as a
world-object. What is it made of? How is it locked? What tools are
available, and what do those tools actually accomplish given the door’s
physical properties? The resolution procedure produces a result
consistent with what the world would do. A crowbar applied to a heavy
iron door in a stone frame produces different outcomes than the same
tool applied to a wooden door with a simple latch. The player who
outsmarts the door — who reads the world correctly and acts on that
reading — has engaged correctly with the design.

A locked door in a fiction engine: the resolution procedure produces a
new fictional state. On a strong success, the door opens cleanly. On a
partial success, it opens but alerts someone nearby. On a failure,
something changes — the lock breaks, someone approaches, a new condition
enters the fiction. Each result is a continuation. The question is not
what the door would do in strict physical terms but what the situation
becomes. A complication on a partial success is not a failure of
world-logic; it is the fiction producing its next moment.

Same door. Same thin procedure. The OSR referee asking “what would this
door actually do?” and the fiction engine referee asking “what does this
situation become?” are applying different criteria even if the mechanics
they reach for are indistinguishable from the outside.

## What Each Tradition Accepts as a Cost

OSR/FKR accepts that world-logic can produce situations where the
fiction stalls — where the correct answer is “no, and nothing changes
yet, because the world’s logic does not support an outcome here.” The
player has not found the solution. The world has not yielded. Play
continues with the player searching for another approach, gathering more
information, or accepting that this particular path is closed. That is
not a design failure. It is the world operating correctly, and the
experience of encountering a coherent world that resists the player is
part of what the tradition is designed to produce.

A fiction engine accepts that fictional momentum can override strict
world-consistency. The ruling that favors a transformation over the most
rigorously logical outcome — that introduces a complication rather than
a flat refusal, that finds the continuation rather than the stopping
point — is making a choice the world might not strictly mandate. That is
not a design failure. It is the fiction operating correctly, and the
experience of a situation that keeps changing and opening is part of
what the paradigm is designed to produce.

Neither tradition is wrong about what it accepts. They are optimized for
different things, and the optimization produces different tolerances for
the other’s cost. An OSR player who finds that a fiction engine will not
let the world simply resist is encountering the paradigm working as
intended. A fiction engine player who finds that an OSR world stops
moving until they solve a problem is encountering a different paradigm
working as intended. The incompatibility is not mutual deficiency. It is
different design goals producing different acceptable failure modes.

## Practical Consequences for Designers

The distinction produces specific divergences in design decisions.

**Rulings.** An OSR/FKR designer builds referee judgment toward
world-coherence: the well-made ruling is the one that is consistent with
the world’s established logic and would not surprise a neutral observer
who understood how the world works. A fiction engine designer builds
referee judgment toward fictional continuation: the well-made ruling is
the one that produces a new fictional state and keeps play moving,
within the constraints of plausibility. The criteria for a good ruling
differ, and the referee who has internalized one set of criteria will
produce different calls than one who has internalized the other, even
when running the same thin procedure.

**Failure states.** In OSR/FKR, failure can close options legitimately.
The player who fails to pick the lock has not picked the lock; the world
does not owe them an alternative path through. They must find another
way, and the design does not guarantee that another way exists or is
accessible. In a fiction engine, failure must open something. The
framework states this plainly: a result that leaves the fiction
suspended rather than transformed indicates a design problem. Failure
that produces “nothing changes” is a failure of the design, not a
correct expression of world-logic.

**Fictional description.** Both traditions value it, but for different
reasons. In OSR/FKR, description communicates world-properties the
player needs in order to engage with the problem: the texture of the
door, the sounds from behind it, the visible condition of the hinges.
The player is reading the world through the description in order to act
on it. In a fiction engine, description is the fictional state: the
output of play, the condition the situation is now in, the shared
imagined space that all participants hold in common. The description is
not instrumental to solving a problem; it is what the resolution
produces.

**Player orientation.** A player in an OSR/FKR design is primarily
asking what can I do here — reading the world in order to engage with it
as a problem space. A player in a fiction engine is primarily asking
what happens next — reading the situation in order to make a choice that
transforms it. Both are engaged with the fiction. The direction of
attention differs, and that difference shapes what a well-played session
looks like in each tradition.

## Practical Consequences for Players

The incompatibility mode is the most practically significant consequence
of the distinction, because the surface similarity of the two traditions
makes the divergence invisible until it causes friction.

Two players at the same table: one oriented toward world-engagement, one
oriented toward fictional flow, both playing a thin-procedure game that
could belong to either tradition. The player oriented toward the world
is making decisions based on what the world would actually support. The
player oriented toward fictional flow is making decisions based on what
would produce the most interesting continuation. Both are playing
correctly by their own tradition’s criteria. They are producing rulings
— and expecting rulings — by different standards, and neither can easily
name why the other player’s calls feel slightly off.

The problem is not that one player is wrong. It is that the procedural
thinness the design shares with both traditions has made the divergence
invisible. In a heavily proceduralized game, the rules encode the
tradition: a codified move list signals PbtA’s approach; a detailed
world-interaction resolution table signals OSR priorities. Thin
procedure does not encode the tradition in the same way. The rules do
not announce which object they are protecting.

This is where the distinction is most practically useful: not to
adjudicate which tradition is correct but to give players and referees a
way to identify which orientation a given design is asking for, and to
recognize when a table is applying two different answers to that
question simultaneously. A referee who knows they are running a fiction
engine can say “that ruling halts the fiction, and a halted fiction is a
design problem — let me find the continuation.” A referee who knows they
are running an OSR game can say “the world genuinely resists here, and
that resistance is the experience the game is producing.” Neither answer
is wrong in its context. The confusion arises from not knowing which
context you are in.

## Two Traditions, One Solution, Different Problems

The convergence at the procedural level is not a coincidence and not an
error. Both traditions arrived at thinness because thinness solves a
real problem: heavy procedure is an obstacle. They diagnosed the same
problem and drew the same procedural conclusion. What differs is what
the procedure was obstructing in each case.

In OSR/FKR, the procedure was getting between the player and the world.
Removing it gave the world back its legibility as a coherent problem
space. In fiction engine design, the procedure was getting between the
player and the fiction’s momentum. Removing it gave the fiction back its
capacity to keep changing. The solution worked in both cases, for
different reasons.

A designer who knows which problem their thinness is solving can make
deliberate decisions when the two traditions pull toward different
rulings: when the world’s logic suggests stopping and the fiction’s
momentum suggests continuing, when description serves as
information-for-problem-solving versus information-as-fictional-state,
when failure should close options versus open complications.

These are not questions a design document can answer in advance for
every situation. They are orientation questions — the kind that get
answered in the moment of the ruling, and that get answered consistently
only when the person making the ruling knows which tradition they are
working inside.

Knowing which problem your thinness is for is the beginning of knowing
what to do with it.

# Fiction and Memory

*Part of the Fiction Engine companion essay series. This essay is the
first in a four-part systems thinking arc and is part of the Fiction
Engine companion essay series. It follows “The Faction and the Fiction”
and is a companion to “Fiction Engine: A Design Framework for Tabletop
RPGs” (v1.0, May 2026).*

## The Structural Difference Between Accumulation and Reset

The fiction engine framework insists that outcomes function as
continuations rather than endpoints. The requirement is stated plainly
and without qualification: a result that leaves the fiction suspended
rather than transformed indicates a design problem. Outcomes should
advance or complicate the fiction. The productive question after any
resolution is what new fictional state the result creates, not simply
whether an action succeeded.

This is correct. What the framework does not do is explain why it must
be correct — not as a stylistic preference or a design aesthetic, but as
a structural requirement. The Traceable criterion in the OSG essay comes
closest to the underlying argument, but operationally: it tells a
referee how to check whether a specific outcome is grounded in prior
fictional events. It does not explain why that grounding matters in the
first place. The structural reason sits one level below where the
framework’s analysis reaches.

This essay names that reason. The question is: what exactly is the
structural difference between a fiction that accumulates and one that
resets, and why does that difference matter for design?

## Two Kinds of Fiction

The distinction is easier to recognize than to name, because both kinds
of fiction feel like fiction while they are running. The structural
difference shows up in what outcomes do to what follows.

Call the first kind episodic. In episodic fiction, each session, each
scene, each significant outcome begins from a roughly equivalent state.
Prior events are acknowledged in tone — the players know what happened,
the characters reference it — but they do not constrain current
possibility in any structural sense. A character who was publicly
humiliated last session carries that humiliation as flavor this session.
Other characters might mention it. The player might invoke it for
characterization. But if no rule encodes it as a disadvantage, and no
established fictional fact makes it a structural obstacle, then the
humiliation is decoration rather than state. It belongs to the record of
what happened, not to the architecture of what can happen next.

Call the second kind accumulated. In accumulated fiction, prior events
are not merely acknowledged; they constrain. The burned bridge cannot be
un-burned. Not as a rule that forbids rebuilding it, but because the
fiction now contains a burned bridge, and any action that treats it as
intact would require the fiction to contradict itself. The alliance
formed under duress carries the weight of that duress: both parties know
the terms were extracted rather than freely given, and that fact is now
part of every subsequent interaction between them. A character who
failed publicly to defend someone who needed defending has established a
reputation, and that reputation shapes what other characters will
plausibly do. Each outcome narrows the possibility space for what can
follow, because each outcome adds to a growing body of fictional fact
that must be honored.

Most games contain both types in different proportions. A campaign with
strong continuity in its political situation but episodic personal-scale
scenes is mostly accumulated with episodic pockets. A one-shot that
takes place in a fixed-situation world is almost entirely episodic.
Neither structure is inherently superior to the other. The distinction
is architectural, not evaluative. What matters is recognizing that they
are genuinely different objects and that the difference has
consequences.

## Why Accumulation Produces Consequence

The fiction engine framework’s treatment of consequences is almost
entirely procedural: produce outcomes that change the situation, keep
things moving, avoid suspended results. What it does not address is why
some outcomes feel consequential and others do not — why the same
mechanical result can land with weight in one fiction and register as
noise in another. The answer is accumulated state.

**Consequence requires prior state.** An outcome feels consequential
when it changes something that mattered. For something to matter inside
the fiction, it must have been established there — through play, through
prior outcomes, through the slow accumulation of fictional facts that
participants have come to treat as real. A character’s death is weighted
by the history that character has accrued inside the fiction: the
relationships established, the obligations left unresolved, the things
they were in the middle of doing. Strip that history away — introduce
the character at the start of the session, kill them at the end — and
the death is a narrative event without fictional consequence. The other
characters can react to it, the players can perform grief, but the
fiction has not lost anything it had built. The difference between a
death that matters and one that does not is not dramatic craft. It is
accumulated state.

This is easy to observe but hard to design for, because accumulated
state cannot be specified in advance. No design document can say: by
session five, the players will have established enough history that
character deaths carry weight. What the design can do is create and
protect the conditions under which accumulation occurs — which is
precisely what the fiction engine framework’s procedural commitments are
doing, without naming that as their function.

**Choices require constrained possibility spaces.** A meaningful choice
is one in which the options are not equivalent — where selecting one
forecloses others, where the costs and benefits differ, where prior
events have made some paths easier and others harder. Accumulated
fiction is what produces that structure. The decision of whether to
trust the Conclave is not equivalent for a character who has already
been deceived by them and one who has only heard rumors. The choice of
which route to take through the mountains is not equivalent once the
fiction has established that one route is watched and the other is
unknown. Those asymmetries do not come from a stat block or a skill
list. They come from prior play, from the specific shape of what has
already happened.

In episodic fiction, choices can be presented as dramatically
significant without being structurally so. The mechanics can produce
difficulty and cost, but the fictional asymmetries between options must
be constructed fresh each scene rather than inherited from what came
before. Players learn to treat choices as important because the game
tells them they are, not because the accumulated situation has actually
made one option different from another. This produces a kind of play,
and some players prefer it. But it is a different structural object from
a fiction where the prior situation is doing the work of
differentiation.

**Surprise requires established expectation.** The most effective
fictional surprises are those that contradict something the fiction has
already established — where an outcome arrives that is consistent with
what the fiction has made possible but contrary to what participants
expected given what they knew. That kind of surprise is only available
to accumulated fiction, because it requires the fiction to have stored
expectations to violate. When the ally betrays the party, the surprise
depends on the alliance having been established as reliable over time.
When the safe route turns out to be watched, the surprise depends on the
fiction having established it as safe. Resetting fiction cannot produce
this kind of surprise, because it has no stored expectations: it can
introduce an unexpected element, but the element arrives from outside
the fiction rather than emerging from within it. The difference is the
difference between a twist and a revelation.

## What the Framework’s Rules Are Actually Protecting

With the structure of accumulated fiction in view, several of the
fiction engine framework’s design guidelines read differently than they
initially appear.

“Outcomes advance or complicate the fiction rather than suspending it.”
This is usually read as a rule about procedural momentum: keep things
moving, don’t stall. That reading is correct as far as it goes. But the
deeper reason the rule exists is that an outcome which fails to change
the fictional situation is an outcome that fails to add to the
accumulated state. The chain loses a link. Not just procedurally —
structurally. The fiction is slightly less accumulated after a suspended
outcome than it would have been after a genuine continuation. A series
of suspended outcomes is a series of missed additions to the causal
tissue.

“Fictional descriptors should do the work that numerical interfaces
often claim to do.” The framework offers this as a cognitive efficiency
principle: “she is exhausted and can barely lift her sword” carries more
usable information than a stamina score of 3. Both observations are
accurate. But there is a deeper structural point. Fictional states
accumulate naturally. A character who has been described as exhausted
across three scenes carries that exhaustion into the fourth scene
whether or not anyone explicitly remembers to apply a penalty. The
fictional fact persists inside the shared imagined space because
participants have been treating it as real. A numerical state, by
contrast, requires explicit tracking to carry forward. If no one checks
the stamina score, the state does not persist. The fictional descriptor
is self-maintaining in a way the numerical record is not, because it
belongs to the accumulated fiction rather than to an administrative
layer outside it.

The failure mode of suspended outcomes is the most obvious case: it
breaks the accumulation chain at a specific link, inserting an
administrative gap between one fictional state and the next. But the
framework’s overhead accumulation failure mode operates the same way at
a larger scale. Each piece of dead overhead inserts an administrative
step between fictional events. Individually, each step may seem
negligible. Cumulatively, they fragment the causal tissue. The chain
does not break at a single point; it thins throughout, and the
accumulated fiction that depends on it becomes progressively less
coherent.

The Traceable criterion from the OSG essay — can you point to earlier
events that led here? — is the operationalization of this structural
requirement as a referee check. Traceability is what accumulated fiction
looks like from the outside: an outcome is traceable when you can follow
the causal chain back from it through the prior fictional events that
made it possible. The criterion exists because an outcome that is not
traceable to prior fictional events is an outcome that was imported from
outside the accumulated fiction rather than emerging from within it. It
may be dramatically appropriate, but it is architecturally incoherent;
it does not connect to the tissue.

The framework has been protecting accumulated fiction throughout. Its
procedural commitments — continuations over endpoints, fictional states
over administrative records, the prohibition on dead overhead, the
Traceable check — are all, in significant part, rules for maintaining
the causal tissue that accumulated fiction requires. The framework does
not name this as the property being protected. The rules are correct;
the account of why they must be correct is what is missing.

## The Design Implication

A designer building a fiction engine is not simply choosing thin
procedures over thick ones. They are making a choice about what kind of
fictional object their game will produce.

Thin procedures that preserve accumulated state produce a fiction in
which prior events constrain current events, in which choices are
differentiated by history, in which surprises emerge from within rather
than arriving from outside. That is one kind of object. Thick procedures
that interrupt or reset state — through administrative layers, through
outcome structures that do not connect back to prior fictional facts,
through resolution chains that produce numbers rather than fictional
conditions — produce a different kind of object. It is possible for
thick procedures to serve accumulated fiction, if they are carefully
designed to pass their outputs into the shared imagined space rather
than into administrative records. But they require more work to do so,
because the structure of the procedure does not naturally tend toward
it.

The implication for the fiction engine paradigm specifically: every rule
that generates dead overhead without producing a fictional state is not
merely a cognitive efficiency problem. It is a structural problem. Dead
overhead inserts an administrative step between fictional events, and
that step is a potential point where the accumulated chain loses
coherence. A chain interrupted long enough and often enough stops being
a chain. The fiction remains present — the players are still imagining,
still describing — but the causal tissue connecting events has thinned
to the point where prior events no longer constrain or differentiate
what follows. The fiction is running in episodic mode even if the
players believe they are accumulating.

This is why the fiction engine framework’s central test — does this rule
produce a new fictional state? — is not only a procedural efficiency
question. It is a question about what kind of fictional object the rule
is helping to maintain.

## When Reset Is the Point

Some designs use episodic structure deliberately, and those designs
should not be analyzed as if accumulation were the goal they are falling
short of.

One-shots are defined by the deliberate limit on accumulation: the
fiction begins fresh and ends before a second layer can form on top of
it. Anthology formats build deliberately around episodic structure,
finding value in the discrete unit of a single session rather than the
developing arc of a campaign. Procedurally generated settings where each
session instantiates a new world make the reset explicit: the fictional
environment does not carry forward, and the play is optimized for the
self-contained encounter rather than the developing situation.

These are coherent choices, and they produce experiences that
accumulated fiction cannot. A one-shot can sustain a level of intensity
and concentration that a long-running campaign must pace around. An
anthology format can explore variety across sessions in a way that
accumulated fiction, with its growing weight of established fact,
resists. The episodic structure is not a lesser version of accumulated
structure; it is a different architecture, optimized for different
things.

The argument this essay is making is not that accumulation is superior.
It is that accumulation is a structural property with structural
consequences, and that the fiction engine framework is implicitly built
around it without naming it as such. A designer building a fiction
engine for a campaign is building an accumulated fiction engine whether
they have named it that or not. The framework’s rules make sense as
rules for accumulated fiction specifically; they are less mandatory —
some of them are actively inappropriate — for designs built around
episodic structure. Knowing which kind of fiction your design is
producing is knowing what the rules are for.

## The Causal Tissue

Accumulated fiction is a causal tissue: a network of established events,
conditions, and facts such that each element is both a consequence of
what preceded it and a constraint on what can plausibly follow. The
tissue is what makes consequences weighty, choices non-equivalent, and
surprises possible from within rather than from outside. Without it, the
fiction is a sequence of events that follow each other but do not cause
each other — a chronicle rather than a situation.

The fiction engine framework’s procedural commitments are, in
significant part, commitments to maintaining that tissue. Continuations
over endpoints preserve the causal connections between events. Fictional
states over administrative records keep the tissue inside the fiction
where it can be seen, responded to, and built upon. The prohibition on
dead overhead prevents administrative fragmentation of what should be a
continuous chain. The Traceable criterion, where it appears in the OSG
essay, is a direct check on whether a specific outcome is connected to
the tissue or floating free of it.

The framework is correct that these commitments matter. What it has not
yet said is why they matter: because a fiction that loses its causal
tissue stops being accumulated fiction, and accumulated fiction is what
the paradigm’s other structural commitments assume.

The next essay will ask why thin mediation specifically is necessary for
that tissue to form. The answer involves agents, the sensitivity between
them, and what happens to that sensitivity when procedure is
foregrounded. Those are questions about how the tissue gets built in the
first place — questions that the distinction between accumulation and
reset cannot answer on its own.

# The Emergence Condition

## Why Thin Mediation Enables What Thick Procedure Suppresses

*Part of the Fiction Engine companion essay series. This essay is the
second in the systems thinking arc of the Fiction Engine companion
series, following “Fiction and Memory: The Structural Difference Between
Accumulation and Reset.” It is also readable as a standalone piece
following the framework and the transparency threshold essay.*

The fiction engine framework ends with a claim it does not explain. “The
story appears to emerge through play rather than being processed through
procedure.” The word “appears” is doing quiet work here. It names an
experience — one that practiced players recognize immediately, the
moment a session goes somewhere nobody planned, when a consequence
follows from twenty minutes ago in a way that surprises everyone
including the referee — without naming the mechanism that produces it.
The framework is correct that emergence happens under these conditions.
It does not say why.

This essay says why. The answer comes from systems thinking, but the
argument is about design, and the vocabulary will be kept in its place:
defined once, used precisely, dropped when it is not doing work. The
practical implication of the argument is significant enough that it
changes how several of the framework’s design commitments should be
understood. Some of what the framework presents as stylistic preference
or design philosophy is, on this account, a structural condition. Remove
it and you are not making a different aesthetic choice. You are breaking
a mechanism.

## What Emergence Actually Means

Emergence is a property of systems: a behavior or characteristic that no
component of the system has individually but that the system produces
through the interaction of its components. A murmuration of starlings
has a shape. No individual starling has a shape. The shape emerges from
local interaction rules: each bird tracks its nearest neighbors and
adjusts its position. No bird is flying the shape. The shape arises from
the network.

This is not magic. It is a structural consequence of how components
interact, and it is predictable in the following sense: if you know the
interaction rules, you know what conditions allow emergence to occur.
You cannot predict the specific shape of a specific murmuration. You can
predict that murmurations will have shapes, that those shapes will
cohere and shift, and that they will stop being shapes if the
interaction rules are removed.

Applied to tabletop play: something emerges during a session when the
fiction produced by the session’s end could not have been predicted from
the elements at its start, and when the unpredictable development is
felt as arising from within the situation rather than being imposed from
outside it. This is a real phenomenon. Every person who has played a
fiction engine game has experienced it. The question is which
interaction rules produce it and which prevent it.

## Agents, Sensitivity, and Feedback

The system that produces emergent fiction has three components worth
naming.

**Agents** are participants in the play system who take inputs and
produce outputs: players describing what their characters do, a referee
describing the world’s response, an oracle answering a question about
the fiction’s state, a procedure transforming fictional intent into a
fictional outcome. Each agent has some model of the fiction’s current
state. Each acts on that model to produce the next state.

**Sensitivity** is the property by which an agent’s output is shaped by
the specific prior outputs of other agents, not merely by the agent’s
own goals. A player who responds to what another character just did —
adjusting their own character’s action because the situation has changed
in a specific way — is exhibiting sensitivity. A player who pursues a
pre-formed plan regardless of what has happened in the last twenty
minutes is not. Sensitivity is the difference between a conversation and
a sequence of speeches.

**Feedback loops** form when agent A produces an output that agent B
uses as input, and agent B’s output becomes input for agent A. The loop
closes when the originating agent responds to the response to their
output. In play terms: a character does something. The world responds.
The character responds to the response. What the character does next is
shaped by the full chain of events that preceded it, not only by their
initial intent.

Now the crucial piece. Feedback loops need substance to run over. A loop
between two agents whose outputs are generic — who could be responding
to any situation rather than this situation — produces only nominal
emergence. The surprise is shallow because the inputs were
undifferentiated. Accumulated fiction, as described in the preceding
essay, is what gives feedback loops their substance. When each agent is
responding to the specific accumulated state of the fiction — to the
particular way this situation has developed from that prior one — the
feedback loops are closing over something specific and irreversible.
That specificity is what makes the emergent development feel like it
came from inside the fiction rather than from outside it.

The conditions for emergent fiction are not mysterious: multiple agents,
sensitivity to each other’s outputs, feedback loops closing over
accumulated state. If those conditions are present, emergence is
possible. If any of them is absent, it is not.

## What Thick Mediation Does to Feedback Loops

This is where the design argument earns its place.

When a procedural foreground is present, the player’s first task upon
receiving the current fictional state is not to respond to it but to
classify it. Which move applies here? Which resource category is at
stake? What does the system accept as valid input for this type of
action? That classification step redirects the player’s attention from
the fiction and from the other agents to the system. While the player is
classifying, the feedback loop between agents is interrupted.

This is not a criticism of the player’s engagement. It is a description
of what classification demands. Classification requires holding a
taxonomy in mind and matching the current situation to one of its
categories. The current situation must be read as a representative
instance of a type rather than as a specific, accumulated state. That is
the cognitive work the classification step asks for, and while that work
is being done, the player is oriented toward the system rather than
toward the other agents.

The interruption is brief in practiced play. An experienced PbtA player
identifies the applicable move quickly, sometimes instantaneously. The
transparency threshold essay makes this point: internalization reduces
the attentional cost of the classification step until it operates nearly
in the background. But it is structurally always there. The loop cannot
close until the classification is resolved. And before internalization
is complete — which takes time that varies significantly across players
and groups — the interruption is real and recurring.

The second effect of thick mediation is more subtle and harder to
recover through internalization. Call it the uniformity effect.

Procedural taxonomies are designed to produce consistent, legible
outputs. This is one of their genuine strengths. A move structure
ensures that a given type of fictional event reliably produces a given
range of outcomes: when you act under fire, the game delivers tension
and consequence in a recognizable form. The consistency creates dramatic
rhythm. The recognizability creates strategic legibility. Players know
what this kind of scene does.

The cost is that different inputs can produce identical procedural
outputs. Two situations that are fictionally distinct — that differ in
everything the accumulated fiction has established about the characters,
their history, the stakes, the relationships in the room — can both
trigger the same move and produce the same range of outcomes. The move’s
output range is not sensitive to the accumulated specificity of the
fiction. It produces drama of a type, not drama of this situation.

The agents are responding to the move’s output range rather than to the
fiction’s specific state. The feedback loops close, but they close over
the move’s output rather than over the accumulated fiction’s full
specificity. Something is lost at the classification step: all the ways
this situation is different from any other situation that would also
trigger this move. That loss is exactly the material from which
unexpected, specific, unrepeatable emergent developments are made.

This is not a flaw in designs that use procedural taxonomies. It is a
tradeoff. The consistent dramatic rhythm that move structures produce is
a real design achievement — one that is not available to designs without
that structure. Some players find the reliability more valuable than the
specificity. That is a rational preference, and the paradigms built
around move structures serve it well. The point is not that move
structures are wrong. It is that they are optimized for something other
than the kind of emergence the fiction engine paradigm is trying to
produce, and the reason is structural.

In a fiction engine, the player receives the current fictional state and
responds to it directly. The procedural layer is thin enough that the
player’s attention passes through it rather than pausing at it. There is
no classification step to perform, or the classification is so obvious
that it requires no conscious engagement. The feedback loop between
agents is not interrupted. Each response is responding to the specific
accumulated state of the fiction, all of it, not to a procedural
category that the fiction instantiates. The specificity is preserved.
The feedback loops close over material that is dense, particular, and
irreversible. That is where emergent development comes from.

## The Social Dimension as a Systems Claim

The framework’s social dimension section makes a claim that is typically
read as cultural advice: “a fiction engine functions only when all
participants are oriented toward the fiction as the primary object of
play.” The systems framing reveals this as something more precise than a
recommendation about table culture. It is a description of the feedback
loop condition.

When participants are oriented toward the fiction, they are oriented
toward each other’s outputs. The shared fictional state is the medium
through which their sensitivity to each other is expressed. A player who
is inside the fiction is reading the outputs of every other agent — what
the referee described, what the other characters did, what the oracle
produced — and responding to them as inputs. The feedback loops are
running.

When a participant is oriented toward the system instead — tracking
resources, consulting the procedure, managing their mechanical position
— they have temporarily withdrawn from the shared fictional medium. They
are still present at the table. They are not producing outputs in
response to the fiction’s current state. Their attention is elsewhere,
and while it is elsewhere, the feedback loops weaken. The other agents
are producing outputs that this participant is not receiving as inputs.
When they return to the fiction, they are responding to a state that has
accumulated in their absence.

This happens briefly and constantly in games above the transparency
threshold. In groups with high experience and strong internalization,
the interruptions are short enough that the loops recover quickly. In
groups with lower experience, or in solo play where there is no
distribution of attentional load, the interruptions accumulate and the
conditions for emergence degrade progressively over the session.

The framework says this is a “table culture condition that design can
support but not guarantee.” The systems account explains exactly why
design cannot guarantee it. Emergence is a property of the
system-in-use, not of the design document. The design creates the
interaction rules. The agents produce the interaction. A fiction engine
is a design whose interaction rules preserve the feedback loop
conditions. Whether those conditions produce emergent fiction depends on
the agents actually running the loops; that is not something a document
can compel.

## Complexity in the Fiction, Complexity in the Procedure

The framework states plainly that it is not a position against
complexity. “A rule can be intricate and still belong to a fiction
engine, provided it either changes the shared imagined space directly or
preserves the coherence, pacing, or legibility that makes fictional
change possible.” The emergence argument supports this position and
makes it more precise.

Complexity in the fiction does not suppress emergence. It feeds it. A
setting with deep historical layering, a character with a long history
of decisions and consequences, a political situation with multiple
competing factions and overlapping obligations — all of this is
accumulated state over which feedback loops can close. Richer
accumulated state means more specific inputs and outputs, more ways the
current situation differs from any other situation, more material from
which unexpected development can arise. If anything, complexity of
fictional state accelerates the conditions for emergence by giving
agents more to be sensitive to.

What suppresses emergence is complexity in the procedure: the
classification steps, the resource economies, the attentional demands
that redirect agents toward the system. These reduce the time agents
spend oriented toward each other and toward the fiction. The distinction
is between complexity that enriches the feedback loop’s content and
complexity that interrupts the loop’s operation. The fiction engine
paradigm is committed to eliminating the second kind. It is not
committed to limiting the first.

The practical implication for design: a fiction engine can support a
richly specified world, a character system with many distinct
descriptors, a political landscape with dozens of named factions and
shifting alliances. All of that complexity lives in the fiction and
feeds the feedback loops. What it cannot support, while staying below
the transparency threshold, is the procedural complexity required to
manage that same richness through a classification-based resolution
system. The fiction holds the complexity. The procedure stays thin.

## The Failure Modes as Feedback Network Degradation

The framework names four failure modes: overhead accumulation, suspended
outcomes, authority drift, and orientational divergence. They are
presented as design problems — things a design can do wrong. The
emergence account reveals something more specific: each failure mode is
a distinct mechanism by which the feedback network can degrade.

**Overhead accumulation** adds classification demands. Each additional
tracking obligation, each additional resource state that must be
consulted before acting, is another moment where the agent’s attention
redirects from the fiction to the system. Individually, each piece of
overhead is a brief interruption. Cumulatively, the interruptions
accumulate and the time agents spend oriented toward the fiction
decreases relative to the time they spend oriented toward the system.
The feedback loops do not break; they weaken progressively, and the
outputs they produce become less specific, less responsive to the
accumulated fiction, more dominated by administrative state.

**Suspended outcomes** are loops that cannot close. A resolution that
requires further resolution before play can continue produces an
incomplete output: an agent has acted, but the system has not yet
produced the fictional consequence of that action. The other agents
cannot respond to an outcome that has not been produced. The feedback
loop is paused mid-cycle. In a design prone to suspended outcomes, the
loop is paused frequently — every time the resolution chain requires
additional steps before a fictional state is produced. Agents develop
habits of waiting rather than responding, and the sensitivity that the
loop requires atrophies.

**Authority drift** transforms an agent in the feedback network into an
obstacle within it. A referee whose rulings require procedural
negotiation before producing a fictional consequence, or an oracle whose
outputs require significant interpretive work before play can continue,
has stopped functioning as a node in the feedback network. It is a point
at which inputs enter and fictional outputs do not emerge. Other agents
cannot close their feedback loops through an authority mechanism that is
not producing fictional outputs. They wait, interpret, translate — and
in doing so, redirect their attention from the fiction to the mechanism.

**Orientational divergence** is the most complete form of network
degradation. When participants hold different models of what the game is
doing, the feedback loops between them are running on different
channels. A player treating the game as a tactical puzzle is producing
outputs calibrated to mechanical optimization. A player treating it as
character exploration is producing outputs calibrated to psychological
consistency. These outputs are not legible to each other as inputs to
the shared fiction: each player is responding to a different game, and
no shared accumulated state develops. The fiction fragments because
there is no shared medium for the feedback loops to run through.

These four failure modes are not arbitrary bad practices. They are the
specific mechanisms by which the conditions for emergent fiction break
down. The framework is correct to name them and correct in the remedies
it implies. The emergence account explains why they matter structurally,
not only pragmatically.

## The Mechanism Behind the Magic

The story does not merely “appear to emerge.” It emerges through a
mechanism, and the mechanism is now visible: feedback loops between
agents sensitive to each other’s outputs, closing over accumulated
fictional state, with a procedural layer thin enough not to interrupt
them. Those are the conditions. When they are present, emergent
development is possible. When any of them is absent, it is not.

The emergence is real. The “magic” quality — the sensation that
something is happening that nobody planned — is a genuine experiential
property of the system, and it is not available to designs that
interrupt the feedback loops, dissolve the accumulated specificity, or
redirect the agents’ attention toward the system rather than toward each
other. The fiction engine paradigm’s commitment to thin mediation is not
a stylistic preference for a certain kind of procedural minimalism. It
is a commitment to the structural condition that keeps the feedback
loops intact.

This matters for how the paradigm’s design commitments should be read.
When the framework says outcomes should be continuations rather than
endpoints, it is protecting the feedback loops from suspended-outcome
interruptions. When it says fictional descriptors should do the work
that numerical interfaces claim to do, it is keeping complexity in the
fiction rather than in the procedure, feeding the loops rather than
interrupting them. When it says the game should remain more visible than
the system, it is stating the orientation condition on which the loops
depend. These are not arbitrary aesthetic choices. They are structural
requirements for the mechanism to run.

A designer who understands the mechanism can evaluate their own
decisions against it. Not “is this rule elegant?” or “does this rule
feel fiction-first?” but “does this rule interrupt the feedback loops,
and if so, does what it produces justify the interruption?” Some
interruptions are worth accepting; the transparency threshold essay
makes this clear, and the four-designs essay demonstrates it in
practice. The question is whether the designer is accepting the
interruption deliberately, knowing what it costs, rather than
accumulating it without accounting for the damage.

Understanding the mechanism is what allows the cost to be seen clearly.

There is one further implication the mechanism produces, which this
series has not yet addressed. If what the fiction engine designer is
building is a set of conditions for feedback loop behavior rather than a
fixed procedural object, then those conditions change as agents use the
design. Internalization moves the classification steps out of the
attentional foreground. Accumulated play builds the fictional state over
which the loops run. The system is modifying its own operating
conditions through use. That property — a design that changes its own
behavior as it is used — is the subject of the next essay.

# The Threshold as Trajectory

*Part of the Fiction Engine companion essay series. This essay is part
of the systems thinking arc of the Fiction Engine companion series,
following “Fiction and Memory” and “The Emergence Condition.” It reads
the transparency threshold concept developed in “The Transparency
Threshold: Abstraction, Mediation, and the Limits of Fiction Engine
Design” and the paradigm comparisons developed in “Four Designs, One
Framework.”*

## Designing a System That Changes Its Own Properties Through Use

The transparency threshold essay makes an observation in the middle of
its argument that it does not stop to develop: “the same game can sit on
different sides of the threshold depending on who is playing it and how
long they have been playing it.” The essay names three consequences of
this — the learning threshold is distinct from a permanent transparency
problem; how a game is introduced affects the threshold experience; solo
play demands a lower threshold because no facilitation distribution is
available — and then moves on to the next argument.

The observation deserved more time. What it describes is not a
complication of the threshold concept. It is a fact about what a game
design actually is: not a fixed object with stable properties, but a
system whose operating conditions change as agents use it. The threshold
is player-variable because players internalize procedures over time.
That internalization is the system changing its own behavior. And if a
design changes as it is used, then the designer is not producing a
document with fixed properties. The designer is producing a trajectory —
an entry experience, a learning curve, and a stable state — and all
three require deliberate design decisions.

The fiction engine framework’s central test asks what a rule does. That
is the right first question. But it is not the only question. A rule
that passes the test on paper may still impose significant attentional
costs during early play, before it has been internalized, and those
costs are as real as any overhead the test is designed to eliminate. The
framework has no vocabulary for this. The trajectory concept is that
vocabulary.

## What Internalization Is

A procedure that requires conscious attention when novel becomes
habitual with repetition. This is not a claim about memory in the
technical sense. It is a description of something any experienced player
recognizes: the moment when you stop consulting the move list and simply
play, when the die results stop requiring translation and their meaning
arrives directly, when the question of what counts as risky no longer
requires deliberate judgment because you have developed a calibrated
feel for it.

The procedure has not changed. The player has changed. The
classification step that once required a conscious stop — which move is
this, what category does this action fall into — now runs below the
level of deliberate attention. The attentional cost has dropped, not
because anything was removed from the design but because the player’s
relationship to it has shifted.

This is a systems phenomenon in the most straightforward sense: the
agents using the system are modifying their own response patterns in
ways that change how the system operates. The feedback loops described
in Essay 2 — the loops between agents that enable emergent behavior —
run faster and with less interruption as classification steps move into
procedural memory. Internalization is the mechanism by which a design
that begins above the transparency threshold can arrive, over time, at a
below-threshold state in practice.

The trajectory concept follows directly. A design that begins above the
threshold and internalizes toward it is not accurately described by a
single threshold position. It has three positions worth naming
separately: where it starts on session one, how quickly and reliably it
internalizes, and where it stabilizes once the learning curve is
complete. A complete description of a design’s relationship to the
transparency threshold requires all three.

This reframing changes the design questions. The framework’s central
test asks what a rule does — whether it produces fictional change,
supports the conditions for it, or generates dead overhead. The
trajectory framing adds: where does this rule sit in the learning curve,
how quickly does it internalize, and what is the experience it produces
while still in the foreground? A rule that passes the first test can
still fail the second set if it internalizes slowly, activates rarely,
or imposes entry costs that drive players away before the stable state
is reached.

## The Four Designs as Trajectories

The four-designs essay placed FU, 24XX, Breathless, and Hints and Hijinx
on the threshold map as if they had fixed positions. Applying the
trajectory lens to each reveals that their positions are more accurately
described as ranges — entry states, curves, and stable states — and that
the ranges differ significantly even among designs clustered in the same
neighborhood.

**24XX** is the clearest case because its trajectory is the shortest.
The core procedure — describe what you do, roll only if it is risky,
interpret the result as a fictional outcome — requires so little
conscious attention from the start that it has almost no learning curve
to speak of. A player is making habitual rolls by the end of the first
session, not because they have learned the rules but because there is so
little to learn. The design starts near its stable state and stays
there. The defense mechanic (say how an item breaks to absorb a hit)
begins below the threshold by design; the mechanical action and the
fictional event are the same action. There is no step to internalize
because there is no step.

The cost of this short trajectory is structural support at the entry
phase. 24XX offers nothing to players who need scaffolding during
session one: no move list to consult, no genre texture baked into the
resolution procedure, no pacing structure beyond the fictional situation
itself. Groups that can bring their own situation and stay in it will
reach the stable state immediately. Groups that need more help during
the learning phase will not find it in the rules.

**Freeform Universal** follows a similar pattern but with a small
variable. The resolution procedure — describe the attempt, roll,
interpret the result on the six-point scale — internalizes quickly,
within the first session for most players. The FU Points economy takes
slightly longer, not because it is complicated but because earning and
spending opportunities arrive intermittently rather than on every roll.
The internalization curve for FU Points is still short by any
comparison. FU’s typical trajectory is a brief learning threshold
followed by rapid arrival at a below-threshold stable state.

The variable is the FU Points dial. Groups that use FU Points heavily
introduce a light metacurrency foreground, and those groups stabilize at
a slightly different position than groups that run without them. The
design presents this as a feature rather than a problem — the dial is
documented and adjustable — and in trajectory terms it means the stable
state is a range rather than a point. Different groups inhabit different
stable states, all within the same general neighborhood, depending on
how they configure the optional economy. That is a coherent design
choice, and it produces a design that is adaptable across table cultures
rather than optimized for a single one.

**Breathless** is where the trajectory analysis does its most useful
work, because it reveals a split that the threshold map alone does not
show.

The die degradation and reset mechanics internalize quickly. They are
coupled to play actions the player is already performing: you roll to
resolve, and the die degrades as part of the same action. There is no
separate tracking obligation. The catch your breath reset is similarly
direct: you declare it, the GM introduces a complication, the dice
reset. By the third or fourth roll, most players have internalized both
mechanics without conscious effort. The fiction engine core of
Breathless — the part that shares its DNA with 24XX — follows a short
trajectory.

The pressure engine layer follows a different trajectory. The stress
track and combined resource management internalize more slowly because
they impose ongoing tracking obligations that are separate from the play
actions they accompany. A player must hold multiple counts in mind
simultaneously: skill die ratings, stress points, loot die rating,
backpack contents. Each is individually trackable. Combined, they
constitute a persistent attentional demand. This layer does not converge
toward background infrastructure through use. It is designed to stay
somewhat foregrounded, because the pressure engine experience — the
tension of depleting resources, the risk calculus of the catch your
breath moment — depends on the player being aware of the resource
states. A stress track the player has stopped noticing is not producing
the experience Breathless is optimized to produce.

The trajectory split is the precise description of what the four-designs
essay called “pressure engine DNA inside a fiction engine core.” The
fiction engine core has a short trajectory. The pressure engine layer
has a stable state that is deliberately above the threshold. For
designers working from the Breathless SRD — and there are many; the SRD
has generated well over two hundred derivatives, the majority of them
solo designs — this split is the most practically important thing the
analysis can tell them. The core can be taken and used with a very short
learning curve. The pressure engine layer is a specific design
commitment with a specific stable state, and adapting it for a different
design goal means reconsidering whether that stable state is the one you
want.

**Hints and Hijinx** presents a different kind of trajectory problem.
The investigation phase — the Breathless-core part of the design —
follows the same curve as Breathless’s fiction engine core: short,
below-threshold stable state, fast internalization. The clue economy in
the deduction phase does not follow any internalization curve because it
activates once per session, at a specific structural moment, rather than
repeatedly throughout play.

Internalization requires repetition. A player who makes a dozen rolls
per session develops procedural memory for the resolution mechanic. A
player who performs the deduction phase calculation once per session
does not develop the same kind of habit for it. The crossing remains
somewhat novel each time. This is neither a success nor a failure of
design; it is the consequence of the architectural containment the
four-designs essay identified. The threshold crossing happens in a
single moment, which means the player never has enough repetitions to
fully automate it. The clue economy is always slightly foregrounded,
always requiring a small amount of conscious attention when it
activates. For a mystery investigation design, this may be exactly
correct. The deduction phase is supposed to feel distinct from the
investigation phase — structurally marked, slightly more effortful. The
trajectory analysis gives a precise account of why it does.

**PbtA as a broader case.** The threshold essay notes that experienced
PbtA players internalize move structure to near-invisibility. This is
the clearest available example of a design with a significant learning
threshold that eventually reaches a below-threshold stable state for the
subset of players who complete the curve. The entry state is
substantially above the threshold: a new player must learn to identify
move triggers, consult move descriptions, identify which move applies,
and track move outputs before the fiction can advance. An experienced
Apocalypse World player has internalized the move taxonomy to
near-automaticity; the classification step’s attentional cost has
dropped close to zero. The design operates at a threshold position that
would be nearly invisible to a veteran and conspicuous to a newcomer.

The trajectory for PbtA is long, and for many players never reaches
completion. PbtA games with large, established communities compensate
through facilitation infrastructure that is not part of the design
document: session-one advice, experienced players who can carry
procedural weight while others are still building habits. A smaller
design without that infrastructure has a real problem if its trajectory
is equally long.

## What Designing a Trajectory Requires

The trajectory concept changes four design questions in ways worth
naming directly.

**The entry experience is a design problem.** Designers frequently treat
a steep learning curve as a documentation problem: the rules are fine,
the explanation needs work. This conflation is common and consequential.
If a design’s entry state is significantly above the transparency
threshold, that is a fact about the architecture, not about the manual.
Better instructions can reduce confusion about rules that are already
well-designed. They cannot reduce the attentional cost of rules that
require conscious engagement to operate, because that cost is built into
the rules themselves.

A designer thinking in trajectory terms asks a different question: what
is the player’s experience on session one, session three, and session
ten, and are all three consistent with the design’s goals? A design
whose goals are best served by a long learning curve — one that rewards
players who invest in it significantly — should be built to support that
investment explicitly, not designed for a short trajectory and then
documented as if complexity can be explained away. A design whose goals
are best served by fiction-transparent play from the first session
should examine every rule that imposes entry costs independently of
whether that rule is load-bearing at the stable state.

**Fast-internalizing rules and slow-internalizing rules differ in kind,
not just rate.** The difference between them is not incidental. Rules
that are tightly coupled to the play actions players are already
performing internalize quickly precisely because the practice is
constant. The 24XX defense mechanic internalizes quickly because
characters take hits throughout play; the player practices the mechanic
under the same conditions where it matters. The Breathless die
degradation internalizes quickly because every resolution step involves
it. Rules that activate rarely — once per session, once per phase, only
when a specific fictional condition is met — internalize slowly because
the practice is infrequent. Reference material compensates but does not
eliminate the cost.

A designer building toward a below-threshold stable state should
front-load fast-internalizing rules wherever possible: rules that are
practiced constantly, rules whose mechanical action and fictional
consequence are unified, rules that respond to player actions the player
is already taking. Slow-internalizing rules are not automatically wrong,
but they deserve scrutiny that fast-internalizing rules do not. A
slow-internalizing rule that is load-bearing at the stable state may
still be worth keeping, with the recognition that it will impose entry
costs for longer than a fast-internalizing equivalent. A
slow-internalizing rule that is only marginally load-bearing is a
stronger candidate for removal than the framework’s standard diagnostic
alone would identify.

**The stable state is not the only design problem.** Every design has a
stable state: the experience it produces once the learning curve is
complete. The fiction engine paradigm’s stable state is
fiction-transparent play, where the procedure operates in the background
and the fiction remains the primary object of attention. That stable
state is worth designing for. It is also only part of the design
problem.

A design that produces an excellent stable state but a poor entry
experience loses most of its players before they reach it. The entry
experience and the learning curve are not preludes to the design; they
are part of it. This is not an argument for making all games easier to
learn. Complexity and depth can be real design values, and designs that
reward significant investment are legitimate. It is an argument for
intentionality: a designer who knows their design has a long learning
curve, and knows why, is better positioned to support players through it
than one who treats the learning curve as a natural consequence of the
design’s depth rather than as a designed feature in its own right.

**The solo implication deserves its own treatment.** The threshold
essay’s observation that the threshold is effectively lower for solo
design is correct, but the trajectory framing makes it more precise. A
solo player’s internalization curve is not merely longer. It is longer
and less reliable, for a structural reason: there is no facilitation
distribution available during the learning phase.

In group play, an experienced player or referee can absorb procedural
weight while others are still building habits. One person at the table
who has internalized the move taxonomy frees the others from having to
consciously perform the classification step themselves; the experienced
player can name the applicable move before the new player has finished
identifying it. This distribution is not available to a solo player.
Every procedural step is the solo player’s sole responsibility, which
means every step that is still in the attentional foreground remains
there until that single player has internalized it alone. The learning
curve is identical in length and harder to complete.

This has a concrete design implication. Solo designs should aim for a
shorter learning curve and a lower entry threshold regardless of their
intended stable state position. The Breathless SRD’s pressure engine
layer is an example of a design commitment whose costs are substantially
higher in solo play than the design’s group context suggests, which is
one reason why the most successful solo Breathless derivatives tend to
either simplify the tracking economy or replace the stress track with
something that has a faster internalization curve. That is not a failure
of the source material. It is a recognition that the trajectory is
different for one player working alone.

## The Teaching Implication

The threshold essay names a practical observation: a game introduced
through its fiction (“here is the situation, here is what your character
might attempt, here is what happened”) feels different from the same
game introduced through its mechanics (“here are the six stats, here are
the move categories, here are the roll triggers”). Both introductions
eventually arrive at the same system. They produce different experiences
of getting there, and those experiences shape how players relate to the
design.

The trajectory framing makes the structural reason visible. Teaching
fiction-first places the player at the design’s stable state before the
mechanics have been introduced. The player’s first orientation is to the
fiction: the situation, the characters, the conditions. When mechanics
emerge from that orientation (you want to force the door, roll; here is
how to interpret the result), they arrive as infrastructure for
something the player is already doing. The attentional habits the stable
state requires are already forming. The learning curve runs in the
background.

Teaching mechanics-first does the opposite. The player’s first
orientation is to the system. The fiction arrives later, filling in
around a procedural architecture already established as the primary
object of attention. That habit — engaging the system consciously — does
not disappear when the fiction is introduced. It competes with the
fiction for the player’s attention during early play.

For fiction engines specifically, the choice is not symmetric. Teaching
fiction-first is consistent with what the design is trying to produce. A
player who encounters the fiction first, and the mechanics second, has
already begun building the attentional habits the stable state requires.

A related implication: the design document’s structure is itself a
teaching choice. A game that opens with the world, the situation, the
characters — and introduces mechanics as they become necessary — makes a
different decision than a game that opens with a structured rules
overview. A fiction engine that opens with a structured rules overview
is asking its reader to begin in the mechanics-first mode, and that
beginning shapes everything that follows. The design document is the
first session.

## Where This Leaves the Framework

The fiction engine framework describes its criteria in synchronic terms:
a game either is or is not producing fictional change, either does or
does not sit below the transparency threshold. The framework’s
diagnostic — remove a rule and observe whether the fiction still moves —
treats the design as a stable object that can be tested in isolation.
This framing is analytically useful. It produces real discriminations.

The trajectory framing adds a dimension the synchronic analysis lacks. A
design is not simply above or below the threshold; it has an entry
position, a learning curve, and a stable state. A rule that passes the
remove-and-observe diagnostic may still impose significant entry costs.
A design whose stable state is below the threshold may lose most of its
players during the learning curve. A design whose stable state is above
the threshold may reach it more reliably than a competing design with a
longer learning curve to a below-threshold destination. The framework’s
criteria evaluate rules in isolation. The trajectory question evaluates
the system over time, across the range of players who will use it.

These are different questions, and they can produce different answers
for the same design. A design evaluated by the framework’s criteria
might look excellent: thin procedures, fictional states, minimal
overhead, outcomes as continuations. The same design evaluated as a
trajectory might reveal a slow-internalizing rule in a critical
position, or an entry experience that does not communicate the design’s
orientation quickly enough to prevent players from establishing
competing attentional habits before the fiction can become primary.
Neither evaluation is wrong. They are operating at different scales —
the synchronic and the temporal — and designs that hold up well under
both deserve more confidence than designs that pass only one.

The framework currently has the tools for the synchronic evaluation. The
trajectory evaluation requires additions: the distinction between
fast-internalizing and slow-internalizing rules, the entry experience as
a separate design problem from the stable state, the question of how the
learning curve is completed and what support the design provides for
completing it. These additions do not contradict the framework’s
existing criteria. They extend the analysis into a dimension the
framework has left unaddressed.

## Designing the Trajectory

A designer who treats their design as a fixed object with stable
properties is optimizing for a single point: the stable state. That
optimization is not wrong. The stable state is what experienced players
inhabit, and a stable state worth reaching is worth designing carefully.

But the stable state is not where new players begin. It is where players
arrive after a trajectory that the design either supports or does not. A
designer who treats their design as a system that changes its properties
through use is optimizing for the whole trajectory — entry, curve, and
stable state — and making deliberate decisions about each.

The fiction engine paradigm’s commitment to thin mediation is partly a
commitment to a short, smooth trajectory: a design that reaches its
stable state quickly and maintains it reliably. Essay 1 showed that
accumulated fiction is the structural property worth protecting. Essay 2
showed that thin mediation creates the feedback conditions under which
accumulated fiction can produce emergent behavior. This essay has argued
that thin mediation also produces a short internalization curve — thin
procedures are fast-internalizing almost by definition — which means the
design reaches the conditions for emergent behavior earlier and holds
them more consistently than designs with longer learning curves.

Understanding this is what allows a designer to choose thin mediation
deliberately rather than by convention, or to choose a longer trajectory
deliberately rather than by accumulation. The choice is not between good
design and bad design. It is between design that knows what it is
building toward and design that discovers its trajectory only after
players have already experienced it.

The trajectory framing has revealed something the earlier essays in this
arc pointed toward without naming. The fiction engine framework
identifies the conditions for a specific kind of play. It does not
produce that play directly; only agents using the design over time can
do that. But the conditions are real, and knowing that they are
conditions rather than guarantees, and that they are experienced by
players across a trajectory rather than at a single point, is the
beginning of designing them with precision. That distinction — between
design properties and the system behaviors they enable — is what the
final essay takes as its subject.

# Properties and Behaviors

*Part of the Fiction Engine companion essay series. This essay concludes
the systems thinking arc of the Fiction Engine companion essay series.
The preceding essays in the arc are “Fiction and Memory,” “The Emergence
Condition,” and “The Threshold as Trajectory.” The full series
accompanies “Fiction Engine: A Design Framework for Tabletop RPGs”
(v1.0, May 2026).*

## What the Fiction Engine Framework Gets Right, and What It Can’t Say

The fiction engine framework is a document about design decisions. It
offers a single test for every rule a designer might write: does this
rule produce a new fictional state, support the conditions for fictional
play, or generate dead overhead? It describes failure modes. It provides
a glossary. It closes with a set of criteria that let a reader assess
whether a given game belongs more or less fully to the paradigm.

The framework is written as if these criteria describe properties of
design documents. Apply the test to a rule and you get an answer. Run
the diagnostic — remove the rule and see if the fiction keeps moving —
and you learn whether the rule is load-bearing or dead weight. The
framework is, in this sense, a set of tools for reading and writing
design documents with precision.

The three preceding essays in this arc have been circling a complication
that this framing produces without acknowledging. Essay 1 showed that
accumulated fiction — the causal tissue connecting prior events to
current situations to future possibilities — cannot be designed into a
document; it can only be produced through play. Essay 2 showed that
emergent behavior requires feedback loops between agents attending to
each other’s outputs, which means it requires agents. Essay 3 showed
that the transparency threshold shifts with player familiarity, which
means a design’s relationship to the threshold is a trajectory through
time rather than a fixed position in a document.

The pattern across all three is consistent: the framework’s properties
are conditions. The behaviors those conditions enable are a different
category. And designing conditions is not the same as producing
behaviors.

This essay makes that argument direct and draws its practical
consequences.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## The Distinction

A design property is a feature of the design document that exists
independently of any particular use. The number of resolution steps in a
procedure is a design property. The presence or absence of a
classification step between fictional intent and fictional outcome is a
design property. Whether a tracked resource has a mandatory diegetic
expression is a design property. All of these can be assessed by reading
the document carefully, before the document has been used once.

A system behavior is a feature of the system-in-use: what happens when
agents interact with the design over time. Fictional momentum is a
system behavior; it exists only when players are generating and
responding to accumulated fictional state, moment to moment. Emergence
is a system behavior; it requires feedback loops between agents, which
requires agents. Fiction-transparency — the experience of procedure
operating in the background while the fiction remains primary — is a
system behavior. It depends on internalization, which takes time and
varies across players. The experience of consequence, of choices having
weight because prior decisions have established asymmetries, is a system
behavior. It depends on accumulated fiction, which only play produces.

None of these can be assessed by reading the document. They are what the
document enables; they are not in the document.

The fiction engine framework describes design properties as if
identifying them is equivalent to specifying system behaviors. A thin
resolution procedure has the design property that could enable
fiction-transparent play. Whether it does enable that play depends on
the system-in-use: the players, the accumulated fiction, the learning
curve, the degree to which the participants are attending to each
other’s outputs rather than to their own agendas. The condition and the
behavior are related — the condition makes the behavior possible — but
they are not the same thing, and the distinction has consequences.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## What the Framework Is Actually Designing

The three preceding essays arrived at a description of what the
framework’s rules are actually protecting, enabling, and shaping.
Bringing those descriptions together makes the pattern plain.

The framework’s insistence on outcomes as continuations rather than
endpoints protects the accumulated causal tissue of the fiction: the
structure that gives each event weight by tying it to prior decisions
and opening it toward future possibilities. The framework cannot produce
that tissue. Only play can. The rules create the conditions under which
the tissue can form: by refusing to break the causal chain with
suspended outcomes, by requiring that fictional states survive the
removal of the game record, by insisting that resolutions advance rather
than close. The conditions are what the designer controls. The causal
tissue is what the system produces.

The framework’s commitment to thin mediation preserves the feedback
loops between agents that emergent behavior requires. When the
procedural layer is thin enough that agents move through it rather than
stopping at it, their attention flows toward each other and toward the
shared fictional state. The feedback loops stay intact. But the loops
are not in the design document; they are in the space between agents
attending to each other’s outputs. The designer creates the conditions.
The agents produce the loops.

The framework’s design choices — fast-internalizing rules, minimal
tracking obligations, coherent resolution cycles — shape a trajectory.
They determine how quickly a design reaches its stable state, how much
cognitive load the entry experience imposes, whether the learning curve
is short enough to complete. But the trajectory is a property of the
design-in-use across time. No design document contains its own learning
curve; that curve is produced by players interacting with the design
across sessions.

A fiction engine is a set of conditions. The fiction is what those
conditions make possible, and what agents actually produce when the
conditions are in place.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## What This Changes Practically

If the framework’s properties are conditions rather than guarantees,
several of its evaluative stances need revision — not replacement, but
precision.

**The question of paradigm membership.** The framework treats belonging
to the fiction engine paradigm as a property of design documents: a game
is more or less fully a fiction engine based on how well its rules
satisfy the criteria. The systems framing suggests a more accurate
formulation: a design belongs more or less fully to the fiction engine
paradigm based on the behaviors it reliably enables in typical use. The
gap between designed conditions and actual behaviors is a fact about the
design’s effectiveness, not a failure of the players.

A design with excellent fiction engine properties that is routinely run
in ways that suppress emergent behavior — because the social agreement
is absent, because the learning curve was never completed, because
participants hold divergent models of what the game is doing — is less
fully a fiction engine in practice than a design with modest fiction
engine properties that consistently enables the relevant behaviors. This
does not make the criterion irrelevant. It means the criterion is
necessary but not sufficient. Designing the conditions is the designer’s
job; producing the behaviors is the system’s job. The designer should
know the difference.

**The failure modes.** The framework names four: overhead accumulation,
suspended outcomes, authority drift, and orientational divergence. They
are presented as design failures, things the design does wrong. The
systems framing reveals something more specific: they are descriptions
of how the conditions for emergent fiction can fail to produce emergent
fiction. Each failure mode is a way the feedback network can degrade.

Overhead accumulation: each additional procedural demand redirects agent
attention from the fiction and from each other toward the system. The
feedback loops weaken not through a single rupture but through
progressive attenuation. Suspended outcomes: a resolution that requires
further resolution is a loop that cannot close; agents cannot respond to
an outcome that has not yet been produced. Authority drift: when the
mechanism that distributes narrative authority begins producing
administrative prompts rather than fictional events, it stops
functioning as a node in the feedback network and becomes an obstacle
between fictional intent and fictional change. Orientational divergence:
when participants hold different models of what the game is doing, they
are effectively running feedback loops on different channels. The
outputs of one agent are not inputs for another because they are not
attending to the same object.

Naming these as conditions for system failure rather than intrinsic
design defects clarifies what the designer’s responsibility actually is.
The designer cannot prevent orientational divergence by writing better
rules; orientational divergence is a failure of the social agreement
that underlies any game. What the designer can do is make the game’s
orientation legible enough that the social agreement is easier to reach
and easier to maintain. That is a different design task, and it produces
different design decisions.

**The social dimension.** The framework’s section on the social
dimension is the most honest passage in the document: “A fiction engine
is a social agreement before it is a mechanical system. The rules
formalize that agreement, but they cannot substitute for it.” The
systems framing shows precisely why this is true rather than offering it
as an observation about table culture.

The social agreement is the ground-level condition for the feedback
loops that everything else depends on. Mutual sensitivity between agents
— participants genuinely attending to each other’s outputs, treating the
fiction as a common object rather than a personal stage — is not a
cultural nicety that improves a game running on good procedures. It is
the functional precondition for the procedures to produce emergent
behavior at all. Without it, the feedback loops do not form. Without the
loops, emergence is not possible regardless of how well the design
conditions are specified.

Design can support the social agreement by making the game’s orientation
legible, by providing procedures that keep the fiction primary, by
minimizing the attentional demands that pull participants toward the
system and away from each other. Design cannot create the agreement. The
participants create the agreement. This is the correct hierarchy, and
the framework’s social dimension section states it. The systems framing
explains the mechanism behind the statement.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## What the Framework Gets Right

The argument so far has been a development, not a critique. The
distinction between conditions and behaviors does not invalidate the
framework’s criteria or its analytical tools. It clarifies what those
tools are actually for.

The framework’s core test is correct. Rules that produce fictional
change rather than overhead do create better conditions for accumulated
fiction and emergent behavior than rules that generate administrative
obligations without fictional output. The diagnostic — remove a rule and
observe whether the fiction keeps moving — is a genuine test of whether
a rule is creating conditions for the relevant system behaviors or
simply adding procedural weight. The test works because design
properties and system behaviors are correlated, even if they are not
identical. Better conditions reliably produce better behaviors. Worse
conditions reliably degrade them.

The failure modes are real. Overhead accumulation does degrade the
feedback conditions. Authority drift does produce obstacles rather than
conduits. The framework names these correctly even if the framing
positions them as design errors rather than condition failures. The
practical guidance they produce is the same either way: remove the
overhead, repair the authority mechanism, address the orientational
divergence. The systems framing adds precision to why this guidance is
correct, not a reason to doubt it.

The framework’s position-taking also holds. It correctly refuses
universality: does not claim that fiction engine principles are criteria
all good design should satisfy, positions itself as one paradigm among
several, acknowledges that other paradigms produce experiences it is not
designed to produce. This is the intellectual honesty that makes the
framework worth developing. It means the framework can be pressed
further, as these essays have pressed it, without the pressure reading
as attack. A framework that admits its own scope can be extended at its
edges without being undermined at its center.

The framework’s analytical tools — the fiction/overhead distinction, the
transparency threshold, the glossary terms — produce discriminating
analysis when applied to real design decisions. The four-designs essay
demonstrated this. The faction essay demonstrated it at the paradigm’s
edge. These tools work. The argument here is not that they should be
replaced but that the framework’s self-description — its account of what
it is doing when it applies these tools — should be updated to reflect
what it is actually doing.

What the framework gets right, taken seriously, is significant. The
procedural commitments it describes are genuine structural conditions
for the class of play it is designed to produce. A designer who builds
to those conditions is building toward the relevant behaviors reliably
rather than hoping for them incidentally. That is what good design does:
it makes the desired behaviors probable. Recognizing that probable is
not certain, and that conditions are not guarantees, does not diminish
the value of getting the conditions right.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## The Honest Account

A fiction engine is a design that creates reliable conditions for
emergent fiction, accumulated fictional consequence, and
fiction-transparent play. It achieves this through a specific set of
structural commitments: thin mediation that preserves agent-to-agent
feedback loops, rules that protect the causal tissue of accumulated
fictional state, a procedural layer that internalizes efficiently and
operates in the background once internalized.

A fiction engine cannot guarantee emergent fiction, accumulated
consequence, or fiction-transparent play. It can maximize the conditions
under which these behaviors arise. The behaviors themselves are
properties of the system-in-use — the design, the agents, the
accumulated fiction, and the time it takes for all three to interact.

The honest account of what a fiction engine is asks more of the
designer, not less. A designer who understands that they are building
conditions rather than outcomes holds those conditions with precision:
they know which rules are doing what kind of work, which elements of the
design belong to the learning curve versus the stable state, what the
social agreement requires and what the design can support without
substituting for it. That precision is what allows the design commitment
to remain useful under pressure rather than collapsing into dogma when
the designed conditions do not produce the expected behaviors.

Concretely, it changes how a designer responds when the design is not
producing the intended experience at a given table. A framework that
treats design properties as system behaviors leads to one diagnosis: the
game is not working because the rules are wrong, so change the rules. A
framework that treats design properties as conditions leads to a more
discriminating set of questions. Are the conditions present? Is the
social agreement in place, or are participants holding divergent models?
Has the learning curve been completed, or are procedural demands still
foregrounded? Is accumulated fiction building across sessions, or is
each session resetting the fiction to something near its starting state?
Each question points to a different intervention, and most of them point
away from the rules entirely. The designer who knows the difference
between conditions and behaviors can ask the right question rather than
the familiar one.

The fiction engine paradigm is a specific bet about what conditions are
worth creating. Thin mediation over thick procedure. Accumulated
fictional state over episodic reset. Short learning curves and efficient
stable states. These are tradeoffs, and every tradeoff has costs. What
is given up to stay below the transparency threshold is real: structural
scaffolding, genre texture, pacing economy, the dramatic rhythm that
procedural foreground can produce. A designer who makes that bet should
make it deliberately, knowing what the costs are and what the conditions
enable in return.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## The Open Question

This series has been precise about what the fiction engine paradigm’s
conditions enable. It has been less precise about what occurs on the
other side of the tradeoffs: what system behaviors the pressure engine
enables that a fiction engine cannot, whether emergence is even the
right concept for what Blades in the Dark produces when running at its
best, what a theory of Fate’s authorial satisfaction would look like if
developed with the same rigor applied here to fictional momentum.

These questions belong to a different arc, and to the theorists of the
paradigms that sit across the threshold. The comparison available from
the fiction engine side is necessarily limited. Other paradigms have
their own structural commitments, their own conditions, their own
behaviors that those conditions enable. Evaluating any of them using
criteria borrowed from the fiction engine paradigm produces bad
analysis, and the fiction engine framework is clear about this. The
invitation here is only to apply the same rigor inward: to hold the
fiction engine paradigm’s own properties and behaviors with the same
analytical honesty applied in the faction essay to the paradigm’s
limits.

What the series has done is make the fiction engine paradigm’s project
precise enough to see clearly against the alternatives — not as a
universal standard but as a design position that knows what it is for
and what it costs.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Conclusion

Four essays. One arc.

The first showed that accumulated fiction — the causal tissue connecting
prior events to current conditions to future possibilities — is a
structural property with architectural consequences. The framework’s
procedural commitments protect it without naming it. Designers who build
fiction engines are, among other things, building structures that allow
that tissue to form.

The second showed that thin mediation creates the feedback conditions
for emergent behavior. When agents attend to each other’s outputs across
accumulated fictional state rather than to a procedural classification
layer, the feedback loops that produce emergent fiction become possible.
The framework’s commitment to transparency is not a stylistic
preference; it is a structural condition for the class of play the
paradigm is designed to produce.

The third showed that a design is a trajectory rather than a fixed
object: an entry experience, a learning curve, and a stable state. The
transparency threshold is player-variable, which means the design’s
relationship to the threshold changes as it is used. Designing the
conditions includes designing the learning curve, not only the
destination.

This essay showed that the conditions the framework identifies are not
design properties but conditions for system behaviors. They are real,
achievable, worth designing for precisely. They are properties of the
system-in-use — the design, the agents, and the accumulated fiction they
produce together — not guarantees inscribed in the document.

The framework correctly identifies the conditions. What the conditions
produce is up to the system.

# One Agent, One Loop

*This essay is part of the Fiction Engine companion essay series,
following the systems thinking arc: “Fiction and Memory,” “The Emergence
Condition,” “The Threshold as Trajectory,” and “Properties and
Behaviors.” It is also a companion to “Fiction Engine: A Design
Framework for Tabletop RPGs” (v1.0, May 2026).*

## The Solo Fiction Engine as a Distinct Design Problem

The solo dimension has appeared three times in this series without
becoming its subject. The transparency threshold essay noted that the
threshold is effectively lower for solo design because no facilitation
distribution is available. The four-designs essay observed that three of
its four designs are solo-capable and that Breathless’s tracking load
carries different costs in solo play than its group context suggests.
The systems thinking arc established the feedback loop conditions that
emergent fiction requires and noted, without developing the point, that
those conditions change when there is only one player.

What has been deferred is this: solo fiction engine design is not group
fiction engine design with one player. The structural conditions are
different, not diminished. The feedback network has a different shape.
The internalization problem has a different character. The contribution
of accumulated fiction has a different source. Each of these differences
produces specific design decisions that a theory of group fiction engine
design cannot fully anticipate. This essay makes those differences
precise.

## The Subtraction Error

The threshold essay frames the solo problem as a resource deficit: a
solo player has no facilitation distribution available, so the threshold
is effectively lower. The observation is correct. The framing is
incomplete.

Treating solo play as group play minus a resource implies that the
structural conditions for the relevant behaviors are present but
strained — that the same feedback loops run, only with more load on one
participant. This is not what the structural difference is. The feedback
network does not thin when a group reduces to one player. It changes
shape.

In group play, the feedback loop runs between agents. A player describes
what their character attempts. The referee or another player produces a
response. The first player responds to that response. The loop closes
over material generated by another agent — material the first player did
not produce and could not fully anticipate. The condition for emergent
development is met: the loop is closing over genuinely independent
outputs.

In solo play, there is one agent. The loop cannot close between agents
because no second agent exists. Whatever the solo player produces in
response to the fiction is a response to material they have, to varying
degrees, already shaped. Left entirely to their own outputs, the loop
closes over the player’s own intent. Emergence of the relevant kind —
the sensation that the fiction is going somewhere nobody planned — is
not possible from a single agent generating all the fiction’s content.
The player can surprise themselves at the margins, through the
specificity of character and situation, but the structural condition for
the kind of emergence the paradigm is designed to produce is absent.

The oracle is what changes this. Not as a convenience for replacing the
referee, not as a yes/no generator for resolving uncertainty, but as the
structural mechanism that introduces outputs the solo player did not
generate. It is the second agent in the feedback loop.

Without an oracle, or something functionally equivalent, solo fiction
engine design does not have the conditions for emergent play. The player
is only ever responding to themselves.

## The Oracle as Second Agent

The fiction engine framework defines the oracle as a mechanism for
distributing narrative authority in solo or low-referee play, one that
takes a closed question about the fiction and produces a yes or no
answer, often with gradations or complications. The functional criterion
is that its outputs should read as fictional events rather than prompts
requiring interpretive work before play can continue.

This is the right criterion for output quality. It says nothing about
what the oracle is structurally doing.

With the feedback loop argument in place, the structural criterion
becomes more precise. An oracle functions as a second agent when its
outputs are independent of the player’s intent and constrained by the
accumulated fictional state. Both conditions are necessary. Each,
without the other, produces a different failure.

An oracle whose outputs are too closely determined by player input is a
weak second agent. If the oracle consistently confirms what the player
intended — if the answer is essentially “yes, and here is the fiction
you described” — the loop closes over the player’s own intent. No
independent material enters the fiction. The player is generating all
the content and receiving confirmation of it. The fiction is coherent
but not surprising in the way accumulated fiction with genuine feedback
can be.

An oracle whose outputs are disconnected from the accumulated fictional
state is also a weak second agent, for the opposite reason. A random
table that ignores fictional context — that produces outputs with no
relationship to what has been established — generates noise rather than
surprise. The loop closes over material that contradicts or ignores the
accumulated state. The fiction does not develop; it fragments.

The productive oracle sits between these positions. It produces outputs
that the player did not generate, which means the loop has genuinely
independent material to close over. It produces outputs constrained by
the accumulated fictional state, which means that independent material
connects to the causal tissue rather than arriving from outside it. The
surprise is real because the player did not cause it. The surprise is
coherent because the fiction prepared the ground for it.

This is the Traceable criterion from the RAT checklist applied to oracle
design specifically. A traceable oracle output is one that could not
have been predicted from the player’s intent but can be followed back to
prior fictional events that made it possible. That combination —
independent of intent, grounded in accumulated state — is what makes an
oracle function as a second agent rather than as a resolution tool.

The practical implication for design: oracle systems that produce
fictional events are building more useful material than oracle systems
that produce binary answers. An oracle that outputs “yes, but the
contact has already left the city” is introducing an independent
fictional fact with consequences already present in the answer. The
player did not generate that fact, and it connects to the accumulated
fiction (there is a contact; the city has an established geography;
timing exists in the fiction). An oracle that outputs “yes” and leaves
the player to generate the complication has resolved the uncertainty
while leaving the generation of independent material to the player. Both
can work. The first is doing more of the second-agent work.

## The Internalization Problem Without Assistance

Essay 3 established that the transparency threshold is player-variable:
the same design can sit above or below it depending on who is playing
and how long they have been playing it. Internalization is the mechanism
that moves a design from one position to the other over time, and
internalization requires repetition.

In group play, an experienced player or referee can carry procedural
weight while others are still building habits. The classification step
that one player has internalized runs fast enough that other players
never have to consciously perform it themselves. A new player at a table
with an experienced referee is working inside a system that is already
partially internalized on their behalf. The learning curve is shorter,
in practice, than the design would require of a single player learning
alone.

In solo play, every procedural step is the solo player’s sole
responsibility. There is no assistance during the learning phase. The
internalization curve is the same length as for any single player
working alone — which is, in most cases, longer than the curve for any
individual player in a group, because group play distributes the
learning. A solo player internalizing a move taxonomy or a tracking
obligation is doing work that a group player partly shares.

This produces a specific design priority. Solo designs should front-load
fast-internalizing rules: rules tightly coupled to actions the player is
already performing, where the mechanical action and the fictional event
are the same action or close to it. The 24XX defense mechanic
internalizes quickly in solo play for the same reason it internalizes
quickly in group play — the player practices it every time a hit occurs,
which is throughout play — but the advantage is more significant in solo
play because there is no alternative source of procedural support. Rules
that require a separate classification step before the fiction can
advance, or that activate rarely enough that the player never builds
habitual responses to them, remain foregrounded longer in solo play than
they would in group play.

Breathless is the case where this cost is most consequential, precisely
because the majority of its derivatives are solo designs. The die
degradation and reset mechanics internalize quickly: they are coupled to
resolution, which is constant, and the reset declaration is a single
clear action with an immediate fictional consequence. These elements are
as solo-appropriate as any in this design neighborhood. The stress track
is the element that does not have this property. It imposes an ongoing
tracking obligation separate from the resolution action, and it stays
somewhat foregrounded by design — the pressure engine experience depends
on the player being aware of the stress count, which means the tracking
is not supposed to recede into background infrastructure. That design
choice carries a different cost for a solo player learning alone than it
does for a group player with facilitation available. It does not make
Breathless wrong as a source for solo adaptation. It makes the stress
track the element most worth reconsidering, and most worth reconsidering
first, when adapting toward a lower-threshold stable state.

## Accumulated Fiction with One Generative Agent

Essay 1 established that accumulated fiction — the causal tissue
connecting prior events to current conditions — is what gives
consequences weight and choices non-equivalence. In group play, multiple
agents contribute to that accumulation. Different players establish
different fictional facts from inside their characters’ perspectives.
The referee adds others from the world’s side. The oracle, where
present, adds others from outside any participant’s intent. The
accumulated state is richer and more unexpected than any single
participant could produce alone.

In solo play, one agent generates all contributions to the accumulated
fiction except what the oracle introduces.

This creates a structural asymmetry. The player’s own contributions to
the fiction are, by definition, expected — they are the player’s
choices, emerging from the player’s model of the character and
situation. The oracle’s contributions are the only source of material
the player did not generate and could not have fully anticipated. The
causal tissue exists on both sides of this divide, but the tissue on the
player-generated side carries less of the surprise that makes
accumulated fiction productive. Prior decisions accumulate, but they
accumulate as the player expected them to. What the oracle contributes
accumulates differently: it adds facts the player had no part in
generating, which means those facts can contradict the player’s
expectations, constrain the player’s options in ways they did not
foresee, and produce the sensation of the fiction going somewhere nobody
planned.

The design implication is direct. Solo fiction engines should be built
to maximize oracle contribution to accumulated state — not only to
resolve individual uncertain outcomes but to introduce fictional facts
that will remain in the fiction after the moment of their introduction.
An oracle that produces an event generates a fact. That fact is now part
of the accumulated fiction. Future oracle outputs can be constrained by
it. The player’s future actions are now responding to something they did
not generate. The more the oracle contributes facts of this kind, the
richer the unexpected side of the accumulated fiction becomes.

This is a different design priority than oracle quality as the framework
defines it. The framework’s criterion — does the oracle output read as a
fictional event rather than a prompt? — addresses what the output does
to the moment of play. The accumulated state criterion addresses what
the output does to the fiction over time. Both matter. The second is the
one solo design specifically needs to build toward.

## The Distinct Design Problem

The three structural differences named above — the feedback loop with
one agent, the internalization curve without assistance, the accumulated
fiction with one generative source — produce a specific design profile
for solo fiction engines. Stated plainly:

A solo fiction engine requires an oracle that functions as a second
agent in the feedback loop. This is not optional in the way that oracles
are optional in group play, where a referee can fill the role. In solo
play, if nothing produces outputs independent of the player’s intent,
the conditions for emergent fiction are absent. The oracle is
load-bearing at the structural level, not as a convenience feature.

A solo fiction engine requires fast-internalizing rules at the entry
phase, because there is no facilitation available to smooth the learning
curve. The threshold is effectively lower not because cognitive load is
undistributed — though it is — but because the feedback loop conditions
are more fragile with one agent. A solo player spending significant
attention on procedural management is a player not attending to the
fiction and not attending to oracle outputs. Both of those are the
materials the feedback loop needs. Procedural overhead in solo play is
not just friction. It is reduction of the loop’s operating conditions.

A solo fiction engine requires oracles designed to generate accumulated
fictional facts, not only to resolve uncertainty in the moment. The
player generates the player’s contributions to the causal tissue. The
oracle generates the unexpected side. A solo design that treats the
oracle as a resolution tool rather than a generative agent is building a
fiction that accumulates largely within the player’s expectations. The
causal tissue forms, but it does not surprise.

None of these is a revision of the fiction engine paradigm’s core
commitments. All three follow directly from applying the systems
thinking arc’s arguments to the structural conditions solo play creates.
The feedback loop argument explains why the oracle is load-bearing. The
internalization argument explains why the entry threshold must be lower.
The accumulated state argument explains why oracle generativity is more
important than the framework’s existing oracle guidance addresses.

## What This Means for How the Oracle Is Positioned

The teaching implication from essay 3 was that a game introduced through
its fiction — here is the situation, here is what your character might
attempt, here is what happened — reaches its stable state faster than
one introduced through its resolution procedure. The fiction is where
the stable state lives; beginning there establishes the attentional
habits the stable state requires.

In solo play, the fiction is where the oracle is. The oracle is not a
procedural add-on consulted after the player has established their
intent and framed their action. It is the entity the player is in
dialogue with throughout play. The feedback loop runs between the player
and the oracle, and that loop is the structural mechanism through which
the fiction develops.

This suggests that a solo fiction engine introduced through its oracle —
here is the situation, here is what you might ask, here is how the
fiction responds — reaches its stable state faster than one introduced
through its resolution mechanics. The resolution mechanics are
infrastructure. The oracle is where the fiction lives in solo play. It
should be where the design begins, both in the design document and at
the table.

There is a practical consequence for how oracle systems are presented in
solo designs. Many solo games present the oracle as one component among
several: rules for resolution, rules for advancement, an oracle section,
tables for scene generation. This organization treats the oracle as
equivalent in weight to the other components. The structural argument
suggests a different organization: the oracle is the primary mechanism,
the one around which the other components are oriented, because it is
the structural source of the conditions the paradigm requires. A
designer who understands this can build toward it explicitly — can make
the oracle the first thing the player encounters, the thing the design
teaches before it teaches anything else.

## The Solo Fiction Engine as Its Own Object

The series has applied the fiction engine framework to group designs, to
specific games within and adjacent to the paradigm, to edge cases at the
paradigm’s boundary, and now to the structural conditions solo play
creates. Each application has revealed something about the paradigm that
the framework alone does not say.

This application reveals that solo fiction engine design is its own
design problem. Not a simplified version of group design. Not group
design with one player and an oracle substituted for a referee. A
different configuration with different structural conditions that
require deliberate design decisions at the level of the oracle, the
learning curve, and the accumulation of fictional state.

A designer adapting a group fiction engine for solo play is not reducing
the design. They are changing its shape. The feedback loop that ran
between agents must now run between one player and an oracle that is
doing structural work the framework does not fully account for. The
internalization curve that was smoothed by facilitation distribution
must now be smoothed by the design itself. The accumulated fiction that
was enriched by multiple agents’ contributions must now be enriched by
oracle generativity rather than group generation.

Each of these is a design decision, and each requires knowing what it is
for. A solo oracle designed as a second agent produces different design
decisions than a solo oracle designed as a resolution tool. The
distinction is not about which tables are included or how questions are
phrased. It is about what the oracle is being asked to do in the
feedback loop, and whether the design is built to let it do that work.

The fiction engine framework offers a test for every rule: does this
rule produce a new fictional state, support the conditions for fictional
play, or generate dead overhead? In solo design, the oracle test is a
version of this question applied to the mechanism that makes the
paradigm’s conditions possible for one player. Does this oracle produce
independent fictional content that accumulates into the causal tissue?
Does it close the feedback loop over material the player did not
generate? Does it do this reliably enough to sustain the conditions for
emergent play across a session, across sessions, across the arc of a
campaign?

A solo oracle that passes those tests is doing what a second agent does.
A solo fiction engine built around such an oracle is building the
conditions the paradigm requires, under the structural conditions solo
play creates. That is the design problem precisely stated. The rest is
design.

# What the Engine Is For

*This essay opens the Fiction Engine limits sub-series, a companion to
“Fiction Engine: A Design Framework for Tabletop RPGs” (v1.0, May 2026).
It follows the systems thinking arc: “Fiction and Memory,” “The
Emergence Condition,” “The Threshold as Trajectory,” and “Properties and
Behaviors.”*

## On the Limits of the Fiction Engine Paradigm

Every design system has a fundamental logic. Not a set of rules — a
logic: the underlying assumption about what the player is doing, what
the resolution procedure is measuring, and what the experience is
supposed to feel like from inside the activity. Rules are expressions of
that logic. When the logic is well-matched to the experience a design is
trying to produce, the rules feel inevitable. When it is not, the rules
compensate, and the compensation shows.

The problem is not poor execution. It is that a system’s logic does not
stay neutral when applied to experiences outside its native territory.
It reshapes them. The fiction it produces is not the fiction the
designer intended; it is the fiction the system’s logic is capable of
producing, regardless of what the designer put in the text. Genre
labels, setting descriptions, and evocative art do not change this. The
logic works on whatever it is given, and what it produces is always, to
some degree, in its own image.

This is not a criticism of any specific system. It is a structural
observation about all of them, including this one.

## The Fiction Engine’s Fundamental Logic

The fiction engine framework is built around one cycle: the player
describes what their character attempts within the fiction; the game
produces an outcome; that outcome modifies the fiction; play continues.
The procedural layer in the middle should be as thin as the situation
allows.

What this cycle assumes is worth stating precisely, because the
assumption is doing more work than the cycle’s description suggests.

It assumes an *agent embedded in a situation*. The player is inside the
fiction, not above it. They are responding to what is immediately
present — the conditions, the characters, the pressures of this moment
in this place — rather than modeling the situation from outside it. What
they choose emerges from that embeddedness: what would this character
do, given what is here.

It assumes *transformation as the desirable output*. Every outcome
should change the situation. A result that leaves the fiction suspended
rather than transformed is a design failure. The fiction is always
moving, always producing a new state that opens into the next moment.

It assumes *continuation as the shape of consequence*. No outcome is a
stop. Yes and, yes but, no but, no and: all four keep something in
motion. Failure is a change of situation, not an end to it. The fiction
engine does not produce closure; it produces succession.

These assumptions are not universally true of all valuable play
experiences. They are commitments — to embeddedness over elevation, to
transformation over stasis, to continuation over closure. Accepting them
produces one kind of play. The moment you want something else, the logic
starts working against you.

## What Colonization Looks Like

When a system with a specific logic is applied to an experience its
logic does not natively support, the result is not simply a weaker
version of the target experience. It is a different experience — one
that resembles the target on the surface while substituting the system’s
native logic underneath.

The colonization is often invisible precisely because it is total. If
the only resolution framework available is the one in front of you, you
will tend to read your experience through its categories. The fiction it
produces begins to feel normal. The target experience — the one that
required a different logic — recedes from view, not because it was never
wanted but because the available system was never capable of producing
it, and after long enough exposure the gap stops being legible.

The fiction engine is not immune to this. Its logic — embedded agency,
transformation, continuation — will colonize experiences that require
the opposite of any of these things, if applied to them without
recognition of the mismatch. It will produce a fiction that keeps
transforming when the experience requires dread, which means the fiction
will not hold still long enough to close in. It will produce open
continuation when the experience requires convergence toward a fixed
answer. It will put the player inside the situation when the experience
requires them above it. It will make procedure invisible when the
experience requires procedure to be felt.

None of these are failures of the rules. They are expressions of the
logic. The rules are working correctly. The logic is wrong for the job.

## What This Sub-Series Is Doing

The companion essays to this point have moved outward: from the
framework’s core commitments, to the transparency threshold that marks
its boundary, to specific designs within and adjacent to the paradigm,
to edge cases where the paradigm ends and adjacent territory begins. The
systems thinking arc then moved inward, examining the structural
mechanisms behind the paradigm’s commitments.

This sub-series moves in a different direction: toward the paradigm’s
limits as a logic, not as a position on a continuum.

The transparency threshold is a measure of how much procedural mediation
a design allows before the player’s attention shifts from fiction to
system. It describes where designs sit relative to each other on a
single dimension. The limit question is different: not how much
mediation, but what the mediation is for — what experiential assumption
the logic is built around — and what happens to experiences that require
a different assumption.

The question is not what the fiction engine does poorly. It is what the
fiction engine’s logic *does to* experiences it is not built for. The
distinction matters because poorly done and structurally mismatched are
not the same failure. A poorly executed fiction engine can be fixed by
better design. A structurally mismatched one cannot be fixed by design
at all; only by recognizing the mismatch and choosing accordingly.

Five experiential territories warrant examination.

**Powerlessness.** The experience of dread — of something closing in
that the character cannot escape, cannot transform, cannot act against
productively — requires that the fiction refuse to advance in the way
the fiction engine demands it must. The outcome that leaves the
situation unchanged is not a design failure here. It is the experience.

**Convergence toward a fixed answer.** Mystery and puzzle require
accumulation toward closure: a state of sufficient information that
makes a correct conclusion possible, and a moment of synthesis with real
stakes. The fiction engine produces open transformation; these
experiences require directed convergence. The clue economy is not
overhead from this perspective. It is the mechanism that makes “enough
to conclude” a real condition rather than a felt impression.

**Procedural tension as the experience itself.** Some designs foreground
procedure because the procedure is the tension — the visible management
of risk, the felt cost of acting under pressure. The fiction engine’s
commitment to transparent procedure does not merely fail to produce this
experience; it prevents it. A stress track the player has stopped
noticing is not producing the experience the design was built for.

**Strategic optimization.** The player above the situation — modeling
multiple turns, optimizing across persistent state, planning rather than
responding — requires elevation the fiction engine’s embedded logic
structurally refuses. The fun is in the quality of the plan, not in the
quality of the response to what just happened.

**Character construction as primary engagement.** Thin procedure removes
the object of engagement when what the player wants is a character
system rich enough to build and optimize over time. This is not a
preference difference. It is a structural consequence of what
transparent procedure does to character systems: it makes them legible,
and legibility is incompatible with the depth that
construction-as-engagement requires.

Each case represents a distinct mechanism of mismatch. Dread inverts the
core commitment. Mystery requires a structural object the logic cannot
produce. Procedural tension requires what the logic treats as a failure
mode. Strategic play requires a positional relationship to the fiction
the logic refuses. Character construction requires an opacity the logic
dissolves.

The essays that follow examine each in turn. They do not argue that the
fiction engine paradigm should be different. They argue that a designer
who knows what the logic enables — and what it colonizes — is better
positioned to choose deliberately than one who discovers the mismatch
after the fact.

# Powerlessness

*This essay is the first in the Fiction Engine limits sub-series and is
part of the Fiction Engine companion essay series. It is a companion to
“Fiction Engine: A Design Framework for Tabletop RPGs” (v1.0, May 2026)
and reads alongside the framing essay “What the Engine Is For.”*

## The Experience the Engine Cannot Produce

The fiction engine framework treats outcomes as continuations. Every
result — success, failure, partial success — must advance the fiction
into a new state. The result that leaves the fiction suspended rather
than transformed is a design failure. Outcomes should open, not close.
This is not an aesthetic preference; it is the structural logic the
paradigm runs on.

That logic is directly incompatible with dread as an experience. Not
with horror as a genre, not with dark content or mortal stakes or
terrible things happening to characters. Those are all available to a
fiction engine without contradiction. Dread specifically — the
experiential state in which agency feels not merely insufficient but
counterproductive, in which the situation is closing in rather than
opening out — requires the fiction to behave in ways the fiction engine
is designed to prevent.

The mismatch is not a bug in the paradigm. It is the paradigm working
correctly in territory where its correctness is precisely the problem.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Horror and Dread Are Not the Same Property

Horror is a property of fiction’s content: the things that happen, the
threats that are present, the nature of what the characters are facing.
A fiction engine can sustain horror content without difficulty. A
monster that cannot be defeated by conventional means is still a
fictional fact the situation responds to. A character who witnesses
something that breaks their understanding of the world is still in a
fictional state that the fiction can continue from. The situation
transforms. Things go wrong and the aftermath is worse than before. The
engine keeps running, and what it produces is recognizably horror —
fiction with terrible things in it.

Dread is a property of the player’s relationship to the fiction: a
specific experiential state in which the player has the sensation that
what they do does not help, that each attempt makes the situation worse
rather than better or simply different, that the field of viable action
is not just currently limited but systematically narrowing. Dread is not
produced by the content of the fiction. It is produced by the structural
behavior of the fiction in response to player action.

This distinction matters because conflating them produces a false
diagnosis when a fiction engine fails to produce dread. That failure is
not evidence that the engine cannot handle dark content. The engine is
handling dark content fine; dread requires something other than dark
content, and that something is what the engine structurally refuses.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## What Dread Requires

Two structural properties produce dread. The fiction engine provides
neither.

The first is foreclosure. In a dread experience, options must narrow
rather than transform. A failed escape attempt should produce a narrower
field than the one before it — not a different field, a smaller one. The
fiction engine produces continuation even in failure: a no-but result
means the attempt failed and something changed, and the something that
changed is a new fictional state the character can act from. The
character is worse off, but they are worse off in a way that opens new
responses. That is the continuation logic operating correctly.
Foreclosure requires the logic to operate differently: the continuation
is a reduction. The character is worse off in a way that removes options
that existed before.

The fiction engine has no mechanism for distinguishing “things changed”
from “things changed in a direction that closes.” Its output grammar —
yes-and, yes-but, no-but, no-and — describes qualitative change but not
directional narrowing. From inside the continuation logic, all
continuation looks equivalent: the fiction moved, and now there is
something new to respond to.

The second structural property is productive stasis. Some of what dread
requires is not change in the wrong direction but no useful change at
all: the character acts, the situation does not respond, or responds
only to press in further. The door remains locked. The phone continues
not to connect. The escape route circles back. These are not stuck
situations waiting for the right action to unlock them. They are the
experience. The stasis is the point.

The framework is explicit about this kind of result: a suspended outcome
indicates a design problem. That judgment is correct inside the fiction
engine paradigm, where a suspended fiction is a fiction that has stopped
functioning. In dread, the suspension is not a malfunction. It is the
designed experience of a system that has decided you cannot win. Calling
it a design failure is the framework applying its logic accurately to a
territory where its logic does not serve.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## The Continuation Logic as the Source of the Problem

The yes-and / yes-but / no-but / no-and structure exists to ensure the
fiction keeps moving. Every branch stays open. No result is final in the
sense of leaving the player without a next move.

Consider a failed escape in dread-forward territory. A no-but result:
the escape fails, but something has changed. The guards have moved,
revealing a gap elsewhere. The door wouldn’t open, but the effort
revealed a weakness in the frame. The fiction engine has done its job. A
new fictional state exists. The player has options.

In dread, the desired result of a failed escape is not a new opening. It
is the same closing trap, now more inescapable. The no-but has generated
a forward path, and generating a forward path is exactly what the
experience requires the fiction not to do. The engine’s success at
continuation is what prevents the experience. It cannot produce the
phenomenological difference between succession — things keep changing —
and foreclosure — the range of things keeps shrinking.

This is not a problem the engine can fix by running differently. The
continuation commitment is not a setting; it is the architecture. A
fiction engine that withheld continuations would not be a fiction engine
producing dread. It would be something else.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## What Dread-Capable Designs Use Instead

Two designs that produce dread successfully use mechanisms that the
fiction engine’s transparency commitment would dissolve.

*Dread* (Tiltfactor, 2005) resolves action through a Jenga tower. The
tower is the procedure, and the procedure is visible, accumulating, and
irreversible. Each pull makes the tower less stable. The player can see
the current state of the system. Each action has an immediate, physical
cost that outlasts the action itself. When the tower falls, the
character is gone. The dread is not produced by what is happening inside
the fiction. It is produced by what is happening to a physical object
that the player is degrading in real time.

This sits above the transparency threshold by necessity. The dread
requires the player’s attention to be on the procedure — to see the
tower, feel the resistance, know the cumulative effect of every prior
pull. A fiction engine that absorbed the tower mechanic into background
infrastructure, that made it a roll rather than a physical act, would
eliminate the experience. The foreground procedure is not in service of
the dread. It is the dread.

*Ten Candles* (Cavalry Games, 2015) produces dread through explicit,
literal irreversibility. Candles go out; they do not come back. Traits
are burned; they are gone. The player’s resources are visibly,
irrecoverably depleting across the session. The design makes foreclosure
mechanical and legible. The player is not inferring that things are
getting worse. They can count the candles. The shrinking resource is not
a fictional state the player must reason about; it is a number they can
see moving in one direction.

Both designs confirm the same structural requirement: dread needs
procedure the player can see, feel, and lose to. Not procedure that
produces fictional states describing loss, but procedure that is itself
the loss — immediate, accumulating, and impossible to reverse through
clever play. Transparency dissolves it because transparency removes the
visible, foregrounded cost. The fiction engine’s commitment to thin,
fast-internalizing procedure is its commitment to the player’s attention
staying in the fiction. Dread needs attention on the procedure.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## What the Fiction Engine Can Produce Instead

The engine is not entirely without resources in dread territory.
Accumulated fictional state can produce something functionally adjacent
to dread through a different mechanism: causal narrowing.

A character who has lost their allies, burned their most reliable
resource, established a reputation that forecloses the alliances they
would need — whose accumulated history has made the situation genuinely
more constrained at the level of fictional fact — can experience
something like dread through the weight of what the fiction has built
up. The options are not just fewer; they are fewer because of
established, irreversible fictional events that the fiction cannot
contradict. The burned bridge cannot be un-burned. The debt called in
cannot be retroactively paid. Prior play has made the present situation
what it is, and the situation is closing.

This is not the same experience as procedural dread, and the difference
is structural. Accumulated fictional narrowing requires the player to
hold the causal tissue in mind and reason from it. The dread is
interpretive: the player understands that the situation is worsening,
traces the history, recognizes the shape of what is happening. The Jenga
tower produces dread immediately and in the body. Accumulated fictional
narrowing produces the understanding of dread, cognitively and sometimes
retrospectively.

A fiction engine can produce the recognition that the situation is
closing in. It cannot produce the sensation of closure in real time. The
latter requires a foregrounded procedure the paradigm treats as
overhead.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## The Design Implication

A designer building toward dread inside a fiction engine is working
against the paradigm’s fundamental logic. Partial compensations are
available. A referee or oracle that consistently narrows rather than
opens outcomes, that applies the continuation logic with a systematic
bias toward worse, can approximate the directional property that dread
requires. Strong fictional description of deteriorating states can make
the closing-in legible as an interpretation. Accumulated loss can
produce the recognition of diminishing options. None of these is dread;
all of them are dread-adjacent, and the gap between adjacent and actual
is structural rather than a matter of execution.

A designer who needs full dread — the procedural immediacy of visible,
felt, accumulating cost — is building a different kind of design. The
choice is not between a good and a bad tool for the same job. It is
between a fiction engine, which produces embedded individual agency in a
situation that keeps transforming, and a design whose primary object is
the visible cost of remaining in the situation at all. Those are
different objects, optimized for different experiences, and the
distinction between them is architectural from the start.

Knowing that the fiction engine cannot produce dread is knowing what the
fiction engine is for. The limit marks the paradigm as precisely as any
of its capabilities.

# The Fixed Answer

*This essay is the second in the limits sub-series of the Fiction Engine
companion series, following “Powerlessness: The Experience the Engine
Cannot Produce.” It is also readable as a standalone piece following the
framework and “Four Designs, One Framework.”*

## Convergence, Closure, and What Mystery Requires

The fiction engine framework’s core commitment is that outcomes function
as continuations. Not because the design insists on optimism — no-but
and no-and are full members of the outcome structure, and they can be
brutal — but because each result must open the next move rather than
arrest the fiction. The shared situation transforms; play proceeds.

Mystery and puzzle do not want the fiction to transform into a new open
state. They want it to converge on a specific answer that exists
independently of the investigation. The killer either did it or didn’t.
The artifact is either in the vault or it isn’t. Getting closer to that
answer is not the same as getting different — and the fiction engine
cannot distinguish between them. That absence is architectural, not
incidental.

## Succession and Convergence

The fiction engine’s development is *successive*: each state gives way
to a different state. The sequence is not oriented toward any particular
destination. The fiction keeps moving, but it is not approaching
anything — there is no correct endpoint, no state that would constitute
resolution in the epistemic sense. What matters is that the fiction
keeps changing and that the changes are genuine continuations.

Mystery’s development is *convergent*: each state moves the fiction
closer to a specific answer that exists independently of the
investigation. The states are not equivalent; they are ranked by
proximity to the truth. A state with more evidence is not just different
from a state with less — it is further along a path that has a
destination.

This is not a preference difference. A fiction engine can contain
investigations, detectives, secret crimes, and hidden truths. The
fiction can transform around them: doors get opened, witnesses get
questioned, evidence gets found. That is fiction engine play running on
mystery content. What the fiction engine cannot produce is the
orientation that mystery play requires: the sense that what is happening
is not just change but approach — that each development measures against
a fixed answer and brings the player closer to or further from it. These
require different structural logics, and the fiction engine only has
one.

## What a Fixed Answer Implies

A fixed answer has three implications that the fiction engine’s logic
cannot absorb.

**Some outcomes are closer to correct than others, not just different.**
A clue that points toward the killer is not merely a new fictional
state. It is evidence that narrows the hypothesis space. The fiction
engine treats outcomes as equivalent in kind: all are continuations, all
open the next move. Mystery requires outcomes that are inequivalent in
direction. Some point toward the answer. Some point away. Some are red
herrings that must be recognized and discarded. The direction is not a
property the fiction engine tracks, because for any other kind of
fiction, it is irrelevant.

**Accumulation has a target.** The causal tissue that accumulated
fiction builds — described in the systems thinking arc as the connective
tissue across which feedback loops run — is directional only in the
sense that it forecloses certain options and opens others. Mystery’s
accumulation is oriented toward a specific conclusion. The clue economy
is not just building a richer fiction; it is assembling a case. The
difference between a richer fiction and a case is that the case has
something it is trying to reach.

**Closure is the goal.** The fiction engine’s yes-and / yes-but / no-but
/ no-and structure exists to prevent closure — to keep the fiction
moving rather than resolved. Mystery’s deduction phase is the moment
closure becomes obligatory. The investigation stops. A conclusion must
be reached. The fiction that was converging must now arrive. A design
that is built around preventing this kind of arrival cannot produce it
when the genre requires it.

## The Epistemic Stakes Problem

The deduction moment has stakes only if “enough information to conclude”
is a real state — one the player can recognize and act from, not merely
feel. If the player can always claim enough information, or can never be
sure they have it, the deduction moment has no purchase. The commitment
to a conclusion carries no weight.

This is what the clue economy in *Hints and Hijinx* solves. Not by
measuring proximity to the truth directly — the player still does not
know what the truth is — but by making the player’s epistemic position
legible as a quantity. More clues means a higher die rating in the
deduction phase. The player knows they have enough when the die rating
is high enough to make the risk acceptable. The clue economy is not
measuring knowledge. It is measuring confidence, and making confidence a
real state that the player can assess and act on.

Without this mechanism, the deduction moment requires the player to
decide they know enough based on felt impression. Two failure modes
follow. The overcautious player keeps investigating rather than
committing to a conclusion under uncertainty: the investigation has no
natural end, so they never end it. The cavalier player concludes too
early and gets lucky or unlucky without the conclusion feeling earned in
either case. Neither failure mode produces the epistemic tension mystery
is built around. Both are structural consequences of the player having
no legible epistemic position.

The fiction engine has no native equivalent of this mechanism. It can
produce a moment where the player commits to a fictional claim and the
oracle or referee adjudicates whether the fiction supports it — whether
the hypothesis is Reasonable, in the RAT checklist’s terms. But
Reasonable is a plausibility check, not an epistemic position check. It
asks whether the conclusion fits the established fiction. It does not
ask whether enough evidence has accumulated to justify the risk of
concluding. A conclusion can be Reasonable from the first scene, long
before any investigation has occurred. Reasonable is a constraint on
internal consistency, not a meter of investigative progress. The two are
different evaluations, and only one of them produces the pressure the
deduction moment requires.

## The Puzzle Extension

Puzzles are mystery’s structural relative without the accumulation
phase. The correct answer exists; the player either reaches it or
doesn’t. There is no investigation arc converging toward it — the
solution is either found through reasoning or it isn’t.

A fiction engine applied to a puzzle produces a fiction that transforms
around the puzzle rather than converging on its solution. The player
attempts approaches; outcomes change the fictional situation. But the
puzzle itself is not a mutable fictional state — it is a fixed problem
with a correct answer — and the fiction engine’s logic of successive
transformation does not produce the cognitive movement toward solution
that puzzle engagement requires.

Puzzle engagement is not primarily about the fiction changing. It is
about the player’s model of the problem changing until the correct
answer becomes apparent. This is internal cognitive movement, not
external fictional movement. The fiction engine’s operation — produce a
new fictional state, continue — is beside the point. The question the
fiction engine is designed to answer (what new fictional state does this
outcome create?) is not the question a puzzle is posing (what is the
answer?). Those are different questions with different satisfying
responses.

## What Remains Available

A fiction engine can produce substantial value in mystery territory
without producing mystery’s core experience.

It can produce the atmosphere and texture of investigation: a world with
things hidden in it, the pleasure of encounter and discovery, the
character relationships that form and test themselves around a shared
investigation. These are genuine goods, and a fiction engine delivers
them well. A session of investigation under fiction engine procedures
can feel like a mystery without being one structurally.

It can produce the experience of a detective character — someone who
investigates, forms hypotheses, takes risks — from inside that
character’s perspective, without the epistemic architecture that makes
those hypotheses genuinely at stake. The detective’s cognitive life is
present. The pressure of the deduction moment is not.

It can produce investigation as a mode of character revelation: who this
person is shows itself in what they notice, who they talk to, what
conclusions they reach. This is character accumulation, not mystery
resolution, and it is something the fiction engine produces well by
design.

What it cannot produce is the moment where the player’s epistemic
position is real — where “do I have enough to conclude?” is a question
with a mechanically legible answer, and where the commitment to a
conclusion carries stakes derived from the accumulated evidence rather
than from the drama of the scene. The scene can be dramatic regardless.
The stakes the clue economy produces are a different kind of stakes, and
they require the clue economy to exist.

## The Hybrid Architecture and Its Seam

The *Hints and Hijinx* architecture is the clearest available example of
how to hold these two structural logics in the same design. Fiction
engine mechanics during investigation; threshold crossing at deduction.
The clue economy is explicitly non-diegetic: clues are tokens, not
fictional states in the framework’s sense. They do not exist inside the
fiction as conditions characters can observe or respond to. They are a
mechanical resource the player accumulates and spends. The deduction
roll converts accumulated tokens into die rating. The architecture
crosses the transparency threshold at one specific structural moment
rather than distributing the crossing across the session.

The cost of this architecture is the one the four-designs essay
identified: the deduction phase activates rarely enough that it never
fully internalizes. The player performs it deliberately each time, aware
of crossing into a different mode. But this cost may be correct. The
deduction phase is supposed to feel distinct. The architectural seam is
load-bearing: it marks the moment the fiction stops accumulating and
starts converging. Making that moment architecturally visible keeps it
functionally honest.

A fiction engine designer approaching mystery territory should
understand this architecture not as a template to copy but as an
argument about structure. The investigation phase and the deduction
phase are different objects. Investigation is successive: each discovery
opens the next move. Deduction is convergent: the accumulated material
is now pressed toward a fixed answer. Designing both phases with the
same tools because they are part of the same genre produces a design
that serves neither fully. The genre does not unify the structural
requirements. It contains two structurally different experiences, and
the seam between them is where the design has to make a choice.

The fiction engine handles the first experience well. The second
requires a mechanism it does not have, and building that mechanism means
crossing the threshold. Knowing this in advance is knowing where the
design problem actually is — not in the rules being written, but in the
structure the rules are being asked to produce.

# The Weight of the System

*This essay is the third in the Fiction Engine limits sub-series. It
follows “Powerlessness” and “The Fixed Answer” and precedes “Above the
Situation.” Readable as a standalone piece following the framework and
the transparency threshold essay.*

## When Procedure Is the Experience

The limits sub-series has been careful to distinguish between two kinds
of mismatch. In the first, a fiction engine that attempts horror content
still produces atmosphere, darkness, terrible events — it fails to
produce dread because its continuation logic works against foreclosure,
but something horror-adjacent can emerge. In the second, a fiction
engine that attempts investigation still produces the texture and
character relationships of inquiry — it fails at epistemic stakes, but
the fiction can read like a mystery in feel if not in structure.

This essay is about a third kind, and the distinction is not subtle. The
previous cases are failures that permit partial compensation. This one
is a success that eliminates the target experience entirely.

When a fiction engine succeeds at its core commitment — transparent
procedure, background infrastructure, the table attending to the fiction
rather than the rules — it has removed the only precondition for
procedural tension. Not weakened it. Removed it. The visibility the
design has achieved is precisely the property that tension requires and
cannot be substituted. A fiction engine that has fully arrived at its
design goal has made procedural tension structurally impossible, and no
adjustment within the paradigm reaches back to restore it.

## What Procedural Tension Requires

Procedural tension requires the player to be simultaneously inside the
fiction and managing a visible system whose states are legible,
meaningful, and costly to engage. Each of those three properties is
individually necessary, and each depends on the others.

*Visibility.* The player must be able to see the system’s state at all
times: how much stress has accumulated, how many clock segments are
filled, what the current resource levels are. States that are invisible
cannot produce tension because tension requires the player to know what
is at risk. A resource the player has stopped noticing is not producing
tension regardless of its value in the system.

*Meaning.* The visible states must differ from each other in ways the
player can assess and that the game confirms are consequential. A stress
track at six of eight segments is not merely different from one at two
of eight. It is worse in a specific, directional sense that the player
can evaluate. The differences must be legible enough to reason about.
Visibility without meaning produces awareness without stake.

*Felt cost.* Engaging with the system must cost something the player can
anticipate and experience as a change. Spending a resource must feel
like a real expenditure, not a bookkeeping action. This requires the
resource to be visible before the spend, during it, and after — the
player experiences the state change as a loss, not as a record update.
The experience of cost is not produced by the math of the transaction.
It is produced by the witnessed change in a visible state.

All three properties depend on the procedure being foregrounded: seen,
tracked, and attended to as an ongoing object of engagement. Background
infrastructure, by the definition the fiction engine paradigm gives it,
cannot be visible in this sense. A fiction engine is a design whose
governing criterion for procedural success is that the system not occupy
the player’s attention. Procedural tension requires exactly that
occupation.

## The Transparency Commitment as Exactly Wrong

The fiction engine framework states one of its criteria as: “procedures
remain transparent in use: the table engages with the situation, not the
system.” This is a success condition. For a design built around
procedural tension, it is the description of an experience destroyed.

Blades in the Dark generates its characteristic tension precisely
because the table engages with the system. Position and effect judgments
are persistent objects of attention during action sequences. The stress
economy is tracked, consulted, and spent in ways the player feels. Clock
management is strategic: the player watches clocks fill and makes
decisions based on where each segment sits. Resistance rolls produce
stress that the player accepts or declines with knowledge of their
current track state. None of this is incidental to the design. It is the
design.

A group running Blades that somehow made all of this background
infrastructure — that stopped noticing the stress track, that processed
position and effect as procedural formality rather than consequential
judgment — would not be running Blades well. They would have removed the
experience the design produces. The fiction engine’s success criteria
are this design’s failure criteria, and the relationship runs in both
directions.

This is not a hierarchy and not a critique of either paradigm. It is a
structural observation: two designs are optimized for experiences that
require opposite relationships between player attention and procedure.
Evaluating either using the other’s criteria produces analysis that
describes neither accurately. The fiction engine’s transparency is an
achievement. Blades’s procedural foreground is an achievement. They are
achievements in different directions, and the distance between them
cannot be closed by adjustment within either paradigm.

## Ritual and Tension Are Not the Same Problem

Foregrounded procedure takes more than one form, and separating them
prevents a false conclusion.

Procedural tension requires continuous management of visible system
state. The player is tracking, spending, and responding to procedural
information throughout the session. The stress track is never fully
background. The clock is always somewhere between empty and full. The
player’s attention to the procedure is not punctuated — it is sustained.

Procedural ritual makes a moment weighty through deliberateness and
formality at a specific structural point. The first pull of the Dread
tower. The Ten Candles player’s description of their character’s death
as the last candle goes out. The formal statement of a hypothesis before
the deduction roll in Hints and Hijinx. Each of these requires the
procedure to step into the foreground and be consciously performed. None
requires the continuous, sustained attentional foreground that tension
demands.

Tension foregrounds the procedure throughout play. Ritual foregrounds it
at selected moments to invest those moments with significance. Both sit
above the transparency threshold. But ritual sits above it briefly, at
specific structural punctuation points, rather than continuously.

This distinction matters for what a fiction engine can and cannot
incorporate. A design below the transparency threshold can include
moments of ritual procedure without abandoning the paradigm: a formal
declaration before a significant action, a physical token placed on the
table to mark a shift in the fiction’s stakes, a specific verbal formula
that changes what follows. The procedure steps forward, does its work,
and recedes. The experience of the foregrounded moment is real —
deliberately so — without requiring the continuous attentional presence
that tension demands.

The deduction phase in Hints and Hijinx is the sharpest available
example. It runs above the threshold by design, but briefly and at a
structurally marked moment. The four-designs essay identified this as
the cost that may be correct: the seam is load-bearing, and the
deliberateness of performing it is part of what the phase produces. A
fiction engine designer who wants weighted moments has this option. The
moments cannot be distributed across the session as continuous
procedural management without the design ceasing to be a fiction engine
in any functionally meaningful sense.

## Resource Economies and the Felt-Cost Problem

The felt cost of spending a resource requires that resource to be
visible before the spend, during it, and after. This is not a
psychological preference. It is a structural condition: the player must
witness the state change to experience it as a cost rather than record
it as a transaction.

The fiction engine’s preference for fictional descriptors over numerical
interfaces — “she is exhausted and can barely lift her sword” rather
than a stamina score — tends to push resource states toward description
rather than quantity. The preference is grounded in what the framework
identifies as a cognitive efficiency principle: the descriptor carries
more immediately usable fictional information than the number. This
argument is sound within the paradigm’s purpose.

Applied to resource economies intended to produce felt cost, the
argument runs backwards. A qualitative change is harder to experience as
a cost than a quantitative one. “She is now deeply exhausted” conveys a
fictional state. Watching a number drop from 4 to 2 produces the
visceral witness of depletion. The change from the descriptor to the
worse descriptor is real, but the player must interpret it as a loss
rather than observe the loss directly. Interpretation requires cognitive
distance from the thing being interpreted. Felt cost requires no such
distance.

The Breathless die degradation works as a partial exception precisely
because the die is simultaneously a fictional state and a visible
quantity. The size of the die is perceptible at all times. Its
degradation — from a d12 to a d10 to a d8 — is observable as a change in
a physical object the player holds. The player does not interpret the
degradation; they see it. The die-size economy sits close enough to the
fiction engine paradigm’s fictional-state preference while producing
something like the directional visibility that felt cost requires.

The stress track in the same design shows the seam. It is a count
without mandatory diegetic expression: a number of filled boxes, visible
as a quantity, not required to read as a fictional condition the
character inhabits. Its states are visible numbers rather than felt
fictional conditions. This is the territory where procedural tension
lives. And it is exactly the territory where the friction between the
fiction engine’s paradigm and the experience of procedural tension
becomes most visible: the stress track has the quantitative visibility
that felt cost needs, and it has migrated outside the diegetic layer to
get it.

## What Remains

The fiction engine cannot produce procedural tension as Blades in the
Dark produces it, or as any design produces it where the system’s state
is the ongoing object of player attention. The prevention is structural
and total: the design that has succeeded at transparency has made the
required visibility impossible, not merely difficult.

Two things remain available inside the paradigm.

The first is fictional-weight tension: the cognitive experience of a
situation that is genuinely, demonstrably costly to be in, derived from
accumulated fictional state. A character whose established relationships
mean that failure here damages something irreplaceable, whose prior
decisions have made the current situation specifically worse than it
would have been otherwise, whose history has made certain outcomes
legible as losses before they occur. This is tension produced by
understanding — by reasoning from accumulated fiction to a recognition
of what is at risk. It is real. It does not feel the same as procedural
tension. The understanding arrives before the loss; the procedural watch
arrives at the moment of it.

The second is uncertainty tension: the genuine not-knowing whether the
outcome will favor the character, available to any design that uses
random resolution. This is baseline available to a fiction engine, and
accumulated fictional state can give it weight — the stakes of this roll
are clearer when the fiction has established what depends on them.
Uncertainty tension and procedural tension are not the same thing.
Procedural tension adds to uncertainty the felt management of visible
state: the player watching the resource they will need to spend in order
to take this risk. The fiction engine produces the uncertainty without
the management.

A designer who wants fictional-weight tension and uncertainty tension
can build a fiction engine and design for both. A designer who wants the
continuous felt management of visible procedural state is building
something else: a design for which the pressure engine paradigm, or
something adjacent to it, is the relevant model. The choice between them
is not a choice between more rigorous and less rigorous design. It is a
choice about what the player’s attention is doing during play, and what
kind of experience that attention produces when the resolution arrives.

Knowing which one the design is producing — and which one the players
are expecting — is, as before, the beginning of designing toward it
deliberately.

# Above the Situation

*This essay is the fourth in a series examining what the fiction engine
paradigm cannot produce and why. It builds on the limits framing essay
“The Bet” and on the companion essays “Four Designs, One Framework” and
“Faction and Fiction.” It is readable as a standalone following the
framework.*

## What Strategic Depth Requires and Why the Engine Refuses It

The first three essays in this sub-series have described three
mechanisms of mismatch. The fiction engine *inverts* dread’s structural
requirements: where dread needs foreclosure, the engine produces
continuation. It is *structurally incompatible* with mystery: where
investigation needs convergence toward a fixed answer, the engine
produces open succession. It *actively prevents* procedural tension by
succeeding at its own core commitment: where tension needs a visible,
costly system, the engine makes the system disappear.

Each of those mismatches involves the fiction engine’s outputs. What it
produces — outcomes as continuations, procedures that recede, fiction
that stays moving — is wrong for those experiences in different ways.
The mismatch with strategic optimization is different in kind. It does
not concern what the fiction engine produces. It concerns where the
player is positioned relative to the fiction.

A fiction engine embeds the player in the current situation. Strategic
depth requires the player above it. The incompatibility is positional,
and positional incompatibility cannot be addressed by revising
procedures.

## The Positional Distinction

Two positions are available relative to any fictional situation.

Inside the situation: the player’s primary object of attention is the
current fictional state — the characters present, the conditions in
play, the pressures of this moment. Decisions emerge from engagement
with that state. The player asks what the character would do here, now,
given what is. Planning happens from within: what would make sense to
attempt, given everything established so far.

Above the situation: the player’s primary object of attention is the
game state as a decision tree. The current fictional moment is an input
to a calculation rather than a field of engagement. The player models
the available branches, evaluates their payoffs, tracks constraints that
extend past the current moment. Planning happens from outside: what is
the optimal move given the full state and the objective function.

These are not cognitive styles applied to the same activity. They are
different activities. The player inside the situation responds; the
player above it calculates. The distinction between responding and
calculating is not a preference difference. It is a difference in what
the player is doing and what kind of object they are doing it with.

The fiction engine assumes the first position. Its design logic follows
from that assumption: thin procedure, outcomes as continuations,
fictional states as the primary content of play. Everything the
framework identifies as load-bearing fiction engine design — the
immediate legibility of fictional states, the minimal classification
step between intent and outcome, the resolution cycle that keeps the
fiction moving — is optimized for embedded, responsive engagement.
Strategic optimization requires the second position, and the fiction
engine’s design logic is constructed around the first.

## What Optimization Requires

Strategic optimization across multiple turns requires the player to hold
the game state as a stable, legible object they can reason about. Three
properties follow from this, and the fiction engine’s logic works
against each.

*State legibility.* The player must be able to read the current state
precisely enough to model its future branches. Fictional descriptors
convey texture; they resist calculation. “The alliance is strained” and
“the city is watchful” communicate conditions the player can respond to
from inside the fiction. They do not communicate quantities a player can
calculate with. How strained is the alliance? At what point does
watchfulness become a concrete obstacle rather than ambient pressure?
Strategic optimization requires answers to these questions as quantities
or at least as ordinal positions. Impressions are insufficient because
they cannot be added, compared, or projected forward.

*State stability.* The player must be able to predict how the state will
develop without encountering the fiction engine’s characteristic
commitment to transformation through surprise. Emergence — the fiction
going somewhere nobody planned — is the fiction engine’s goal.
Predictability is the optimizer’s requirement. A game state that keeps
transforming in ways the player could not anticipate is not a useful
object for multi-turn planning. It is a useful object for embedded,
responsive play. These are opposite design commitments.

*Multi-turn visibility.* Strategic optimization requires that the
player’s current decision be evaluable in terms of its effects several
turns forward. The fiction engine’s continuation logic — each outcome
opens into the next, the situation transforms — means the state beyond
the next turn is genuinely uncertain in ways that resist modeling. That
uncertainty is the fiction engine’s feature. It is the optimizer’s
obstacle. A player planning six turns ahead inside a fiction engine is
not planning; they are guessing, because the fiction will have
transformed several times before the plan could execute.

None of these is a superficial property of a design that could be
adjusted without changing the paradigm. All three follow from the same
commitment: the fiction is primary, procedure is infrastructure, and the
player’s engagement is with the fiction as it is right now. That
commitment is what makes optimization unavailable.

## The Legibility Inversion

The fiction engine’s preference for fictional descriptors over numerical
interfaces is explicit in the framework and treated as a strength. A
fictional descriptor — “she is exhausted and can barely lift her sword”
— is better than a stamina score of 3 for a player inside the current
situation. It tells them what the character can plausibly attempt right
now. It maintains the fiction’s texture. It survives the removal of the
game record because the condition exists in the world the characters
inhabit.

For strategic optimization, the same preference is exactly wrong. “She
is exhausted and can barely lift her sword” cannot be added, subtracted,
compared across turns, or used to calculate whether resting now or
pushing through produces a better state at turn 5. A stamina score of 3
can do all of those things. The numerical interface is inferior for
embedded engagement because it requires translation — the player must
convert the number into a fictional condition before they know what it
means in play. The numerical interface is superior for strategic
calculation because that translation is the overhead being paid for
legibility as a calculable quantity.

The friction the fiction engine is designed to eliminate — the
translation step between system state and fictional meaning — is the
friction strategic optimization depends on. The translation step is
where the calculation lives. Remove it and you have fiction that is
immediately legible to a player inside the situation; the player inside
the situation is exactly who you have it for. But you have also removed
the object the strategic optimizer was working with.

This is not a coincidence or an oversight. The preference for fictional
descriptors is a choice that optimizes for one kind of play by removing
the preconditions for another. A designer who wants strategic depth and
reaches for fictional descriptors has removed the tool that strategic
depth needs.

## The OSG Architecture Revisited

The OSG companion essay identified OSG as sitting above the transparency
threshold deliberately, with a procedural architecture designed to
produce the information asymmetry and strategic depth the form requires.
The action format — Action, Outcome, Leverage — is worth revisiting here
as the clearest available case of a fiction-primary design that is also
above the threshold specifically because it needs to serve strategic
play.

The action format is a translation mechanism. The player plans above the
fiction: they assess the strategic situation, identify the objective,
evaluate the leverage available. They then express that plan in
fictional terms as a submitted action: what the character attempts, what
they expect to result, what established fictional fact supports the
attempt. The Leverage requirement grounds the strategic move in the
accumulated fiction — the plan must be traceable to prior events, to
relationships established in play, to conditions the fiction has made
real. Strategic thinking above the fiction; fictional expression within
it. The RAT checklist evaluates the submission on the fiction engine’s
terms: Reasonable given what has been established, Actionable as a
coherent attempt, Traceable to prior fictional facts.

This architecture does something the fiction engine paradigm cannot
natively do: it maintains two positions simultaneously. The planning
phase requires the player above the situation. The submission and
adjudication phases require the player inside it. The design holds both
by separating them into distinct structural moments — not by asking the
player to occupy both simultaneously, but by sequencing them so each
phase has its own positional demand.

A pure fiction engine has no concept of a planning phase above the
fiction. Every engagement the framework describes is embedded: the
player states what their character attempts from inside the fictional
situation. There is no prior step where the player models the game state
from outside it, evaluates branches, considers optimal moves. The
fiction engine assumes that step does not exist and builds accordingly.
The OSG architecture assumes it does exist and builds a structure to
contain it.

The cost the OSG essay identified — the threshold crossing imposes
procedural demands that persist above the fiction throughout play, not
only at discrete resolution moments — is also the cost the fiction
engine cannot absorb. The planning phase is the overhead. It is exactly
the kind of step the fiction engine is designed to eliminate, and it
cannot be eliminated here because it is where the strategic engagement
lives.

## Strategy-as-Fiction and Strategy-as-Optimization

A fiction engine can produce the experience of a character who is a
strategist: someone who plans, calculates, schemes, outmaneuvers. This
is strategy-as-fiction. The player occupies the perspective of someone
who thinks strategically, expressed through fictional choices and
fictional outcomes. The character is clever; the player experiences what
that cleverness feels like from inside.

This is not strategy-as-optimization: the player actually performing
strategic calculation over a game state and making decisions based on
that calculation. In strategy-as-fiction, the player is in the fiction,
experiencing the character’s perspective. In strategy-as-optimization,
the player is above the game state, manipulating it toward an objective.

Both can describe themselves as planning. The character planning an
infiltration and the player optimizing a position in a 4X game are both
“planning.” The object of the plan differs — the fiction versus the game
state — and that object is what the fiction engine cannot bridge. A
player whose character is a master tactician, playing that tactician
from inside the fiction, is doing something different from a player who
is a master tactician themselves, performing that mastery over the
game’s state. The fiction engine produces the former. The latter
requires a different relationship to the game’s state, a different
positional orientation, and a different kind of rule that makes that
orientation possible.

The distinction matters because it prevents a common misdiagnosis. A
fiction engine that allows players to describe tactical decisions is not
providing strategic depth. It is providing the fiction of tactical
thinking. A player can describe their character devising a careful plan,
and the fiction can reflect that plan’s cleverness, without the player
ever performing the calculation themselves. The calculation is narrated
rather than executed. Strategy-as-fiction is available to any fiction
engine that allows clever characters. Strategy-as-optimization requires
a game state the player can actually calculate with — which means a game
state legible as a decision object, not as a shared situation.

## The Wargame Limit

At the far end of this territory are wargames and 4X designs where the
fiction has thinned to decoration or disappeared entirely. The
optimization logic has gone to completion: strategic reasoning has
shaped the entire design toward a legible, calculable state, and the
fictional layer — where it remains — is aesthetic rather than
functional. Unit names, faction histories, thematic art: these produce
texture without serving the state as a fiction engine would define it.
The fiction does not transform in response to the players’ choices; it
is backdrop for the moves they are making in a state space.

The colonization runs in both directions. The fiction engine’s logic
shapes whatever it touches toward embedded, responsive, fictional
engagement — toward a player inside the situation, responding to what is
present. Strategic optimization’s logic shapes whatever it touches
toward legible, calculable, plannable state — toward a player above the
situation, modeling what is possible. When these two logics meet in a
single design, one tends to dominate. Which one depends on which
commitment is more fundamental to what the design is for.

OSG holds both by building an explicit architectural seam: a planning
phase above the fiction, a submission phase inside it, an adjudication
phase that evaluates the submission on fictional terms. The seam is
visible and deliberate. It does not eliminate the tension between the
two positional demands; it manages that tension by giving each demand
its own structural moment.

A designer who wants strategic depth inside a fictional frame is working
against both logics simultaneously, and will need to make the same kind
of explicit decision: which phase of play is above the situation, which
is inside it, and how does the design mark the boundary. Neither the
pure fiction engine nor the pure strategy game provides the hybrid. The
hybrid requires designing the seam with the same care brought to
everything else — because the seam is where the incompatibility lives,
and a seam ignored is a seam that breaks.

# Building Into Nothing

## Character Construction and the Object Thin Procedure Dissolves

The fiction engine is not indifferent to character. The opposite is
true: the paradigm is optimized for character in a specific and precise
sense. It produces a character who is present inside the fiction — who
has a history, a reputation, a set of capabilities and limits that shape
what the fiction will accept from them — and who changes through
consequence rather than through deliberate construction. The character
accumulates. They do not build.

That distinction is the source of the mismatch this essay examines.
Character construction as a primary engagement requires something the
fiction engine cannot provide, not because the paradigm refuses to
engage with character but because it dissolves the structural object
that construction requires. The experience is not degraded. The object
is absent.

## Two Kinds of Character

The argument depends on a distinction the framing essay introduces as a
mechanism of mismatch: dissolution. To see what gets dissolved and why,
it is necessary first to name the two objects.

*Character-as-fiction* is a set of fictional facts that establish who
this person is inside the shared situation. What they are capable of and
what they cannot do. What relationships they hold and what standing they
have. What their history has made of them and what the fiction’s other
inhabitants expect from them. These facts shape what the character can
plausibly attempt and what outcomes will be accepted as consistent. They
accumulate over play through consequence: a reputation established by
action, a relationship formed under specific conditions, a physical or
psychological state that developed from prior events. The character
becomes a richer fictional object as play continues, but no one designed
it that way. The fiction produced it.

*Character-as-system* is a set of mechanical properties that interact
with each other and with the resolution procedure in ways that reward
understanding, planning, and optimization. The properties have numerical
or categorical values. Their interactions produce effects the player can
discover through play and exploit through deliberate choice. Building
the character means making decisions about these properties that produce
a mechanically distinct profile: not just a differently described
character but a genuinely different mechanical object, one that plays
differently because of how its properties interact. The player is not
finding out who their character becomes. They are designing what their
character will be.

These are different objects. A fiction engine produces the first.
Character construction as a primary engagement requires the second. The
distinction is not between players who prefer narration and players who
prefer mechanics, or between roleplaying and system mastery as
orientations to the game. It is between two structural objects, one of
which a fiction engine cannot supply.

## Why Opacity Is Load-Bearing

A system worth building into must have depth that is not immediately
visible from inspection. Properties whose interactions are not obvious
on first reading. An optimization space that opens as the player engages
more closely with the design. Effects that reward sustained attention
rather than full legibility from the outside.

Opacity is load-bearing because without it there is nothing to discover.
A character system whose properties and their interactions are
immediately apparent from reading the sheet offers the player no
direction to build toward. The exploration that
construction-as-engagement requires cannot occur if the system is
already fully legible. Construction engagement requires something to be
worked out, discovered through use, assembled from choices whose
combined effects only become clear once made.

The fiction engine’s transparency commitment produces exactly this
legibility, and produces it deliberately. A fiction engine character —
fictional descriptors, minimal tracking, conditions expressed as
diegetic states rather than numerical flags — is designed to be clear on
inspection. “She is a skilled fencer with a reputation for reckless
courage and a crippling debt to the wrong people” is a complete
fictional picture. A reader encounters it and knows who this person is.
There is nothing hidden in it. No mechanical interactions the player can
optimize toward. No build direction to pursue over the next six
sessions.

This is the design working correctly. The fiction engine solves opacity
as a problem: it wants the character to be immediately legible so the
player can attend to the fiction rather than to the character system.
Construction engagement requires the problem to remain unsolved. The
dissolution mechanism runs in both directions — the fiction engine
cannot provide the opaque system, and a design that provides the opaque
system has given up the transparency commitment that allows the fiction
engine’s resolution procedure to sit below the transparency threshold.

## The Advancement Problem

Fiction engine advancement — where it exists — tends to be minimal and
fictional: new capabilities described in fictional terms, new
relationships established through play, new reputation accrued through
the consequences of past decisions. A character who began as a cautious
negotiator and survived three violent confrontations has changed, and
the change can be registered as a new fictional fact. But it is not a
mechanically different profile. The player did not design toward it. It
is what the fiction produced.

Construction engagement requires advancement that changes the mechanical
profile in ways the player can plan before they happen. Session 20
should offer the player a mechanical identity that was unavailable at
session 1, traceable to specific build decisions made over the
intervening sessions. The player is not discovering what their character
became; they are building what their character will be. The intention
precedes the outcome. That precession is what makes it construction
rather than accumulation.

The fiction engine produces accumulation without precession. A character
who survives twenty sessions inside a fiction engine has a history whose
weight is genuinely irreplaceable. Relationships tested by specific
events. Decisions whose consequences are woven into the causal tissue of
the fiction. A reputation that other characters in the fiction respond
to because of specific things that actually happened. None of this was
designed; all of it was produced by play. That production is not a
failure to provide the built mechanical profile. It is a different
achievement, valuable on its own terms. But it is not what the player
who wants to engage in character construction is seeking, and knowing
this is not a preference question.

## Why This Is Structural Rather Than Preferential

The common framing treats this as a compatibility issue: some players
prefer deep character systems, and the fiction engine is not designed
for those players. The framing is not wrong, but it misses the sharper
point.

The fiction engine does not discourage character construction or design
against it as a preference. It dissolves the precondition for it. A
player who wants to engage in character construction inside a fiction
engine cannot do so not because the paradigm is hostile to that
engagement but because the object the engagement requires — a mechanical
system rich enough to build into, opaque enough to reward sustained
exploration, deep enough that session 20 reveals properties session 1
did not — does not exist inside the paradigm. You cannot build into a
system that has nothing to build into.

The mechanism is dissolution rather than prohibition. A design that
prohibits character construction leaves the player frustrated because
what they want is present but inaccessible. A design that dissolves the
structural precondition leaves the player without a target: there is no
opaque system to engage with, no optimization space to explore, no
mechanical depth to discover. The absence is not a locked door. It is an
empty room.

This means the problem cannot be solved by encouraging players to adopt
a different orientation. A fiction engine player who is told to
“appreciate the accumulated fictional weight rather than seeking
mechanical depth” is being asked to want a different thing. That is not
a design solution. It is an admission that the design does not provide
what the player is looking for, stated as advice.

## The Hybrid Position

Some designs have tried to combine a fiction engine resolution procedure
with a character system opaque enough to support construction
engagement. The architecture of these designs is instructive.

The resolution procedure sits below the transparency threshold: thin,
fast to internalize, fictional in its outputs. The character system sits
above it: persistent between sessions, legible as a mechanical object
rather than a purely fictional one, the source of ongoing attentional
demand during session preparation and during play whenever mechanical
build decisions become relevant. The character sheet is a persistent
object of engagement even when the resolution dice are not in play.

This produces a design occupying two threshold positions at once: below
the threshold during resolution, above it during character engagement.
The seam between them — the moment when the player’s attention must
shift from the fiction to the character’s mechanical properties — is the
same architectural feature identified in the Hints and Hijinx deduction
phase and in OSG’s strategic planning layer. Each of these is a
deliberate threshold crossing, contained to a specific moment in the
design rather than distributed across play.

The cost is specific. A player attending to their mechanical build is
not attending to the current fictional situation. These orientations are
not simultaneously available: the attention that reads the fiction as a
set of possible character actions and the attention that reads the
character sheet as a set of mechanical interactions to optimize are
oriented toward different objects. A design that requires both is asking
the player to hold two positions at once, and the orientational
divergence this produces is precisely what the fiction engine paradigm
is built to prevent. The hybrid design puts the failure mode back in, as
a deliberate trade for the construction engagement it wants to provide.

This is not an argument against hybrid designs. It is an argument for
knowing what the trade is. A design with a fiction engine resolution
procedure and a rich character construction system is not a fiction
engine with bonus features. It has a seam — between its two positional
demands — that requires explicit architectural management. The seam does
not disappear through calibration or good facilitation. It remains a
feature the design has to own.

## What the Fiction Engine Does Produce Over Time

The comparison should be fair.

A character who has played for twenty sessions inside a fiction engine
carries something that a mechanically built character typically does
not. The causal tissue connecting prior decisions to current fictional
facts. Relationships that formed under specific conditions, tested in
specific ways, carrying the weight of what they have been through. A
reputation that precedes the character into situations, built from
actions whose specific content the other characters in the fiction know
and respond to. None of this can be read from a sheet at session 20. It
is distributed across the fiction itself, held in common by the
participants who were there.

This is not a lesser version of the built mechanical profile. It is a
different object. A built mechanical profile is legible to anyone who
can read the design’s notation — a new player reading the character
sheet at session 20 can understand who this character is mechanically.
The twenty-session fiction engine character cannot be introduced in a
character sheet at all. The character is, in large part, the history the
participants share, and someone who was not present for it does not have
access to it.

The choice between these objects is a choice about what kind of thing
the character is. A designed mechanical object that develops through
deliberate construction, whose session 20 state is the result of choices
the player made toward it. Or an accumulated fictional entity whose
session 20 state is the result of consequences the fiction produced from
it. Both are achievable design goals. Neither produces the other as a
byproduct. Knowing which one a design is building toward, and which one
its players are expecting, is the beginning of designing toward it
deliberately — and of being honest about what the other costs to
produce.

# Appendix

# Fiction Engine: A Design Framework for Tabletop RPGs

*Version 1.0 — May 2026*

A fiction engine is a game design that treats play as a system for
producing, sustaining, and transforming fiction with minimal friction.
Its function is to keep the fiction moving while preserving uncertainty,
agency, and consequence. The procedural layer between fictional intent
and fictional change should be as thin as the situation allows. The
rules are infrastructure, not experience.

## What is fiction

In this framework, fiction means the diegetic layer: the characters,
places, events, and conditions that participants hold as real within the
game. Some of this layer is shared by all participants at any given
moment; some is held only by a subset — the referee’s hidden map, an
NPC’s undisclosed motive, a player’s private knowledge. Both are part of
the fiction. The distinction is not between fiction and non-fiction but
between what is currently known and what is not yet known within the
diegetic layer. Fiction is not narrative craft, literary quality, or
storytelling in the literary sense. It is the living, mutable situation
that play produces and transforms moment to moment.

When this framework says a rule should serve the fiction, it means the
rule should produce changes to the fiction rather than changes to a
score, a resource pool, or an administrative record that exists outside
it.

## What counts as fictional state

A fictional state is any condition that exists inside the fiction and is
visible to participants as something they can see, respond to, and
reason about within it. It is a property of the world, not a property of
the game record.

A character’s exhaustion is a fictional state when it is something other
characters can observe, respond to, and factor into their decisions
inside the fiction: she is pale, her movements are slow, she cannot lift
the bar. It stops being a fictional state when it is only a number that
modifies future rolls without producing any change to how the situation
reads inside the world.

This distinction resolves several apparent edge cases:

**Positioning.** Fictional state when it describes where a character
actually is and what that means for what they can do or see. Not
fictional state when it is a grid coordinate with no imagined spatial
equivalent.

**Inventory depletion.** Fictional state when it changes what the
character can plausibly attempt inside the situation: she has no rope,
so the climb is now different. Not fictional state when it is a count
that has no consequence until a threshold is crossed arbitrarily.

**Emotional state.** Fictional state when it is a condition other
characters can perceive and that shapes what the situation will accept
as plausible action. Not fictional state when it is a tag attached to a
character sheet with no behavioral consequence inside the fiction.

**Tactical or mechanical bonuses.** Not fictional state. A +1 ongoing is
a modification to a future roll. It becomes fictional state only if it
reflects something that exists inside the fiction — she has the high
ground, she has momentum, the crowd is watching — and would be visible
to anyone present in the scene.

The test in each case is the same: if you removed the game record and
asked whether the condition still exists inside the fiction as something
participants can see, respond to, and reason about, the answer tells you
what kind of state it is. Fictional states survive that removal.
Administrative records do not.

## Definition

The label “narrative game” is too broad to be analytically useful. Games
grouped under that heading differ significantly in where they place
authority, how they resolve uncertainty, and how much interpretive labor
they require. A fiction engine is the design family defined by a single
structural commitment: every rule exists to serve the fiction, and any
rule that fails that test is a candidate for removal.

This commitment is structural, not stylistic. It applies to every design
decision: resolution procedures, character traits, pacing tools,
uncertainty mechanisms. Each one either produces fictional change,
supports the conditions under which fictional change can occur, or
generates dead overhead that serves neither purpose. The third category
is what a fiction engine works to eliminate.

Importantly, this distinction operates on function rather than form. A
rule that is non-diegetic in form — a stress track, a pacing clock, a
campaign log — can still be fiction-supporting in function if it
reliably preserves the coherence, legibility, or continuity that makes
fictional change possible. The question is never whether a rule looks
mechanical. It is whether the rule is carrying its weight in fictional
terms.

## Position and scope

This framework describes one design paradigm among several. It is not a
claim that all good tabletop RPG design should follow these principles,
nor that other paradigms are deficient versions of this one. Other
coherent approaches exist — games built around codified move triggers,
managed pressure economies, explicit metacurrency, character-world
interfaces, or world-as-procedure — and each makes tradeoffs that
reflect a genuine design philosophy. Evaluating any of them using
criteria borrowed from a different paradigm produces bad analysis.

Fiction-first design is not identical to the fiction engine paradigm.
Many games prioritize fiction while still relying on highly formalized
procedural grammars, codified move structures, explicit pacing
economies, or persistent mechanical interfaces that remain foregrounded
during play. A fiction engine describes the narrower subset of designs
that attempt to minimize mediation itself, keeping the procedural layer
as transparent as possible while still preserving uncertainty and
consequence. That narrowness is a deliberate tradeoff, not a judgment on
fiction-oriented systems that sit outside it.

A fiction engine has no winning condition. The fiction moves because the
situation keeps changing (outcomes produce new fictional states that
open into further play), not because participants are working toward a
predetermined resolution. A fiction engine produces a situation that
responds to what participants do, without building toward a
predetermined endpoint. This is different from a design that uses
fiction as the medium for a competitive or goal-directed experience,
where fictional actions accumulate toward a resolution condition that
exists outside the fiction itself. Designs of that kind are not worse
designs; they are built around a different structural logic. But that
logic is not the fiction engine’s logic, and evaluating either by the
other’s criteria produces bad analysis. The distinction is not
incidental. It is load-bearing.

What this framework offers is a precise description of a specific
approach: its structural commitments, its operational logic, and its
tradeoffs. The fiction engine paradigm values thin mediation, fictional
momentum, and minimal procedural overhead. It accepts, as a consequence
of those values, that it will provide less structural scaffolding than
paradigms built around dramatic prescription or managed pressure. That
is not a flaw. It is the shape of the tradeoff.

A reader coming from a different design tradition should treat this
document as a map of one region, not a map of the whole territory.

## How to use this framework

This framework serves three distinct purposes, and a reader can apply it
in any of them.

**As a design checklist.** When building a game, use the criteria and
failure modes sections later in this document to evaluate individual
rules and the overall system. The central test — does this rule produce
a new fictional state or overhead? — applies to every design decision.

**As an evaluative lens.** When analyzing an existing game, use the
definition and criteria sections to determine how fully it belongs to
this paradigm. A game that does not meet these criteria is not
necessarily a worse game, only a game built around a different engine of
play.

**As a shared vocabulary.** When discussing design decisions with other
designers or players, this framework offers precise terms for concepts
that are often discussed imprecisely: fictional intent, the fiction, the
diegetic layer, overhead, suspended outcomes, authority drift. All of
these are defined in the glossary. Using them consistently makes design
conversations more productive by reducing the ambiguity that labels like
“narrative” or “story-driven” carry.

## Core operation

A fiction engine turns fictional intent into fictional change.

The player states what their character attempts within the fiction. The
game produces an outcome. That outcome modifies the fiction. Play
continues. This cycle should run with as little procedural overhead as
the situation allows.

A fiction engine does not require translation into detailed tactical
subsystems, elaborate metacurrency loops, or dense narrative grammar. It
does not ask players to negotiate with the rules themselves in order to
advance a scene. This is not a position against complexity. It is a
position against complexity that produces neither fictional output nor
the conditions for it. A rule can be intricate and still belong to a
fiction engine, provided it either changes the shared imagined space
directly or preserves the coherence, pacing, or legibility that makes
fictional change possible.

The goal is not to simulate everything, nor to formalize everything. The
goal is to maintain momentum.

## Range of application

This framework applies across formats and scales. A fiction engine is as
valid for a solo player as for a group of five. It applies to a one-shot
as readily as to a multi-session campaign. It is genre-neutral: horror,
comedy, space opera, and intimate drama can all be built as fiction
engines without modification to the core commitment.

What changes across formats is how authority is distributed and what
mechanisms sustain uncertainty. Solo play, small groups, and large
tables each solve these problems differently. The framework does not
prescribe those solutions. It asks only that whatever mechanism is used
produces fictional output and preserves the conditions for meaningful
play.

## How outcomes function

A fiction engine treats outcomes as continuations, not endpoints.

The productive question after any resolution is not simply whether an
action succeeded. It is: what new fictional state does this result
create, and how does play proceed from there? The range of possible
outcomes — yes and, yes but, no but, no and — each forces the fiction to
keep changing. Each opens the next move rather than closing the last
one.

A result that leaves the fiction suspended rather than transformed
indicates a design problem, not a neutral state.

## Authority and procedure

Fiction engines distribute narrative authority in different ways. Some
use a referee. Some use shared authorship. Some use structured
uncertainty mechanisms such as oracles in solo or low-referee play. All
of these are workable configurations. What the rules must do in each
case is make the distribution of authority visible and operable, rather
than leaving it to convention or improvisation.

The principle that applies across all configurations is the same:
whatever mechanism handles authority must produce fictional output
rather than administrative prompts. When the procedure begins to
dominate the fiction — when the table is spending more time tracking
states, resolving subsystems, or consulting tables than engaging with
the situation — the engine is no longer functioning as intended.

## Failure modes

A fiction engine can fail in characteristic ways that are worth naming
directly.

**Overhead accumulation.** Rules that generate tracking obligations,
resource management, or procedural steps without producing fictional
output are overhead. Individually each may seem minor. Cumulatively they
shift the table’s attention from the situation to the system. The engine
stalls not through a single bad rule but through the weight of many
marginal ones.

**Suspended outcomes.** A resolution that produces a result requiring
further resolution before play can continue breaks the engine’s
fundamental cycle. Designs that chain resolution steps — roll to
attempt, then roll to determine degree, then consult a table to
translate degree into fictional terms — are prone to this. Each
additional step is a potential suspension point.

**Authority drift.** Any mechanism for distributing narrative authority
— a referee, a shared authorship structure, an oracle, a randomized
prompt system — can drift from producing fictional output toward
producing administrative prompts that require significant interpretive
work before the fiction can advance. In solo and low-referee play this
most commonly takes the form of oracle outputs that read as tasks rather
than events. In group play it manifests as referee rulings that generate
procedural negotiation rather than fictional consequence. The failure
mode is the same across configurations: the authority mechanism has
become an obstacle between fictional intent and fictional change rather
than a conduit for it.

**Orientational divergence.** When participants at the same table hold
different implicit models of what the game is doing — one treating it as
a tactical puzzle, another as collaborative story, another as character
study — the engine loses coherence even if all the rules are functioning
correctly. This failure mode is addressed in the social dimension
section below.

## Examples

Three cases illustrate where the framework’s distinctions do their most
useful work.

**A rule that clearly belongs in a fiction engine.** When you force a
locked door, roll. On a strong success, it opens cleanly. On a partial
success, it opens but alerts someone nearby. On a failure, the lock
breaks and something approaches. Every outcome is a new fictional state.
The player knows immediately what the situation has become and how play
continues.

**A rule that generates dead overhead.** Track door integrity across
five damage boxes. Once all boxes are filled, make a secondary roll to
determine whether the door opens or jams. The procedure produces a
number, then another roll, before the fiction can change. Neither step
is a fictional event. Both are administrative steps between fictional
intent and fictional consequence.

**A borderline case.** A stress track that has no diegetic equivalent —
it does not represent anything the character experiences inside the
fiction — but reliably produces visible changes in character behavior as
it fills. The track is non-diegetic in form but fiction-supporting in
function. It belongs to the second category: infrastructure that
preserves the conditions for meaningful play rather than changing the
fiction directly. Whether it belongs in a fiction engine depends on
whether its administrative cost is proportionate to the fictional
coherence it maintains. That is a design judgment, not a rule violation.

The borderline case is where the framework does its most useful work,
because it forces the question: is this rule carrying its weight, and in
what currency?

## For designers

Every design decision can be held against a single test: does this rule
produce a new fictional state, support the conditions for fictional
play, or generate dead overhead?

Guidelines that follow from that test:

- Resolution should be short enough to remember without reference.
- Outcomes should advance the fiction rather than suspend it pending
  further resolution.
- Fictional descriptors should do the work that numerical interfaces
  often claim to do: “she is exhausted and can barely lift her sword”
  carries more usable information than a stamina score of 3.
- Uncertainty should create stakes, not punishment.
- Cognitive load should be minimized everywhere it does not directly
  serve meaningful choice.
- The situation should remain more visible to the table than the system.

A useful diagnostic: remove a rule and observe whether the fiction still
moves and remains coherent. If it does, the rule was dead overhead. If
play stalls or loses legibility, the rule was load-bearing: either
producing fictional change or supporting the conditions for it. Keep the
load-bearing rules. Cut the rest.

## For players

The player’s task inside a fiction engine is to make informed choices
about what their character attempts and to treat every outcome —
including failure — as something that changes the situation rather than
something that ends the scene.

Within this paradigm, the relevant question is usually what the
character attempts from inside the situation, not how to optimise an
external procedure. Consequences are part of the structure of play, not
interruptions to it. When the fiction becomes unstable or surprising,
that is the framework operating correctly.

A useful diagnostic: if you find yourself thinking about what the rules
allow rather than what the character would do, the fiction has slipped.
Pause, return to the situation, and restate it before continuing.

## The social dimension

A fiction engine functions only when all participants are oriented
toward the fiction as the primary object of play. This is not a design
problem that rules alone can solve. It is a table culture condition that
design can support but not guarantee.

When participants hold divergent models of what the game is doing — one
treating it as a tactical puzzle, another as character exploration,
another as improvised theater — the engine loses coherence independently
of whether the rules themselves are well designed. The fiction fragments
because participants are not holding it in common.

Designers can reduce this risk by making the game’s orientation legible
from the first page: what kind of object is this, what does it ask of
you, and what will it produce. Players can reduce it by stating their
expectations before play begins and by treating the fiction as a genuine
common object rather than a stage for individual expression.

A fiction engine is a social agreement before it is a mechanical system.
The rules formalize that agreement, but they cannot substitute for it.

## Criteria

The core criteria for a fiction engine are procedural rather than
scalar. A game belongs more fully to this framework when:

- Every rule either produces fictional change or supports the conditions
  for it.
- Procedures remain transparent in use: the table engages with the
  situation, not the system.
- Outcomes advance or complicate the fiction rather than suspending it
  pending further resolution.
- It remains legible when the fiction becomes unstable or surprising.
- Players describe what happens in fictional terms rather than
  mechanical ones.
- The game makes its own orientation legible enough that all
  participants can hold the fiction as a common object rather than a
  personal stage.

Several qualities often accompany fiction engine design without being
definitional to it. A fiction engine tends to be learnable quickly,
requires little setup, and keeps cognitive load low. These are common
tendencies that follow from the commitment to thin mediation, not
requirements of the paradigm itself. A game with a rich setup procedure
that establishes a deep fictional situation before play begins can still
be a fiction engine, provided the play procedures themselves serve the
fiction once it is running.

A game moves away from this framework when players spend more time
talking about the system than about the situation.

## Glossary

**Diegetic.** Belonging to the fiction: the layer of characters, places,
events, and conditions that all participants hold in common and treat as
real within the game. A diegetic element — a character’s wound, a
crumbling building, a political alliance — exists inside the fiction. A
non-diegetic element — a hit point total, a fate point, a stress track —
exists outside the fiction as part of the game’s administrative layer.
Fiction engines aim to keep as much as possible diegetic.

**Fictional intent.** What a player’s character is attempting within the
fiction. Fictional intent is the input to any resolution procedure: the
player describes what they want to do inside the fiction, and the game
produces a fictional outcome in response.

**Oracle.** One mechanism for distributing narrative authority in solo
or low-referee play. An oracle typically takes a closed question about
the fiction — does the door open? is the guard suspicious? — and
produces a yes or no answer, often with gradations or complications. It
functions as a fiction engine component when its outputs read as
fictional events rather than prompts requiring interpretive work before
play can continue. Other authority mechanisms — referees, shared
authorship structures, randomized prompt systems — are subject to the
same functional test.

**Overhead.** Procedural steps, tracking obligations, or resource
management layers that neither produce a change to the fiction nor
support the conditions under which fictional change can occur. This
document distinguishes dead overhead from fiction-supporting
infrastructure: safety tools, pacing mechanisms, campaign logs, and
similar systems may be non-diegetic while still being load-bearing. Dead
overhead is the target for removal; fiction-supporting infrastructure is
not.

**Referee.** The participant whose role is to mediate between the
fiction and the other players: describing the world’s response to player
action, adjudicating outcomes, and maintaining the consistency of the
fiction. Some games distribute this role across all participants; others
eliminate it in favor of procedural tools such as oracles.

## Conclusion

The fiction engine paradigm rests on a single bet: that a system
transparent enough to disappear into the fiction produces better play —
for the specific aims this paradigm serves — than one that makes its own
operation the object of attention. This does not mean that other
paradigms are wrong. Some games intentionally foreground procedure
because procedure itself creates tension, ritual, thematic pressure, or
strategic identity. That is a coherent design choice, and it produces
experiences a fiction engine is not designed to produce. Different
designs are optimized for different things, and knowing what your design
is optimized for is more useful than claiming universality.

A fiction engine is not an argument that stories can be mechanically
controlled. It is a design approach in which the system is light enough,
responsive enough, and fiction-facing enough that the story appears to
emerge through play rather than being processed through procedure. The
rules are infrastructure. The fiction is the point.

# Glossary

Abstraction of form  
A mechanical notation for a condition that exists inside the fiction.
Hit point totals, wound tracks, and exhaustion counters are abstractions
of form: the character’s physical state is diegetic; the number that
tracks it is not. Abstractions of form can operate in the background of
play — the player thinks “she is badly hurt” rather than “she has 4 hit
points” — which means they can carry their weight without demanding
conscious attention. Contrast with abstraction of procedure.

Abstraction of procedure  
A required classification step between fictional intent and fictional
outcome. When a player must identify which move applies, which category
an action falls into, or which subsystem governs the current situation
before the fiction can advance, that step is an abstraction of
procedure. Unlike abstraction of form, it cannot operate fully in the
background: it requires conscious engagement to complete. This is the
mechanism by which designs cross the transparency threshold.

Accumulated fiction  
A fiction in which prior events constrain current possibility in
structural terms. In accumulated fiction, a burned bridge cannot be
treated as intact; an alliance formed under duress carries that duress
into every subsequent interaction; a public failure has established a
reputation that shapes what other characters will plausibly do. Each
outcome adds to a causal tissue that narrows and differentiates the
possibility space for what can follow. Contrast with episodic fiction.

Agent  
A participant in the play system who takes inputs and produces outputs:
a player describing what their character attempts, a referee describing
the world’s response, an oracle answering a question about the fiction’s
state. Each agent has some model of the fiction’s current state and acts
on that model to produce the next state. The feedback loops that enable
emergent fiction run between agents.

Authority drift  
The failure mode in which a narrative authority mechanism — a referee,
an oracle, a prompt system — shifts from producing fictional events
toward producing administrative prompts that require significant
interpretive work before the fiction can advance. An oracle output that
reads as a task (“find a way to resolve the tension”) rather than an
event (“the contact is already gone when you arrive”) exemplifies
authority drift. The mechanism has become an obstacle between fictional
intent and fictional change rather than a conduit for it.

Causal tissue  
The network of established fictional events, conditions, and facts such
that each element is both a consequence of what preceded it and a
constraint on what can plausibly follow. Causal tissue is what gives
fictional consequences weight, makes choices non-equivalent, and allows
surprises to emerge from within the fiction rather than arriving from
outside it. The fiction engine paradigm’s procedural commitments —
outcomes as continuations, fictional states over administrative records,
the prohibition on dead overhead — are, in significant part, commitments
to maintaining the causal tissue that accumulated fiction requires.

Dead overhead  
Procedural steps, tracking obligations, or resource management layers
that neither produce a change to the fiction nor support the conditions
under which fictional change can occur. The distinction between dead
overhead and fiction-supporting infrastructure is one of function rather
than form. A rule is dead overhead when removing it leaves the fiction
moving and coherent. A rule is load-bearing when removing it causes play
to stall or lose legibility. Dead overhead is the target for removal;
fiction-supporting infrastructure is not.

Diegetic  
Belonging to the fiction — the layer of characters, places, events, and
conditions that all participants hold in common and treat as real within
the game. A character’s wound, a crumbling building, a political
alliance: these are diegetic elements. They exist inside the fiction.
Hit point totals, fate points, and stress tracks are non-diegetic: they
exist outside the fiction as part of the game’s administrative layer.
Fiction engines aim to keep as much as possible diegetic without
treating non-diegetic elements as automatically invalid.

Emergence / emergent fiction  
A property of the play system in which the fiction produced by a
session’s end could not have been predicted from the elements at its
start, and in which the unpredictable development is felt as arising
from within the situation rather than being imposed from outside it.
Emergence is not a mystical property. It is a structural consequence of
feedback loops between agents sensitive to each other’s outputs, closing
over accumulated fictional state, with a procedural layer thin enough
not to interrupt them. When those conditions are present, emergence is
possible. When any of them is absent, it is not.

Entry state  
The position a design occupies relative to the transparency threshold on
the first session of play, before any internalization has occurred. The
entry state is one component of a design’s trajectory. A design whose
entry state is significantly above the transparency threshold imposes
real attentional costs on new players regardless of how clean its stable
state is. Better documentation can reduce confusion about unfamiliar
rules; it cannot reduce the attentional cost of rules that require
conscious engagement to operate.

Episodic fiction  
A fiction in which each session, scene, or significant outcome begins
from a roughly equivalent state. Prior events are acknowledged in tone —
players know what happened, characters reference it — but they do not
constrain current possibility in structural terms. Episodic and
accumulated fiction are not a hierarchy. They are different
architectural choices, each optimized for different things. A one-shot
is defined by deliberate episodic structure. A long campaign that fails
to accumulate fictional weight across sessions is producing episodic
fiction unintentionally.

Facilitation distribution  
The absorption of procedural weight by participants other than the
player in question. In group play, an experienced referee or player who
has internalized a procedure can carry the classification step, hold the
taxonomy in mind, or manage the pressure economy in the background,
allowing other players to stay oriented toward the fiction. No
facilitation distribution is available in solo play: every procedural
step is the solo player’s sole responsibility. This is the structural
reason the transparency threshold is effectively lower for solo design.

Feedback loop  
A cycle in the play system in which one agent’s output becomes another
agent’s input, and the second agent’s output in turn becomes input for
the first. In play terms: a character acts, the world responds, the
character responds to the response. Feedback loops enable emergent
fiction when they close over accumulated fictional state and when the
agents involved are genuinely sensitive to each other’s outputs rather
than pursuing fixed agendas. Thick procedural mediation interrupts
feedback loops at the classification step; thin mediation preserves
them.

Fiction  
The shared diegetic situation: the characters, places, events, and
conditions that all participants hold in common during play and treat as
real within the game. Fiction is not narrative craft, literary quality,
or storytelling in the writerly sense. It is the living, mutable
situation that play produces and transforms moment to moment. When this
book says a rule should serve the fiction, it means the rule should
produce changes to the fiction rather than changes to a score, a
resource pool, or an administrative record that exists outside it.

Fiction engine  
A design whose every rule either produces fictional change, supports the
conditions under which fictional change can occur, or is a candidate for
removal. The commitment is structural: it applies to every design
decision, and the test applies to each rule individually and to the
system as a whole. The fiction engine paradigm values thin mediation,
fictional momentum, and minimal procedural overhead. It accepts, as a
consequence, that it provides less structural scaffolding than paradigms
built around dramatic prescription or managed pressure.

Fiction-supporting infrastructure  
Non-diegetic rules that preserve the coherence, legibility, or
continuity that makes fictional change possible. Safety tools, pacing
mechanisms, campaign logs, and certain tracking systems may be
non-diegetic in form while still being load-bearing in function. The
distinction between fiction-supporting infrastructure and dead overhead
is one of function: does removing this rule cause the fiction to stall
or lose coherence? If yes, the rule is infrastructure. The category can
expand to absorb almost anything if left unbounded, which is why the
combined attentional cost of all load-bearing rules must also be
evaluated, not only each rule in isolation.

Fictional intent  
What a player’s character is attempting within the fiction. Fictional
intent is the input to any resolution procedure: the player describes
what they want to do inside the fiction, and the game produces a
fictional outcome in response. The procedural layer between fictional
intent and fictional outcome should be as thin as the situation allows.

Fictional state  
A condition that exists inside the fiction and is visible to the
characters within it. A fictional state is a property of the world, not
a property of the game record. The test: remove the game record and ask
whether the condition still exists inside the fiction as something
participants can see, respond to, and reason about. Fictional states
survive that removal. Administrative records do not.

Internalization  
The process by which a procedure that initially requires conscious
attention becomes habitual through repetition, and its attentional cost
drops. A classification step that demands deliberate engagement during
the first sessions of play may operate nearly invisibly after extended
exposure. Internalization is the mechanism by which a design that begins
above the transparency threshold can arrive at a below-threshold
position in practice. The speed of internalization varies by rule: rules
tightly coupled to actions players are already performing internalize
quickly; rules that activate rarely accumulate fewer repetitions and
internalize slowly.

Learning curve  
The middle component of a design’s trajectory — the span of play across
which procedures move from the attentional foreground toward the
background through internalization. The length and steepness of the
learning curve are design properties with real consequences, not
documentation problems. A design with a long learning curve that
provides no support for completing it loses players before they reach
the stable state, regardless of how good that stable state is.

Metacurrency  
A resource that exists outside the fiction as a mechanical buffer,
earned and spent by players to modify outcomes or shape narrative. Fate
points, action points, and similar tokens are metacurrencies. A
metacurrency is not automatically dead overhead: some metacurrency
systems are fiction-supported in their generation, fiction-oriented in
their effect, and kept minimal enough that they operate in the
background. Whether a given metacurrency belongs in a fiction engine
depends on its combined administrative cost and the degree to which it
shifts the player’s attention from the fiction to the resource pool.

Oracle  
A mechanism for distributing narrative authority in solo or low-referee
play. An oracle typically takes a closed question about the fiction — is
the guard suspicious? does the contact show up? — and produces an
answer, often with gradations or complications. It functions as a
fiction engine component when its outputs read as fictional events
rather than prompts requiring interpretive work before play can
continue. In solo play specifically, the oracle functions as a second
agent in the feedback loop: it introduces outputs the solo player did
not generate, which is the structural condition for emergent fiction
when only one person is playing. An oracle that merely resolves
uncertainty without generating independent fictional facts is a weaker
second agent than one whose outputs add to the accumulated causal
tissue.

Orientational divergence  
The failure mode in which participants hold different implicit models of
what the game is doing — one treating it as a tactical puzzle, another
as character exploration, another as improvised theater — and the engine
loses coherence as a result, independently of whether the rules are
functioning correctly. The fiction fragments because participants are
not holding it in common. Orientational divergence is a failure of the
social agreement that underlies any game. Design can reduce the risk by
making the game’s orientation legible from the first page; it cannot
eliminate the risk by rule.

Overhead accumulation  
The failure mode in which rules individually justifiable each impose
small attentional costs that collectively shift the table’s attention
from the fiction to the system. The engine stalls not through a single
bad rule but through the accumulated weight of many marginal ones. A
more insidious variant is fiction-supporting overhead accumulation:
rules that are each producing real fictional value while collectively
driving the design above the transparency threshold. The combined
attentional cost of all load-bearing rules must be evaluated as a
system, not only rule by rule.

Pressure engine  
A design paradigm in which a managed resource economy — stress,
momentum, heat, capacity — creates tension through the strategic
visibility of its states and the cost of engaging with the fiction under
pressure. A pressure engine sits above the transparency threshold by
design: the resource economy is not background infrastructure but a
persistent object of attention whose visible management is the
experience the design is producing. Breathless is the clearest example
in this book’s comparative analysis: fiction engine DNA in its
resolution core, pressure engine structure in its die degradation and
reset economy.

RAT checklist  
A three-part test for evaluating referee outcomes, developed in the
context of open strategy game design and applicable to fiction engine
adjudication generally. Reasonable: is this outcome proportionate to
what the fiction has established? Actionable: does this outcome advance
the fiction rather than suspend it, leaving at least one participant
something to respond to? Traceable: can you point to prior fictional
events that led here — does the outcome feel like a consequence rather
than a contrivance? An outcome that fails any test should be rewritten
before play continues. The Traceable criterion is the element not
captured by the fiction engine framework’s existing guidance on
outcomes; it is what distinguishes a fiction that accumulates from one
that resets.

Referee  
The participant whose role is to mediate between the fiction and the
other players: describing the world’s response to player action,
adjudicating outcomes, and maintaining the consistency of the fiction.
Some games distribute this role across all participants; others
eliminate it in favor of procedural tools such as oracles. In both
configurations, the principle is the same: the authority mechanism must
produce fictional output rather than administrative prompts.

Second agent  
In solo play, the structural role an oracle or equivalent mechanism must
fill to enable emergent fiction. A solo player cannot form a genuine
feedback loop with themselves: without an independent source of
fictional content, the loop closes over the player’s own intent and
emergence of the relevant kind is not possible. An oracle functions as a
second agent when its outputs are independent of the player’s intent and
constrained by the accumulated fictional state. Both conditions are
necessary. An oracle too responsive to player intent is confirming
rather than contributing. An oracle disconnected from the accumulated
fictional state generates noise rather than surprise.

Sensitivity  
The property by which an agent’s output is shaped by the specific prior
outputs of other agents, not merely by the agent’s own goals or
intentions. A player who adjusts what their character does because the
situation has changed in a specific way is exhibiting sensitivity. A
player pursuing a pre-formed plan regardless of what has happened is
not. Sensitivity is the difference between a conversation and a sequence
of speeches. It is the precondition for feedback loops to carry
genuinely independent material, and therefore the precondition for
emergent fiction.

Stable state  
The position a design occupies relative to the transparency threshold
once the learning curve is complete — the experience the design produces
for players who have internalized its procedures. A design’s stable
state is its intended operating condition. Designing only for the stable
state and treating the entry experience as a documentation problem is a
common error: players who leave during the learning curve never reach
the stable state, regardless of its quality.

Suspended outcome  
A resolution that produces a result requiring further resolution before
play can continue. Designs that chain resolution steps — roll to
attempt, then roll to determine degree, then consult a table to
translate degree into fictional terms — are prone to this. Each
additional step is a potential suspension point where the fiction waits
on the procedure. A result that leaves the fiction suspended rather than
transformed indicates a design problem, not a neutral state.

Trajectory  
The full arc of a design’s relationship to the transparency threshold
across time: its entry state, its learning curve, and its stable state.
A design is not accurately described by a single threshold position; it
has three. The trajectory is a property of the design-in-use across
sessions, not a property of the document. Two designs with identical
stable states can have dramatically different trajectories if one
internalizes quickly and one does not. Designing deliberately means
designing all three phases, not only the destination.

Transparency threshold  
The point at which the player’s attention must shift from the fiction to
the procedure in order to advance play. Below the threshold, procedure
operates as background infrastructure: the player stays inside the
fiction and the rules respond. Above the threshold, the player must
engage the system as an object in order to produce fictional output. The
threshold is not fixed: it is player-variable, shifting as procedures
internalize and as facilitation distribution becomes available. In solo
play, the effective threshold is lower because no facilitation
distribution exists. The threshold marks a location on a continuum, not
a pass/fail test. Knowing which side of it a design inhabits, and why,
is more useful than claiming a generic fiction-first identity.

World engine  
The OSR and FKR design tradition, in which thin procedures serve world
interaction and player problem-solving rather than fictional flow. A
world engine keeps procedure light for the same reason a fiction engine
does — to prevent overhead from occluding the game’s primary object —
but the primary object differs. In a world engine, the primary object is
the world as a coherent problem space: what is actually here, what can I
actually do, what are the real consequences of this action inside a
world that operates according to its own logic. A ruling that halts play
because the world’s logic requires it is a correct ruling in a world
engine. A ruling that halts the fiction is a failed ruling in a fiction
engine. The procedural thinness the two traditions share does not make
them the same design.
