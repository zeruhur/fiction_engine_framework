# The Threshold as Trajectory
## Designing a System That Changes Its Own Properties Through Use

*Part of the Fiction Engine companion essay series.*
*Application essay for the systems thinking arc.*

---

The transparency threshold essay makes an observation in the middle of its argument that it does not stop to develop: "the same game can sit on different sides of the threshold depending on who is playing it and how long they have been playing it." The essay names three consequences of this — the learning threshold is distinct from a permanent transparency problem; how a game is introduced affects the threshold experience; solo play demands a lower threshold because no facilitation distribution is available — and then moves on to the next argument.

The observation deserved more time. What it describes is not a complication of the threshold concept. It is a fact about what a game design actually is: not a fixed object with stable properties, but a system whose operating conditions change as agents use it. The threshold is player-variable because players internalize procedures over time. That internalization is the system changing its own behavior. And if a design changes as it is used, then the designer is not producing a document with fixed properties. The designer is producing a trajectory — an entry experience, a learning curve, and a stable state — and all three require deliberate design decisions.

The fiction engine framework's central test asks what a rule does. That is the right first question. But it is not the only question. A rule that passes the test on paper may still impose significant attentional costs during early play, before it has been internalized, and those costs are as real as any overhead the test is designed to eliminate. The framework has no vocabulary for this. The trajectory concept is that vocabulary.

---

## What Internalization Is

A procedure that requires conscious attention when novel becomes habitual with repetition. This is not a claim about memory in the technical sense. It is a description of something any experienced player recognizes: the moment when you stop consulting the move list and simply play, when the die results stop requiring translation and their meaning arrives directly, when the question of what counts as risky no longer requires deliberate judgment because you have developed a calibrated feel for it.

The procedure has not changed. The player has changed. The classification step that once required a conscious stop — which move is this, what category does this action fall into — now runs below the level of deliberate attention. The attentional cost has dropped, not because anything was removed from the design but because the player's relationship to it has shifted.

This is a systems phenomenon in the most straightforward sense: the agents using the system are modifying their own response patterns in ways that change how the system operates. The feedback loops described in Essay 2 — the loops between agents that enable emergent behavior — run faster and with less interruption as classification steps move into procedural memory. Internalization is the mechanism by which a design that begins above the transparency threshold can arrive, over time, at a below-threshold state in practice.

The trajectory concept follows directly. A design that begins above the threshold and internalizes toward it is not accurately described by a single threshold position. It has three positions worth naming separately: where it starts on session one, how quickly and reliably it internalizes, and where it stabilizes once the learning curve is complete. A complete description of a design's relationship to the transparency threshold requires all three.

This reframing changes the design questions. The framework's central test asks what a rule does — whether it produces fictional change, supports the conditions for it, or generates dead overhead. The trajectory framing adds: where does this rule sit in the learning curve, how quickly does it internalize, and what is the experience it produces while still in the foreground? A rule that passes the first test can still fail the second set if it internalizes slowly, activates rarely, or imposes entry costs that drive players away before the stable state is reached.

## The Four Designs as Trajectories

The four-designs essay placed FU, 24XX, Breathless, and Hints and Hijinx on the threshold map as if they had fixed positions. Applying the trajectory lens to each reveals that their positions are more accurately described as ranges — entry states, curves, and stable states — and that the ranges differ significantly even among designs clustered in the same neighborhood.

**24XX** is the clearest case because its trajectory is the shortest. The core procedure — describe what you do, roll only if it is risky, interpret the result as a fictional outcome — requires so little conscious attention from the start that it has almost no learning curve to speak of. A player is making habitual rolls by the end of the first session, not because they have learned the rules but because there is so little to learn. The design starts near its stable state and stays there. The defense mechanic (say how an item breaks to absorb a hit) begins below the threshold by design; the mechanical action and the fictional event are the same action. There is no step to internalize because there is no step.

The cost of this short trajectory is structural support at the entry phase. 24XX offers nothing to players who need scaffolding during session one: no move list to consult, no genre texture baked into the resolution procedure, no pacing structure beyond the fictional situation itself. Groups that can bring their own situation and stay in it will reach the stable state immediately. Groups that need more help during the learning phase will not find it in the rules.

**Freeform Universal** follows a similar pattern but with a small variable. The resolution procedure — describe the attempt, roll, interpret the result on the six-point scale — internalizes quickly, within the first session for most players. The FU Points economy takes slightly longer, not because it is complicated but because earning and spending opportunities arrive intermittently rather than on every roll. The internalization curve for FU Points is still short by any comparison. FU's typical trajectory is a brief learning threshold followed by rapid arrival at a below-threshold stable state.

The variable is the FU Points dial. Groups that use FU Points heavily introduce a light metacurrency foreground, and those groups stabilize at a slightly different position than groups that run without them. The design presents this as a feature rather than a problem — the dial is documented and adjustable — and in trajectory terms it means the stable state is a range rather than a point. Different groups inhabit different stable states, all within the same general neighborhood, depending on how they configure the optional economy. That is a coherent design choice, and it produces a design that is adaptable across table cultures rather than optimized for a single one.

**Breathless** is where the trajectory analysis does its most useful work, because it reveals a split that the threshold map alone does not show.

The die degradation and reset mechanics internalize quickly. They are coupled to play actions the player is already performing: you roll to resolve, and the die degrades as part of the same action. There is no separate tracking obligation. The catch your breath reset is similarly direct: you declare it, the GM introduces a complication, the dice reset. By the third or fourth roll, most players have internalized both mechanics without conscious effort. The fiction engine core of Breathless — the part that shares its DNA with 24XX — follows a short trajectory.

The pressure engine layer follows a different trajectory. The stress track and combined resource management internalize more slowly because they impose ongoing tracking obligations that are separate from the play actions they accompany. A player must hold multiple counts in mind simultaneously: skill die ratings, stress points, loot die rating, backpack contents. Each is individually trackable. Combined, they constitute a persistent attentional demand. This layer does not converge toward background infrastructure through use. It is designed to stay somewhat foregrounded, because the pressure engine experience — the tension of depleting resources, the risk calculus of the catch your breath moment — depends on the player being aware of the resource states. A stress track the player has stopped noticing is not producing the experience Breathless is optimized to produce.

The trajectory split is the precise description of what the four-designs essay called "pressure engine DNA inside a fiction engine core." The fiction engine core has a short trajectory. The pressure engine layer has a stable state that is deliberately above the threshold. For designers working from the Breathless SRD — and there are many; the SRD has generated well over two hundred derivatives, the majority of them solo designs — this split is the most practically important thing the analysis can tell them. The core can be taken and used with a very short learning curve. The pressure engine layer is a specific design commitment with a specific stable state, and adapting it for a different design goal means reconsidering whether that stable state is the one you want.

**Hints and Hijinx** presents a different kind of trajectory problem. The investigation phase — the Breathless-core part of the design — follows the same curve as Breathless's fiction engine core: short, below-threshold stable state, fast internalization. The clue economy in the deduction phase does not follow any internalization curve because it activates once per session, at a specific structural moment, rather than repeatedly throughout play.

Internalization requires repetition. A player who makes a dozen rolls per session develops procedural memory for the resolution mechanic. A player who performs the deduction phase calculation once per session does not develop the same kind of habit for it. The crossing remains somewhat novel each time. This is neither a success nor a failure of design; it is the consequence of the architectural containment the four-designs essay identified. The threshold crossing happens in a single moment, which means the player never has enough repetitions to fully automate it. The clue economy is always slightly foregrounded, always requiring a small amount of conscious attention when it activates. For a mystery investigation design, this may be exactly correct. The deduction phase is supposed to feel distinct from the investigation phase — structurally marked, slightly more effortful. The trajectory analysis gives a precise account of why it does.

**PbtA as a broader case.** The threshold essay notes that experienced PbtA players internalize move structure to near-invisibility. This is the clearest available example of a design with a significant learning threshold that eventually reaches a below-threshold stable state for the subset of players who complete the curve. The entry state is substantially above the threshold: a new player must learn to identify move triggers, consult move descriptions, identify which move applies, and track move outputs before the fiction can advance. An experienced Apocalypse World player has internalized the move taxonomy to near-automaticity; the classification step's attentional cost has dropped close to zero. The design operates at a threshold position that would be nearly invisible to a veteran and conspicuous to a newcomer.

The trajectory for PbtA is long, and for many players never reaches completion. PbtA games with large, established communities compensate through facilitation infrastructure that is not part of the design document: session-one advice, experienced players who can carry procedural weight while others are still building habits. A smaller design without that infrastructure has a real problem if its trajectory is equally long.

## What Designing a Trajectory Requires

The trajectory concept changes four design questions in ways worth naming directly.

**The entry experience is a design problem.** Designers frequently treat a steep learning curve as a documentation problem: the rules are fine, the explanation needs work. This conflation is common and consequential. If a design's entry state is significantly above the transparency threshold, that is a fact about the architecture, not about the manual. Better instructions can reduce confusion about rules that are already well-designed. They cannot reduce the attentional cost of rules that require conscious engagement to operate, because that cost is built into the rules themselves.

A designer thinking in trajectory terms asks a different question: what is the player's experience on session one, session three, and session ten, and are all three consistent with the design's goals? A design whose goals are best served by a long learning curve — one that rewards players who invest in it significantly — should be built to support that investment explicitly, not designed for a short trajectory and then documented as if complexity can be explained away. A design whose goals are best served by fiction-transparent play from the first session should examine every rule that imposes entry costs independently of whether that rule is load-bearing at the stable state.

**Fast-internalizing rules and slow-internalizing rules differ in kind, not just rate.** The difference between them is not incidental. Rules that are tightly coupled to the play actions players are already performing internalize quickly precisely because the practice is constant. The 24XX defense mechanic internalizes quickly because characters take hits throughout play; the player practices the mechanic under the same conditions where it matters. The Breathless die degradation internalizes quickly because every resolution step involves it. Rules that activate rarely — once per session, once per phase, only when a specific fictional condition is met — internalize slowly because the practice is infrequent. Reference material compensates but does not eliminate the cost.

A designer building toward a below-threshold stable state should front-load fast-internalizing rules wherever possible: rules that are practiced constantly, rules whose mechanical action and fictional consequence are unified, rules that respond to player actions the player is already taking. Slow-internalizing rules are not automatically wrong, but they deserve scrutiny that fast-internalizing rules do not. A slow-internalizing rule that is load-bearing at the stable state may still be worth keeping, with the recognition that it will impose entry costs for longer than a fast-internalizing equivalent. A slow-internalizing rule that is only marginally load-bearing is a stronger candidate for removal than the framework's standard diagnostic alone would identify.

**The stable state is not the only design problem.** Every design has a stable state: the experience it produces once the learning curve is complete. The fiction engine paradigm's stable state is fiction-transparent play, where the procedure operates in the background and the fiction remains the primary object of attention. That stable state is worth designing for. It is also only part of the design problem.

A design that produces an excellent stable state but a poor entry experience loses most of its players before they reach it. The entry experience and the learning curve are not preludes to the design; they are part of it. This is not an argument for making all games easier to learn. Complexity and depth can be real design values, and designs that reward significant investment are legitimate. It is an argument for intentionality: a designer who knows their design has a long learning curve, and knows why, is better positioned to support players through it than one who treats the learning curve as a natural consequence of the design's depth rather than as a designed feature in its own right.

**The solo implication deserves its own treatment.** The threshold essay's observation that the threshold is effectively lower for solo design is correct, but the trajectory framing makes it more precise. A solo player's internalization curve is not merely longer. It is longer and less reliable, for a structural reason: there is no facilitation distribution available during the learning phase.

In group play, an experienced player or referee can absorb procedural weight while others are still building habits. One person at the table who has internalized the move taxonomy frees the others from having to consciously perform the classification step themselves; the experienced player can name the applicable move before the new player has finished identifying it. This distribution is not available to a solo player. Every procedural step is the solo player's sole responsibility, which means every step that is still in the attentional foreground remains there until that single player has internalized it alone. The learning curve is identical in length and harder to complete.

This has a concrete design implication. Solo designs should aim for a shorter learning curve and a lower entry threshold regardless of their intended stable state position. The Breathless SRD's pressure engine layer is an example of a design commitment whose costs are substantially higher in solo play than the design's group context suggests, which is one reason why the most successful solo Breathless derivatives tend to either simplify the tracking economy or replace the stress track with something that has a faster internalization curve. That is not a failure of the source material. It is a recognition that the trajectory is different for one player working alone.

## The Teaching Implication

The threshold essay names a practical observation: a game introduced through its fiction ("here is the situation, here is what your character might attempt, here is what happened") feels different from the same game introduced through its mechanics ("here are the six stats, here are the move categories, here are the roll triggers"). Both introductions eventually arrive at the same system. They produce different experiences of getting there, and those experiences shape how players relate to the design.

The trajectory framing makes the structural reason visible. Teaching fiction-first places the player at the design's stable state before the mechanics have been introduced. The player's first orientation is to the fiction: the situation, the characters, the conditions. When mechanics emerge from that orientation (you want to force the door, roll; here is how to interpret the result), they arrive as infrastructure for something the player is already doing. The attentional habits the stable state requires are already forming. The learning curve runs in the background.

Teaching mechanics-first does the opposite. The player's first orientation is to the system. The fiction arrives later, filling in around a procedural architecture already established as the primary object of attention. That habit — engaging the system consciously — does not disappear when the fiction is introduced. It competes with the fiction for the player's attention during early play.

For fiction engines specifically, the choice is not symmetric. Teaching fiction-first is consistent with what the design is trying to produce. A player who encounters the fiction first, and the mechanics second, has already begun building the attentional habits the stable state requires.

A related implication: the design document's structure is itself a teaching choice. A game that opens with the world, the situation, the characters — and introduces mechanics as they become necessary — makes a different decision than a game that opens with a structured rules overview. A fiction engine that opens with a structured rules overview is asking its reader to begin in the mechanics-first mode, and that beginning shapes everything that follows. The design document is the first session.

## Where This Leaves the Framework

The fiction engine framework describes its criteria in synchronic terms: a game either is or is not producing fictional change, either does or does not sit below the transparency threshold. The framework's diagnostic — remove a rule and observe whether the fiction still moves — treats the design as a stable object that can be tested in isolation. This framing is analytically useful. It produces real discriminations.

The trajectory framing adds a dimension the synchronic analysis lacks. A design is not simply above or below the threshold; it has an entry position, a learning curve, and a stable state. A rule that passes the remove-and-observe diagnostic may still impose significant entry costs. A design whose stable state is below the threshold may lose most of its players during the learning curve. A design whose stable state is above the threshold may reach it more reliably than a competing design with a longer learning curve to a below-threshold destination. The framework's criteria evaluate rules in isolation. The trajectory question evaluates the system over time, across the range of players who will use it.

These are different questions, and they can produce different answers for the same design. A design evaluated by the framework's criteria might look excellent: thin procedures, fictional states, minimal overhead, outcomes as continuations. The same design evaluated as a trajectory might reveal a slow-internalizing rule in a critical position, or an entry experience that does not communicate the design's orientation quickly enough to prevent players from establishing competing attentional habits before the fiction can become primary. Neither evaluation is wrong. They are operating at different scales — the synchronic and the temporal — and designs that hold up well under both deserve more confidence than designs that pass only one.

The framework currently has the tools for the synchronic evaluation. The trajectory evaluation requires additions: the distinction between fast-internalizing and slow-internalizing rules, the entry experience as a separate design problem from the stable state, the question of how the learning curve is completed and what support the design provides for completing it. These additions do not contradict the framework's existing criteria. They extend the analysis into a dimension the framework has left unaddressed.

## Designing the Trajectory

A designer who treats their design as a fixed object with stable properties is optimizing for a single point: the stable state. That optimization is not wrong. The stable state is what experienced players inhabit, and a stable state worth reaching is worth designing carefully.

But the stable state is not where new players begin. It is where players arrive after a trajectory that the design either supports or does not. A designer who treats their design as a system that changes its properties through use is optimizing for the whole trajectory — entry, curve, and stable state — and making deliberate decisions about each.

The fiction engine paradigm's commitment to thin mediation is partly a commitment to a short, smooth trajectory: a design that reaches its stable state quickly and maintains it reliably. Essay 1 showed that accumulated fiction is the structural property worth protecting. Essay 2 showed that thin mediation creates the feedback conditions under which accumulated fiction can produce emergent behavior. This essay has argued that thin mediation also produces a short internalization curve — thin procedures are fast-internalizing almost by definition — which means the design reaches the conditions for emergent behavior earlier and holds them more consistently than designs with longer learning curves.

Understanding this is what allows a designer to choose thin mediation deliberately rather than by convention, or to choose a longer trajectory deliberately rather than by accumulation. The choice is not between good design and bad design. It is between design that knows what it is building toward and design that discovers its trajectory only after players have already experienced it.

The trajectory framing has revealed something the earlier essays in this arc pointed toward without naming. The fiction engine framework identifies the conditions for a specific kind of play. It does not produce that play directly; only agents using the design over time can do that. But the conditions are real, and knowing that they are conditions rather than guarantees, and that they are experienced by players across a trajectory rather than at a single point, is the beginning of designing them with precision. That distinction — between design properties and the system behaviors they enable — is what the final essay takes as its subject.
