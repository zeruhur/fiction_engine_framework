# One Agent, One Loop

_This essay is part of the Fiction Engine companion essay series, following the systems thinking arc: "Fiction and Memory," "The Emergence Condition," "The Threshold as Trajectory," and "Properties and Behaviors." It is also a companion to "Fiction Engine: A Design Framework for Tabletop RPGs" (v1.0, May 2026)._

## The Solo Fiction Engine as a Distinct Design Problem

The solo dimension has appeared three times in this series without becoming its subject. The transparency threshold essay noted that the threshold is effectively lower for solo design because no facilitation distribution is available. The four-designs essay observed that three of its four designs are solo-capable and that Breathless's tracking load carries different costs in solo play than its group context suggests. The systems thinking arc established the feedback loop conditions that emergent fiction requires and noted, without developing the point, that those conditions change when there is only one player.

What has been deferred is this: solo fiction engine design is not group fiction engine design with one player. The structural conditions are different, not diminished. The feedback network has a different shape. The internalization problem has a different character. The contribution of accumulated fiction has a different source. Each of these differences produces specific design decisions that a theory of group fiction engine design cannot fully anticipate. This essay makes those differences precise.

## The Subtraction Error

The threshold essay frames the solo problem as a resource deficit: a solo player has no facilitation distribution available, so the threshold is effectively lower. The observation is correct. The framing is incomplete.

Treating solo play as group play minus a resource implies that the structural conditions for the relevant behaviors are present but strained — that the same feedback loops run, only with more load on one participant. This is not what the structural difference is. The feedback network does not thin when a group reduces to one player. It changes shape.

In group play, the feedback loop runs between agents. A player describes what their character attempts. The referee or another player produces a response. The first player responds to that response. The loop closes over material generated by another agent — material the first player did not produce and could not fully anticipate. The condition for emergent development is met: the loop is closing over genuinely independent outputs.

In solo play, there is one agent. The loop cannot close between agents because no second agent exists. Whatever the solo player produces in response to the fiction is a response to material they have, to varying degrees, already shaped. Left entirely to their own outputs, the loop closes over the player's own intent. Emergence of the relevant kind — the sensation that the fiction is going somewhere nobody planned — is not possible from a single agent generating all the fiction's content. The player can surprise themselves at the margins, through the specificity of character and situation, but the structural condition for the kind of emergence the paradigm is designed to produce is absent.

The oracle is what changes this. Not as a convenience for replacing the referee, not as a yes/no generator for resolving uncertainty, but as the structural mechanism that introduces outputs the solo player did not generate. It is the second agent in the feedback loop.

Without an oracle, or something functionally equivalent, solo fiction engine design does not have the conditions for emergent play. The player is only ever responding to themselves.

## The Oracle as Second Agent

The fiction engine framework defines the oracle as a mechanism for distributing narrative authority in solo or low-referee play, one that takes a closed question about the fiction and produces a yes or no answer, often with gradations or complications. The functional criterion is that its outputs should read as fictional events rather than prompts requiring interpretive work before play can continue.

This is the right criterion for output quality. It says nothing about what the oracle is structurally doing.

With the feedback loop argument in place, the structural criterion becomes more precise. An oracle functions as a second agent when its outputs are independent of the player's intent and constrained by the accumulated fictional state. Both conditions are necessary. Each, without the other, produces a different failure.

An oracle whose outputs are too closely determined by player input is a weak second agent. If the oracle consistently confirms what the player intended — if the answer is essentially "yes, and here is the fiction you described" — the loop closes over the player's own intent. No independent material enters the fiction. The player is generating all the content and receiving confirmation of it. The fiction is coherent but not surprising in the way accumulated fiction with genuine feedback can be.

An oracle whose outputs are disconnected from the accumulated fictional state is also a weak second agent, for the opposite reason. A random table that ignores fictional context — that produces outputs with no relationship to what has been established — generates noise rather than surprise. The loop closes over material that contradicts or ignores the accumulated state. The fiction does not develop; it fragments.

The productive oracle sits between these positions. It produces outputs that the player did not generate, which means the loop has genuinely independent material to close over. It produces outputs constrained by the accumulated fictional state, which means that independent material connects to the causal tissue rather than arriving from outside it. The surprise is real because the player did not cause it. The surprise is coherent because the fiction prepared the ground for it.

This is the Traceable criterion from the RAT checklist applied to oracle design specifically. A traceable oracle output is one that could not have been predicted from the player's intent but can be followed back to prior fictional events that made it possible. That combination — independent of intent, grounded in accumulated state — is what makes an oracle function as a second agent rather than as a resolution tool.

The practical implication for design: oracle systems that produce fictional events are building more useful material than oracle systems that produce binary answers. An oracle that outputs "yes, but the contact has already left the city" is introducing an independent fictional fact with consequences already present in the answer. The player did not generate that fact, and it connects to the accumulated fiction (there is a contact; the city has an established geography; timing exists in the fiction). An oracle that outputs "yes" and leaves the player to generate the complication has resolved the uncertainty while leaving the generation of independent material to the player. Both can work. The first is doing more of the second-agent work.

## The Internalization Problem Without Assistance

Essay 3 established that the transparency threshold is player-variable: the same design can sit above or below it depending on who is playing and how long they have been playing it. Internalization is the mechanism that moves a design from one position to the other over time, and internalization requires repetition.

In group play, an experienced player or referee can carry procedural weight while others are still building habits. The classification step that one player has internalized runs fast enough that other players never have to consciously perform it themselves. A new player at a table with an experienced referee is working inside a system that is already partially internalized on their behalf. The learning curve is shorter, in practice, than the design would require of a single player learning alone.

In solo play, every procedural step is the solo player's sole responsibility. There is no assistance during the learning phase. The internalization curve is the same length as for any single player working alone — which is, in most cases, longer than the curve for any individual player in a group, because group play distributes the learning. A solo player internalizing a move taxonomy or a tracking obligation is doing work that a group player partly shares.

This produces a specific design priority. Solo designs should front-load fast-internalizing rules: rules tightly coupled to actions the player is already performing, where the mechanical action and the fictional event are the same action or close to it. The 24XX defense mechanic internalizes quickly in solo play for the same reason it internalizes quickly in group play — the player practices it every time a hit occurs, which is throughout play — but the advantage is more significant in solo play because there is no alternative source of procedural support. Rules that require a separate classification step before the fiction can advance, or that activate rarely enough that the player never builds habitual responses to them, remain foregrounded longer in solo play than they would in group play.

Breathless is the case where this cost is most consequential, precisely because the majority of its derivatives are solo designs. The die degradation and reset mechanics internalize quickly: they are coupled to resolution, which is constant, and the reset declaration is a single clear action with an immediate fictional consequence. These elements are as solo-appropriate as any in this design neighborhood. The stress track is the element that does not have this property. It imposes an ongoing tracking obligation separate from the resolution action, and it stays somewhat foregrounded by design — the pressure engine experience depends on the player being aware of the stress count, which means the tracking is not supposed to recede into background infrastructure. That design choice carries a different cost for a solo player learning alone than it does for a group player with facilitation available. It does not make Breathless wrong as a source for solo adaptation. It makes the stress track the element most worth reconsidering, and most worth reconsidering first, when adapting toward a lower-threshold stable state.

## Accumulated Fiction with One Generative Agent

Essay 1 established that accumulated fiction — the causal tissue connecting prior events to current conditions — is what gives consequences weight and choices non-equivalence. In group play, multiple agents contribute to that accumulation. Different players establish different fictional facts from inside their characters' perspectives. The referee adds others from the world's side. The oracle, where present, adds others from outside any participant's intent. The accumulated state is richer and more unexpected than any single participant could produce alone.

In solo play, one agent generates all contributions to the accumulated fiction except what the oracle introduces.

This creates a structural asymmetry. The player's own contributions to the fiction are, by definition, expected — they are the player's choices, emerging from the player's model of the character and situation. The oracle's contributions are the only source of material the player did not generate and could not have fully anticipated. The causal tissue exists on both sides of this divide, but the tissue on the player-generated side carries less of the surprise that makes accumulated fiction productive. Prior decisions accumulate, but they accumulate as the player expected them to. What the oracle contributes accumulates differently: it adds facts the player had no part in generating, which means those facts can contradict the player's expectations, constrain the player's options in ways they did not foresee, and produce the sensation of the fiction going somewhere nobody planned.

The design implication is direct. Solo fiction engines should be built to maximize oracle contribution to accumulated state — not only to resolve individual uncertain outcomes but to introduce fictional facts that will remain in the fiction after the moment of their introduction. An oracle that produces an event generates a fact. That fact is now part of the accumulated fiction. Future oracle outputs can be constrained by it. The player's future actions are now responding to something they did not generate. The more the oracle contributes facts of this kind, the richer the unexpected side of the accumulated fiction becomes.

This is a different design priority than oracle quality as the framework defines it. The framework's criterion — does the oracle output read as a fictional event rather than a prompt? — addresses what the output does to the moment of play. The accumulated state criterion addresses what the output does to the fiction over time. Both matter. The second is the one solo design specifically needs to build toward.

## The Distinct Design Problem

The three structural differences named above — the feedback loop with one agent, the internalization curve without assistance, the accumulated fiction with one generative source — produce a specific design profile for solo fiction engines. Stated plainly:

A solo fiction engine requires an oracle that functions as a second agent in the feedback loop. This is not optional in the way that oracles are optional in group play, where a referee can fill the role. In solo play, if nothing produces outputs independent of the player's intent, the conditions for emergent fiction are absent. The oracle is load-bearing at the structural level, not as a convenience feature.

A solo fiction engine requires fast-internalizing rules at the entry phase, because there is no facilitation available to smooth the learning curve. The threshold is effectively lower not because cognitive load is undistributed — though it is — but because the feedback loop conditions are more fragile with one agent. A solo player spending significant attention on procedural management is a player not attending to the fiction and not attending to oracle outputs. Both of those are the materials the feedback loop needs. Procedural overhead in solo play is not just friction. It is reduction of the loop's operating conditions.

A solo fiction engine requires oracles designed to generate accumulated fictional facts, not only to resolve uncertainty in the moment. The player generates the player's contributions to the causal tissue. The oracle generates the unexpected side. A solo design that treats the oracle as a resolution tool rather than a generative agent is building a fiction that accumulates largely within the player's expectations. The causal tissue forms, but it does not surprise.

None of these is a revision of the fiction engine paradigm's core commitments. All three follow directly from applying the systems thinking arc's arguments to the structural conditions solo play creates. The feedback loop argument explains why the oracle is load-bearing. The internalization argument explains why the entry threshold must be lower. The accumulated state argument explains why oracle generativity is more important than the framework's existing oracle guidance addresses.

## What This Means for How the Oracle Is Positioned

The teaching implication from essay 3 was that a game introduced through its fiction — here is the situation, here is what your character might attempt, here is what happened — reaches its stable state faster than one introduced through its resolution procedure. The fiction is where the stable state lives; beginning there establishes the attentional habits the stable state requires.

In solo play, the fiction is where the oracle is. The oracle is not a procedural add-on consulted after the player has established their intent and framed their action. It is the entity the player is in dialogue with throughout play. The feedback loop runs between the player and the oracle, and that loop is the structural mechanism through which the fiction develops.

This suggests that a solo fiction engine introduced through its oracle — here is the situation, here is what you might ask, here is how the fiction responds — reaches its stable state faster than one introduced through its resolution mechanics. The resolution mechanics are infrastructure. The oracle is where the fiction lives in solo play. It should be where the design begins, both in the design document and at the table.

There is a practical consequence for how oracle systems are presented in solo designs. Many solo games present the oracle as one component among several: rules for resolution, rules for advancement, an oracle section, tables for scene generation. This organization treats the oracle as equivalent in weight to the other components. The structural argument suggests a different organization: the oracle is the primary mechanism, the one around which the other components are oriented, because it is the structural source of the conditions the paradigm requires. A designer who understands this can build toward it explicitly — can make the oracle the first thing the player encounters, the thing the design teaches before it teaches anything else.

## The Solo Fiction Engine as Its Own Object

The series has applied the fiction engine framework to group designs, to specific games within and adjacent to the paradigm, to edge cases at the paradigm's boundary, and now to the structural conditions solo play creates. Each application has revealed something about the paradigm that the framework alone does not say.

This application reveals that solo fiction engine design is its own design problem. Not a simplified version of group design. Not group design with one player and an oracle substituted for a referee. A different configuration with different structural conditions that require deliberate design decisions at the level of the oracle, the learning curve, and the accumulation of fictional state.

A designer adapting a group fiction engine for solo play is not reducing the design. They are changing its shape. The feedback loop that ran between agents must now run between one player and an oracle that is doing structural work the framework does not fully account for. The internalization curve that was smoothed by facilitation distribution must now be smoothed by the design itself. The accumulated fiction that was enriched by multiple agents' contributions must now be enriched by oracle generativity rather than group generation.

Each of these is a design decision, and each requires knowing what it is for. A solo oracle designed as a second agent produces different design decisions than a solo oracle designed as a resolution tool. The distinction is not about which tables are included or how questions are phrased. It is about what the oracle is being asked to do in the feedback loop, and whether the design is built to let it do that work.

The fiction engine framework offers a test for every rule: does this rule produce a new fictional state, support the conditions for fictional play, or generate dead overhead? In solo design, the oracle test is a version of this question applied to the mechanism that makes the paradigm's conditions possible for one player. Does this oracle produce independent fictional content that accumulates into the causal tissue? Does it close the feedback loop over material the player did not generate? Does it do this reliably enough to sustain the conditions for emergent play across a session, across sessions, across the arc of a campaign?

A solo oracle that passes those tests is doing what a second agent does. A solo fiction engine built around such an oracle is building the conditions the paradigm requires, under the structural conditions solo play creates. That is the design problem precisely stated. The rest is design.
