# Fiction Engine: A Chapter-by-Chapter Stylistic and Structural Guide

This document is a reference for writing and editing the book. It covers what each chapter needs to accomplish, how to structure it, and the tone and vocabulary choices that keep it consistent with the framework's voice. Two layers per chapter: **Structure** (what goes where, in what order) and **Style** (register, vocabulary, what to avoid).

---

## Chapter 1 — The Honest Introduction

**Goal**: Frame the book as a tool discovered through play, not invented at a desk. Establish scope and build trust before the reader commits to the framework.

### Structure

1. **Open with a concrete play memory.** A specific moment at the table — the rules either vanished into the background or jammed the scene to a halt. No definition of Fiction Engine yet. Let the scene do the work of making the problem visible.

2. **Name the pattern.** After the scene, observe: this kept happening. Across games, across groups, across years. Something about the rules was either feeding the story or stalling it. Still no jargon — just the observation that something was going on.

3. **Introduce "Fiction Engine" as the name you gave it.** One sentence, plain: "I started calling this the Fiction Engine." Don't define it yet. That comes in Chapter 2. Here, you're just naming the pattern.

4. **State what the book is.** A diagnostic tool for understanding what happens to your attention, your story, and your group during actual play. Not a prescription — a lens.

5. **State what the book is not — explicitly, without apology.** It is not a game design manual. It will not help you write a game. It is not a ranking system; nothing in it implies that games closer to the Fiction Engine ideal are better. It will not help you balance combat math, run a simulation, or evaluate whether a game is "good." These are real limits, and they go here, in the body, not in a footnote.

6. **Map the book.** One clause per chapter: what the reader will find there, what question each chapter answers. This is a table of contents that reads like a sentence, not a list of titles.

### Style

- First person is appropriate here and only here. "I kept noticing..." not "One observes..." The introduction earns a personal register because it's grounding the discovery in actual experience.
- No superlatives. The discovery was quiet and practical. "Revolutionary" or "transformative" would be false to the tone.
- The scope limitations are not a weakness to manage. State them at the same register as the book's strengths.
- End on an invitation, not a promise: "This is the lens I use. You'll decide if it fits."

## Chapter 2 — The Engine Under the Table

**Goal**: Define "Fiction Engine" through felt experience before introducing any formal vocabulary.

### Structure

1. **Open with a mechanical image that shows transparency as a sensory phenomenon.** The windshield metaphor from the example chapter works here or as a model: transparency is not an aesthetic quality — it's whether you're seeing through something or looking at it. The rules are either in the foreground of your attention or they've dissolved into the background. The reader should recognize the feeling before they have a word for it.

2. **Two tables, same challenge.** A door that won't open. A rival who appears in the marketplace. A moment of crisis. Show what each group of players actually does — where their eyes go, what they say, what they reach for. Not the rule text in each game: the behavior at the table. The contrast should be visible before you've named it.

3. **Annotate what just happened.** After the scenes, label the structure explicitly: "Story beat — Engine moment — Return to story." Show that both tables have all three stages; the difference is in what the engine moment costs and what it produces. This is the step where the analytical vocabulary earns its place — after the reader has already seen what it's describing.

4. **Name the key terms, anchored to the scenes.** Each term gets one defining sentence, followed immediately by a pointer back to the scenes already shown:
   - *Fictional state*: a condition inside the shared situation that characters can see, respond to, and reason about. Point to the moment in Scene 1 where the injury became a visible constraint. Point to the moment in Scene 2 where the number on the sheet didn't change anything in the room.
   - *Overhead*: procedural steps that produce neither a fictional change nor the conditions for one. Point to the scene moment where the players were talking to each other about the rules rather than through their characters about the situation.
   - *Transparency threshold*: the point where a rule shifts from background infrastructure to a simultaneous object of attention. Point to the moment one table stopped being in the scene.

5. **Close with the question the book will keep asking.** "What is this rule doing to your attention right now?" This is the lens. Every case study chapter will return to it.

### Style

- Define terms once, precisely, through examples — never before them. If you catch yourself writing a definition before you've shown the thing it names, reverse the order.
- "Overhead" means something narrower here than readers will expect. Say so once, plainly: "I'm using this word in a specific way."
- Preferred vocabulary: "what happens in the shared situation," "what characters can see and respond to," "the scene," "the table," "the group."
- Avoid: "narrative" (too literary), "diegetic" (too academic), "emergent" (too design-desk).
- Short, declarative sentences during the example scenes. The prose slows down and qualifies when stepping back to label.

## Chapter 3 — Attention, Pacing, and the Group

**Goal**: Translate the abstract concepts from Chapter 2 into the lived experience of a session — where player attention goes, how rules shape pacing, and how the group distributes cognitive load.

### Structure

1. **Start with the question: where does your attention go during play?** Walk through what a player is actually doing moment to moment: tracking what's happening in the shared situation, decoding what the rules say to do, negotiating silently with the group about whose turn it is to drive, imagining what might happen next. None of this is abstract — it's the texture of play.

2. **Introduce cognitive load as a felt phenomenon.** Not a psychology concept — a recognition. The moment the rulebook becomes a third participant at the table. The moment two players start arguing about whether the rule applies rather than about what the characters do. That's the feeling. Name it as cognitive load after showing it, not before.

3. **Show how different engines distribute attention.** Not a ranking — a map. Some engines ask players to track the fiction and almost nothing else. Some ask them to track the fiction and a small set of mechanical states simultaneously. Some ask them to periodically step out of the fiction to run a procedure and step back in. These are different attentional patterns, not different qualities of play.

4. **Group dynamics.** Load distributes differently across a table. The GM carries something the players don't; the players carry something the GM doesn't. A solo player carries all of it — this is worth naming as a distinct case, not a variant of group play. Don't fold it into a footnote.

5. **Pacing: two short vignettes.** One scene where the engine stalls the fiction — the group's momentum visibly breaks as they move from scene to procedure and struggle to return. One scene where the engine accelerates the fiction — the resolution happens fast and the situation immediately becomes more interesting. Both vignettes are neutral: one isn't a failure, the other isn't a success. They're examples of how attentional cost plays out in time.

### Style

- "Cognitive load" is acceptable because it names something every player has felt. Introduce it as the name for a familiar feeling, not as an imported theoretical term.
- The solo play note is brief but explicit — two or three sentences. Don't make it a sidebar or a footnote.
- "Fast" and "deliberate" are neutral descriptors. "Efficient" is a value judgment — avoid it.

## Chapter 4 — 24XX: The Minimal Engine

**Goal**: Show what a Fiction Engine looks like when it's been stripped to its operational minimum — what that costs and what it enables at the table.

### Structure

1. **Describe sitting down to run 24XX for the first time.** Sensory and behavioral, not evaluative. The minimal text. The open space where a bigger game would have a procedure. The sensation, before first roll, that the rules are going to stay out of the way. Maybe they will. Maybe the group will feel the absence.

2. **A session moment.** A player declares an action. A die hits the table. A result comes up. Show what happens next: the GM's move, the fictional consequence, the scene continuing. Annotate the engine steps after the scene — what was fiction, what was engine, what was the return.

3. **Name the design commitment.** Minimum overhead, maximum fictional continuity. What this means in practice: the GM is not consulting tables, the players are not tallying modifiers, the scene doesn't pause to run a procedure. But this means the fiction has to carry the weight. The shared situation has to be rich enough that results always land somewhere interesting.

4. **The cost, with full weight.** Sparse scaffolding is the direct consequence of minimum overhead. When the fiction runs thin — when the group can't generate interesting situations from a low roll — there's nothing structural to fall back on. The engine doesn't offer a procedure to bridge the gap. The group carries that weight. This paragraph gets the same space as the paragraph naming the benefits.

5. **Two behavioral portraits.** Who this engine serves well: "If your group generates fiction spontaneously — if a low result immediately prompts someone at the table to ask what it costs and the answer is already interesting — this engine stays out of the way and the session moves fast." Who might find it disorienting: "If your group needs the rules to propose situations — if a low result produces silence rather than a question — the absence of scaffolding can feel like an empty room."

### Style

- Never say "rules-light." That label describes quantity, not function. Use function: what the rules do, what they don't do, what that means for the group at the table.
- The cost paragraph carries as much weight as the praise paragraph. Give them equal space and equal directness.
- Present tense for session descriptions: "You roll the dice. The result is low. The GM asks what it costs you." This keeps the analysis immediate and behavioral rather than retrospective.

## Chapter 5 — Breathless: The Engine Under Pressure

**Goal**: Show how resource depletion functions as fictional information — and what it feels like when that engine is working.

### Structure

1. **Open mid-scene.** A character's stats have degraded. Don't explain Breathless first. Show what a session moment feels like at that point: the player looking at a stat that started high and is now down to a d4, the GM describing the situation, the decision about whether to push. The reader is inside the scene before the analysis begins.

2. **Introduce the engine's logic.** Depletion is fictional information. The degraded die isn't just a mechanical penalty — it's a condition inside the shared situation. Something has happened to the character that everyone at the table can see and respond to. Show this with a before/after: the same character early in the session (d10) and late (d4), and how the GM frames the same type of obstacle differently because the fictional information has changed.

3. **The feel of running Breathless.** Urgency as mechanical pressure that translates into fictional stakes. What the GM is tracking — not just who has what die, but what the degradation map says about where the fiction is heading. What the players are tracking — not just their own sheets, but the whole table's state, which is the group's shared picture of how much runway is left.

4. **The point of friction.** When does the pressure enhance the fiction and when does it tip into bookkeeping? Show both. A scene where the degradation is doing fictional work — where the low die is a visible condition that makes the next choice feel real. Then a scene where the tracking has become administrative — where players are consulting sheets rather than inhabiting the situation.

5. **Honest note.** This engine assumes the group buys into urgency as a fictional premise. If the group reads the stat block as just hit points — if the d4 is "I'm almost out" rather than "I'm barely holding together" — the engine loses its leverage. The numbers need to be fictional states, not administrative overhead. The group has to make that choice to make the engine work.

### Style

- The opening scene should be tense. Short sentences. Present tense. Drop the reader in without preamble.
- "Resource depletion" is acceptable terminology — follow it immediately with what it feels like, not what it means structurally.
- Don't summarize Breathless' rules. Assume the reader can read the game. Show how the rules behave at the table.

## Chapter 6 — Freeform Universal: The Flexible Engine

**Goal**: Show what "freeform" actually means at the table — and where flexibility shades into ambiguity.

### Structure

1. **Open with the appeal.** An engine that shapes itself to the fiction rather than imposing a shape. What that promise feels like before play begins — the sense that this tool will serve whatever story the group wants to tell, that no situation will be outside its scope. That appeal is real. It's also not the complete picture.

2. **A session moment showing narrative authority in motion.** Who proposes the action? Who frames the consequence? How does the result (yes-and, yes-but, no-but, no-and) get translated into the shared situation? Annotate: who held what authority, at which moment, and how the outcome moved the fiction forward rather than pausing it.

3. **The engine's actual mechanism.** FU's yes/no/and/but structure as a tool for fictional continuation. Every result forces the situation to change. A result never suspends the fiction — it always produces a new condition that everyone at the table can see and respond to. This is the design commitment, and it's a strong one. Name it.

4. **Where it gets complicated.** Without structural scaffolding from the rules — without a procedure that proposes situations when the group doesn't — the group must supply the fiction. This is a feature: a highly generative group will find this engine invisible. But it's also a real cost: when the group's fictional output runs thin, there's nothing in the rules to bridge the gap. Show a moment where this cost becomes visible — a session where a result lands and the table goes quiet because no one knows what the "yes, but" means.

5. **Comparison to 24XX and Breathless — one sentence each, no ranking.** What FU does that they don't: it makes no assumptions about the fictional domain; it will work for any setting or situation. What 24XX does that FU doesn't: it bundles the engine into a setting-specific frame that gives the group a fictional starting point. What Breathless does that FU doesn't: it builds urgency into the mechanical structure; the pressure is always there whether or not the group generates it. The comparison is a map, not a verdict.

### Style

- "Freeform" must be defined in behavioral terms: "the rules don't specify which situations call for a roll, so the group must agree." That's the real meaning. Don't let the word carry its own vague implication.
- The authority question — who has narrative authority, when, and over what — is the central one for this engine. It should be addressed directly, not gestured at.
- The three-game comparison at the end uses parallel structure. One sentence per game. No evaluative adjectives.

## Chapter 7 — The Limits of the Lens

**Goal**: State openly where the framework ends and what it leaves unaddressed. Build trust by not overclaiming.

### Structure

1. **Return to the book's central question.** "What is this rule doing to your attention right now?" Name, with equal directness, what that question cannot answer.

2. **List what the framework does not cover.** These are real limits, not hedges:
   - Tactical or mathematical balance — whether a game's numbers produce fair outcomes
   - Simulation fidelity — whether a game's procedures accurately model a physical or historical reality
   - Competitive or adversarial play structures — games where players have opposing objectives and information asymmetry
   - Whether a game is "good" — this framework has no verdict on quality, only on how a particular design handles the relationship between procedure and fiction

3. **Show a moment where the framework reaches its edge.** Take an Open Strategy Game or a highly procedural simulation. Run the analysis. Watch the vocabulary run dry. The friction isn't a flaw in the framework — it's the edge of its scope. Say so.

4. **Invitation to use this as one lens among several.** Other lenses exist for the questions this one can't answer. This book gives you one instrument. You'll need others.

5. **Close with the original question.** The next time you sit down to play, notice where your attention goes. Notice when the rules recede and when they step forward. That noticing is all the framework asks. The last paragraph is quiet, not rallying. The book earns its ending by not overselling what it delivered.

### Style

- No apology for the limits. State them in the same register as the rest of the book — precise, plain, direct.
- Don't introduce new terminology here. The final chapter uses only what the book has already established.
- The closing paragraph is short. One moment of recognition, then done.

## Global Style Rules

These apply throughout all chapters.

| Do | Avoid |
|----|-------|
| Define terms through examples before naming them | Defining before showing |
| State costs alongside benefits, with equal weight | Praise without reckoning |
| Use present tense in session scenes and vignettes | Past tense for play moments (makes them distant) |
| Short sentences during action; longer when stepping back to label | Uniform sentence length throughout |
| "Fictional state," "overhead," "transparency threshold," "shared situation" | "Narrative," "diegetic," "emergent," "systemic" |
| "Neither better — different purposes" | Any implicit ranking |
| Qualify claims in body text | Caveats buried in footnotes |
| Name the framework's limits plainly, without apology | Treating scope as a weakness to manage |
| Use present tense for session descriptions: "You roll. The result is low." | "The player rolled" |

### Vocabulary consistency

The following words have precise meanings in this book. Use them consistently and don't substitute synonyms where the precise term applies:

- **Fiction Engine** — the design family defined by the structural commitment that every rule serves the fiction. Always capitalized. Never "fiction engine" or "the engine" alone (except as shorthand after the full term has been established in a chapter).
- **Fictional state** — a condition inside the shared situation that characters can observe and respond to. Not synonymous with "mechanic" or "stat."
- **Overhead** — procedural steps that produce neither a fictional change nor the conditions for one. Narrower than "mechanics."
- **Transparency threshold** — the attentional boundary where a rule shifts from background to foreground.
- **Shared situation** — what is happening in the fiction that everyone at the table can see and respond to. Preferred over "the narrative" or "the story."

### The player-chair test

Before each section, ask: is this written from the player's chair or the designer's desk? If the passage analyzes how a designer achieved an effect, revise it to analyze how a player experiences that effect during play. The question is not "how was this designed?" — it's "what does this do to my attention and my group's story when we're in the middle of a session?"
