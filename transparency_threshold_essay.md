# The Transparency Threshold
## Abstraction, Mediation, and the Limits of Fiction Engine Design

*A companion essay to "Fiction Engine: A Design Framework for Tabletop RPGs." Also readable as a standalone piece following "Fiction Engines, Pressure Engines, and World Engines."*

---

The fiction engine framework makes a deliberate choice not to place specific games on a map. It describes a paradigm -- its commitments, its failure modes, its criteria -- without naming which systems belong inside it and which do not. That restraint is principled. A framework that generates taxonomy risks becoming a ranking, and a ranking is not what the framework is trying to produce.

But the framework does raise a question it does not answer: how much abstraction can exist in a design before the system stops being fiction-transparent? The framework says that a rule can be non-diegetic in form while still being fiction-supporting in function. It says that the distinction between dead overhead and load-bearing infrastructure is one of function rather than form. What it does not say is where the boundary lies -- at what point the accumulation of non-diegetic elements, however individually justifiable, crosses into a different kind of design.

This essay is an attempt to answer that question. It is comparative in ways the framework is not, and it names specific paradigms and their characteristic behaviors. The goal is not to rank those paradigms but to locate the boundary more precisely -- to describe what happens when a design crosses it, and why that matters for both designers and players.

---

## Two Kinds of Abstraction

Not all abstraction works the same way in play, and the distinction matters before anything else can be said clearly.

The first kind is abstraction of form. A hit point total is an abstraction of form: it represents something that exists inside the fiction -- a character's physical capacity to keep functioning -- in a notation that exists outside the fiction. The character's condition is diegetic. The number that tracks it is not. But in many contexts this abstraction stays invisible during play. The player does not think "my character has 14 hit points." They think "she is badly hurt and one more hit might finish her." The number is infrastructure for that thought, not an object of attention in itself.

The second kind is abstraction of procedure. A move trigger in a Powered by the Apocalypse game is an abstraction of procedure: it takes fictional action and classifies it into a predefined taxonomic category before the fiction can advance. When a player "acts under fire" or "goes aggro," they are not simply describing what their character does. They are identifying which procedural category that action falls into, and that identification is a step the fiction must pass through before producing an outcome. The procedure is not notation for a diegetic thing. It is a required translation layer between fictional intent and fictional consequence.

These two kinds of abstraction are different in kind, not just degree. Abstraction of form can operate in the background of play -- it does its bookkeeping without demanding the player's attention. Abstraction of procedure cannot stay in the background: the player must engage it consciously to move play forward. This distinction is where the transparency threshold begins.

---

## The Threshold Defined

A system crosses the transparency threshold when the player's attention must shift from the fiction to the procedure in order to advance play.

This is not the same as being aware of mechanics. Every player of every game is aware that mechanics exist. It is not the same as engaging with rules: players engage with rules constantly without those rules becoming objects of attention in themselves. The threshold is specifically attentional and cognitive: it marks the point where the player's primary focus must redirect from the fiction -- the situation, the characters, the conditions inside the diegetic layer -- to the system -- the classifications, the resource states, the procedural steps that govern what the system will accept as valid input.

Below the threshold, procedure operates as background infrastructure. The player stays inside the fiction and the rules respond. The rules are the pipes; the fiction is what flows through them.

Above the threshold, procedure moves to the foreground. The player must engage the system as an object in order to produce any fictional output at all. The fiction and the system are both objects of attention, and the player must manage their relationship.

Neither position is wrong. They produce different kinds of play. But they are genuinely different, and the difference is not simply about how many rules a game has.

## Where Paradigms Sit

With the threshold defined, it becomes possible to place different design paradigms relative to it -- not as a ranking but as a map of where each paradigm's primary attention demands fall.

*A note on what follows: if you are arriving at this essay through the Zotiquest newsletter article "Fiction Engines, Pressure Engines, and World Engines," the paradigm descriptions below will be familiar territory. Read them as anchors for the threshold argument rather than as new material -- the placement of each paradigm relative to the threshold is the point, not the paradigm descriptions themselves. If you are arriving through the design framework, what follows is a brief map of the design landscape the framework sits inside.*

**FU and Loner** sit clearly below the threshold. The procedure is: describe what you attempt, roll, interpret the result as a fictional outcome, continue. There is no classification step. There is no taxonomy of action types. The resolution procedure is thin enough to become habitual almost immediately, which means it operates in the background after very little play. The player's attention stays on the fiction because the rules do not require it elsewhere.

**PbtA** sits at the threshold, and this is worth being precise about. PbtA games are fiction-first in their input structure: the fiction triggers mechanics rather than mechanics triggering fiction. That is a genuine and important design commitment. But the move structure introduces a procedural foreground at the moment of resolution. The player must identify which move applies -- or whether any move applies -- before the fiction can advance. That identification is a classification step, and it requires the player to hold the system's taxonomy in mind while reading the fiction. Experienced PbtA players internalize this quickly and the step becomes nearly invisible. But it is structurally always there, which is why PbtA sits at the threshold rather than clearly below it. Fiction-first in input. Procedurally mediated in output.

**FitD** sits above the threshold by design, and Blades in the Dark is the clearest case. Position and effect judgments, stress economy, resistance rolls, clock management -- these are not background infrastructure. They are persistent objects of attention throughout play. The system is designed to make risk legible, costly, and strategically interesting, and it achieves this by keeping the procedure visible. A player running Blades well is simultaneously inside the fiction and actively managing a pressure economy. That dual attention is not a flaw. It is the experience the game is designed to produce. But it means Blades is not attempting fiction-transparency and should not be evaluated as if it were.

**Fate** sits above the threshold more completely than FitD, because in Fate the procedure is not just visible but explicitly the object of authorial attention. Aspects and fate points are the primary design surface. Invoking an aspect is not a background action: it is a deliberate act of narrative leverage, a conscious claim about the fictional world's relevance to a mechanical outcome. Fate externalizes dramatic control and makes authorship visible. This is internally coherent and serves players who want explicit authorial agency over their story's shape. But the fiction does not flow through Fate's mechanics -- it flows through its authors' decisions about when and how to engage those mechanics.

**OSR** presents an interesting case because it often sits below the threshold without being a fiction engine in the sense the framework describes. OSR games typically have thin procedures in service of world interaction and player problem-solving rather than fictional flow. The mechanics -- reaction rolls, resource checks, save-or-die -- are often background infrastructure. But the primary object of attention is the world as a puzzle and a danger, not the fiction as a mutable situation. OSR and fiction engine designs share a value for procedural thinness without sharing a goal for what that thinness is supposed to serve.

## The Player-Variable Threshold

The threshold is not fixed, and this is where the analysis becomes most interesting.

The same game can sit on different sides of the threshold depending on who is playing it and how long they have been playing it. A player encountering PbtA move structure for the first time experiences it as foreground: they are actively consulting the move list, identifying categories, checking trigger conditions. A player who has run Apocalypse World for two years has internalized that structure to the point where it operates nearly invisibly. The move identification step has become habitual, and habitual steps do not demand conscious attention in the same way that novel steps do.

This means the transparency threshold is partly a function of familiarity, and that has real consequences.

The first consequence is for game design. A game that places a high attentional demand on new players but a low demand on experienced ones has a significant learning threshold rather than a permanent transparency problem. Many PbtA games are like this: the procedural foreground is most visible during the first few sessions and fades as the move structure is internalized. This is a design choice, and it is worth naming as such. The learning cost is real, but it is amortized over time.

The second consequence is for how games are introduced. If the transparency threshold is player-variable, then the experience of a game depends heavily on how it is taught. A game introduced through its fiction -- here is the situation, here is what your character might attempt, here is what happened -- will feel different at the table than the same game introduced through its mechanics -- here are the six stats, here are the move categories, here are the roll triggers. Both approaches eventually arrive at the same system, but they cross the threshold in opposite directions. The first approach keeps the fiction primary and allows the mechanics to emerge from play. The second approach foregrounds the procedure from the start and asks the fiction to fill in around it.

This has a practical implication that goes beyond advice about running games. It suggests that the transparency threshold is not just a property of the design but a property of the design-in-use. A fiction engine that is poorly introduced can feel procedurally heavy. A PbtA game that is masterfully run can feel nearly frictionless. The threshold is real, but it is not solely determined by the rules.

The third consequence is for solo play specifically, and it is one the framework document acknowledges indirectly. A solo player has no external facilitator to absorb procedural weight. In group play, a skilled referee or experienced player can smooth the attentional transitions -- can hold the taxonomy in mind so others do not have to, can manage the pressure economy in the background while other players stay in the fiction. In solo play, every procedural step is the player's responsibility, which means the threshold is effectively lower for solo design. A procedural foreground that a group can distribute becomes a full cognitive load for one person. This is one reason why fiction engine designs, with their thin procedural layers, are particularly well-suited to solo play: they minimize the load that the solo player has to carry alone.

## Load-Bearing Abstraction and Its Limits

The framework distinguishes dead overhead from fiction-supporting infrastructure, and that distinction does real analytical work. But the essay needs to press it further, because the category of fiction-supporting infrastructure can expand to absorb almost anything if it is not bounded.

Consider Blades in the Dark clocks. A clock is a non-diegetic element: it is a pie chart divided into segments that tracks progress toward an event. It is not something characters can see or touch inside the fiction. But clocks reliably produce genuine fictional pressure. When the clock for the city watch response fills another segment, something changes inside the fiction even if no character has noticed it yet. The clock is doing fictional work while remaining non-diegetic in form. By the framework's definition of fiction-supporting infrastructure, it qualifies.

But clocks in Blades are also persistent objects of attention. A player managing multiple clocks simultaneously is doing real cognitive work that exists outside the fiction -- tracking segment counts, estimating trigger distances, evaluating which clocks to prioritize. That cognitive work pushes the design above the threshold even though the clocks are producing fictional output. The abstraction is load-bearing and it is also foregrounded.

This reveals the limit of the fiction-supporting infrastructure category: a rule can be simultaneously fiction-supporting in function and procedurally foregrounded in use. These are not mutually exclusive. The distinction between dead overhead and load-bearing infrastructure tells you whether a rule is worth having. The transparency threshold tells you what kind of experience it produces while you have it.

Both questions matter. The fiction engine framework answers the first. The threshold answers the second. A design that sits below the threshold will tend to contain only background infrastructure -- mechanics that do their work without demanding conscious attention. A design that sits above the threshold may contain deeply fiction-supporting mechanics that nevertheless require active management. Blades is a system full of the latter. It is not dead overhead. It is high-cost fiction-supporting infrastructure, and whether that cost is worth paying is a question each designer and each table answers for themselves.

## What Lives Above the Threshold

The essay would be dishonest if it implied that fiction-transparency is the only legitimate design goal, or that everything above the threshold is a lesser achievement.

Foregrounded procedure produces experiences that a fiction engine cannot. Blades in the Dark generates a specific kind of tension -- the tension of managed risk, of calibrated expenditure, of strategic decision-making under pressure -- that emerges precisely because the pressure economy is visible and actively managed. If the stress track became invisible infrastructure, the experience of spending stress would lose its weight. The foreground is the point.

PbtA's move structure produces dramatic rhythm. The moment of move identification is the moment where the fiction is sorted into meaning -- where the game says: this is the kind of thing that is happening, and here is what that kind of thing costs and produces. That sorting is a form of interpretation, and the game performs it structurally rather than leaving it entirely to the table. For players who want consistent dramatic coherence, that structure is not overhead. It is the engine.

Fate produces authorial satisfaction. The explicit negotiation over aspects and fate points is a form of collaborative dramatic authorship that cannot happen without the metacurrency layer. Removing it in favor of thin mediation would not produce a better Fate. It would produce a different game that no longer does what Fate is trying to do.

These are genuine design achievements. The transparency threshold marks a boundary, not a hierarchy. Below the threshold, fiction engines produce a specific kind of play: responsive, situational, lightly framed, with the mechanics receding into the background and the fiction remaining primary. Above the threshold, other paradigms produce other kinds of play: structured, rhythmic, pressurized, explicitly authored. Neither set of properties is superior. They serve different aims, appeal to different players, and reward different kinds of engagement.

What matters is knowing which side of the threshold your design inhabits, and inhabiting it deliberately.

---

## The Threshold as a Design Decision

The most common way to cross the transparency threshold is not through a deliberate design choice but through accumulation. Each individual rule seems justified. Each abstraction earns its place in isolation. The move trigger provides clarity. The stress track provides pacing. The clock provides tension. The metacurrency provides authorial control. None of these additions is obviously wrong. But together they shift the table's primary attention from the fiction to the system, and at some point the design has crossed the threshold without anyone having decided to cross it.

This is the design failure that the fiction engine framework's overhead accumulation failure mode is pointing at. But overhead accumulation as named in the framework refers to dead overhead -- rules that produce no fictional output. The more insidious version is fiction-supporting overhead accumulation: rules that are each producing genuine fictional value while collectively driving the design above the threshold.

The diagnostic for this is not "is each rule load-bearing?" -- they may all be load-bearing. It is "what is the combined attentional cost of all the load-bearing rules together, and is that cost consistent with the kind of play this design is trying to produce?" A design aiming for fiction-transparency cannot answer that question rule by rule. It has to answer it for the system as a whole.

Conversely, a design that deliberately aims to sit above the threshold should own that position fully rather than claiming fiction-first virtue while building procedural foreground into every resolution step. The most coherent designs in any paradigm are the ones that know what they are and build toward it without apology.

The transparency threshold is not a test that designs pass or fail. It is a location on a continuum, and knowing where your design sits -- and why -- is the beginning of designing deliberately rather than by accumulation.
