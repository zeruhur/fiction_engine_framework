# Glossary

Abstraction of form
: A mechanical notation for a condition that exists inside the fiction. Hit point totals, wound tracks, and exhaustion counters are abstractions of form: the character's physical state is diegetic; the number that tracks it is not. Abstractions of form can operate in the background of play — the player thinks "she is badly hurt" rather than "she has 4 hit points" — which means they can carry their weight without demanding conscious attention. Contrast with abstraction of procedure.

Abstraction of procedure
: A required classification step between fictional intent and fictional outcome. When a player must identify which move applies, which category an action falls into, or which subsystem governs the current situation before the fiction can advance, that step is an abstraction of procedure. Unlike abstraction of form, it cannot operate fully in the background: it requires conscious engagement to complete. This is the mechanism by which designs cross the transparency threshold.

Accumulated fiction
: A fiction in which prior events constrain current possibility in structural terms. In accumulated fiction, a burned bridge cannot be treated as intact; an alliance formed under duress carries that duress into every subsequent interaction; a public failure has established a reputation that shapes what other characters will plausibly do. Each outcome adds to a causal tissue that narrows and differentiates the possibility space for what can follow. Contrast with episodic fiction.

Agent
: A participant in the play system who takes inputs and produces outputs: a player describing what their character attempts, a referee describing the world's response, an oracle answering a question about the fiction's state. Each agent has some model of the fiction's current state and acts on that model to produce the next state. The feedback loops that enable emergent fiction run between agents.

Authority drift
: The failure mode in which a narrative authority mechanism — a referee, an oracle, a prompt system — shifts from producing fictional events toward producing administrative prompts that require significant interpretive work before the fiction can advance. An oracle output that reads as a task ("find a way to resolve the tension") rather than an event ("the contact is already gone when you arrive") exemplifies authority drift. The mechanism has become an obstacle between fictional intent and fictional change rather than a conduit for it.

Causal tissue
: The network of established fictional events, conditions, and facts such that each element is both a consequence of what preceded it and a constraint on what can plausibly follow. Causal tissue is what gives fictional consequences weight, makes choices non-equivalent, and allows surprises to emerge from within the fiction rather than arriving from outside it. The fiction engine paradigm's procedural commitments — outcomes as continuations, fictional states over administrative records, the prohibition on dead overhead — are, in significant part, commitments to maintaining the causal tissue that accumulated fiction requires.

Dead overhead
: Procedural steps, tracking obligations, or resource management layers that neither produce a change to the fiction nor support the conditions under which fictional change can occur. The distinction between dead overhead and fiction-supporting infrastructure is one of function rather than form. A rule is dead overhead when removing it leaves the fiction moving and coherent. A rule is load-bearing when removing it causes play to stall or lose legibility. Dead overhead is the target for removal; fiction-supporting infrastructure is not.

Diegetic
: Belonging to the fiction — the layer of characters, places, events, and conditions that all participants hold in common and treat as real within the game. A character's wound, a crumbling building, a political alliance: these are diegetic elements. They exist inside the fiction. Hit point totals, fate points, and stress tracks are non-diegetic: they exist outside the fiction as part of the game's administrative layer. Fiction engines aim to keep as much as possible diegetic without treating non-diegetic elements as automatically invalid.

Emergence / emergent fiction
: A property of the play system in which the fiction produced by a session's end could not have been predicted from the elements at its start, and in which the unpredictable development is felt as arising from within the situation rather than being imposed from outside it. Emergence is not a mystical property. It is a structural consequence of feedback loops between agents sensitive to each other's outputs, closing over accumulated fictional state, with a procedural layer thin enough not to interrupt them. When those conditions are present, emergence is possible. When any of them is absent, it is not.

Entry state
: The position a design occupies relative to the transparency threshold on the first session of play, before any internalization has occurred. The entry state is one component of a design's trajectory. A design whose entry state is significantly above the transparency threshold imposes real attentional costs on new players regardless of how clean its stable state is. Better documentation can reduce confusion about unfamiliar rules; it cannot reduce the attentional cost of rules that require conscious engagement to operate.

Episodic fiction
: A fiction in which each session, scene, or significant outcome begins from a roughly equivalent state. Prior events are acknowledged in tone — players know what happened, characters reference it — but they do not constrain current possibility in structural terms. Episodic and accumulated fiction are not a hierarchy. They are different architectural choices, each optimized for different things. A one-shot is defined by deliberate episodic structure. A long campaign that fails to accumulate fictional weight across sessions is producing episodic fiction unintentionally.

Facilitation distribution
: The absorption of procedural weight by participants other than the player in question. In group play, an experienced referee or player who has internalized a procedure can carry the classification step, hold the taxonomy in mind, or manage the pressure economy in the background, allowing other players to stay oriented toward the fiction. No facilitation distribution is available in solo play: every procedural step is the solo player's sole responsibility. This is the structural reason the transparency threshold is effectively lower for solo design.

Feedback loop
: A cycle in the play system in which one agent's output becomes another agent's input, and the second agent's output in turn becomes input for the first. In play terms: a character acts, the world responds, the character responds to the response. Feedback loops enable emergent fiction when they close over accumulated fictional state and when the agents involved are genuinely sensitive to each other's outputs rather than pursuing fixed agendas. Thick procedural mediation interrupts feedback loops at the classification step; thin mediation preserves them.

Fiction
: The shared diegetic situation: the characters, places, events, and conditions that all participants hold in common during play and treat as real within the game. Fiction is not narrative craft, literary quality, or storytelling in the writerly sense. It is the living, mutable situation that play produces and transforms moment to moment. When this book says a rule should serve the fiction, it means the rule should produce changes to the fiction rather than changes to a score, a resource pool, or an administrative record that exists outside it.

Fiction engine
: A design whose every rule either produces fictional change, supports the conditions under which fictional change can occur, or is a candidate for removal. The commitment is structural: it applies to every design decision, and the test applies to each rule individually and to the system as a whole. The fiction engine paradigm values thin mediation, fictional momentum, and minimal procedural overhead. It accepts, as a consequence, that it provides less structural scaffolding than paradigms built around dramatic prescription or managed pressure.

Fiction-supporting infrastructure
: Non-diegetic rules that preserve the coherence, legibility, or continuity that makes fictional change possible. Safety tools, pacing mechanisms, campaign logs, and certain tracking systems may be non-diegetic in form while still being load-bearing in function. The distinction between fiction-supporting infrastructure and dead overhead is one of function: does removing this rule cause the fiction to stall or lose coherence? If yes, the rule is infrastructure. The category can expand to absorb almost anything if left unbounded, which is why the combined attentional cost of all load-bearing rules must also be evaluated, not only each rule in isolation.

Fictional intent
: What a player's character is attempting within the fiction. Fictional intent is the input to any resolution procedure: the player describes what they want to do inside the fiction, and the game produces a fictional outcome in response. The procedural layer between fictional intent and fictional outcome should be as thin as the situation allows.

Fictional state
: A condition that exists inside the fiction and is visible to the characters within it. A fictional state is a property of the world, not a property of the game record. The test: remove the game record and ask whether the condition still exists inside the fiction as something participants can see, respond to, and reason about. Fictional states survive that removal. Administrative records do not.

Internalization
: The process by which a procedure that initially requires conscious attention becomes habitual through repetition, and its attentional cost drops. A classification step that demands deliberate engagement during the first sessions of play may operate nearly invisibly after extended exposure. Internalization is the mechanism by which a design that begins above the transparency threshold can arrive at a below-threshold position in practice. The speed of internalization varies by rule: rules tightly coupled to actions players are already performing internalize quickly; rules that activate rarely accumulate fewer repetitions and internalize slowly.

Learning curve
: The middle component of a design's trajectory — the span of play across which procedures move from the attentional foreground toward the background through internalization. The length and steepness of the learning curve are design properties with real consequences, not documentation problems. A design with a long learning curve that provides no support for completing it loses players before they reach the stable state, regardless of how good that stable state is.

Metacurrency
: A resource that exists outside the fiction as a mechanical buffer, earned and spent by players to modify outcomes or shape narrative. Fate points, action points, and similar tokens are metacurrencies. A metacurrency is not automatically dead overhead: some metacurrency systems are fiction-supported in their generation, fiction-oriented in their effect, and kept minimal enough that they operate in the background. Whether a given metacurrency belongs in a fiction engine depends on its combined administrative cost and the degree to which it shifts the player's attention from the fiction to the resource pool.

Oracle
: A mechanism for distributing narrative authority in solo or low-referee play. An oracle typically takes a closed question about the fiction — is the guard suspicious? does the contact show up? — and produces an answer, often with gradations or complications. It functions as a fiction engine component when its outputs read as fictional events rather than prompts requiring interpretive work before play can continue. In solo play specifically, the oracle functions as a second agent in the feedback loop: it introduces outputs the solo player did not generate, which is the structural condition for emergent fiction when only one person is playing. An oracle that merely resolves uncertainty without generating independent fictional facts is a weaker second agent than one whose outputs add to the accumulated causal tissue.

Orientational divergence
: The failure mode in which participants hold different implicit models of what the game is doing — one treating it as a tactical puzzle, another as character exploration, another as improvised theater — and the engine loses coherence as a result, independently of whether the rules are functioning correctly. The fiction fragments because participants are not holding it in common. Orientational divergence is a failure of the social agreement that underlies any game. Design can reduce the risk by making the game's orientation legible from the first page; it cannot eliminate the risk by rule.

Overhead accumulation
: The failure mode in which rules individually justifiable each impose small attentional costs that collectively shift the table's attention from the fiction to the system. The engine stalls not through a single bad rule but through the accumulated weight of many marginal ones. A more insidious variant is fiction-supporting overhead accumulation: rules that are each producing real fictional value while collectively driving the design above the transparency threshold. The combined attentional cost of all load-bearing rules must be evaluated as a system, not only rule by rule.

Pressure engine
: A design paradigm in which a managed resource economy — stress, momentum, heat, capacity — creates tension through the strategic visibility of its states and the cost of engaging with the fiction under pressure. A pressure engine sits above the transparency threshold by design: the resource economy is not background infrastructure but a persistent object of attention whose visible management is the experience the design is producing. Breathless is the clearest example in this book's comparative analysis: fiction engine DNA in its resolution core, pressure engine structure in its die degradation and reset economy.

RAT checklist
: A three-part test for evaluating referee outcomes, developed in the context of open strategy game design and applicable to fiction engine adjudication generally. Reasonable: is this outcome proportionate to what the fiction has established? Actionable: does this outcome advance the fiction rather than suspend it, leaving at least one participant something to respond to? Traceable: can you point to prior fictional events that led here — does the outcome feel like a consequence rather than a contrivance? An outcome that fails any test should be rewritten before play continues. The Traceable criterion is the element not captured by the fiction engine framework's existing guidance on outcomes; it is what distinguishes a fiction that accumulates from one that resets.

Referee
: The participant whose role is to mediate between the fiction and the other players: describing the world's response to player action, adjudicating outcomes, and maintaining the consistency of the fiction. Some games distribute this role across all participants; others eliminate it in favor of procedural tools such as oracles. In both configurations, the principle is the same: the authority mechanism must produce fictional output rather than administrative prompts.

Second agent
: In solo play, the structural role an oracle or equivalent mechanism must fill to enable emergent fiction. A solo player cannot form a genuine feedback loop with themselves: without an independent source of fictional content, the loop closes over the player's own intent and emergence of the relevant kind is not possible. An oracle functions as a second agent when its outputs are independent of the player's intent and constrained by the accumulated fictional state. Both conditions are necessary. An oracle too responsive to player intent is confirming rather than contributing. An oracle disconnected from the accumulated fictional state generates noise rather than surprise.

Sensitivity
: The property by which an agent's output is shaped by the specific prior outputs of other agents, not merely by the agent's own goals or intentions. A player who adjusts what their character does because the situation has changed in a specific way is exhibiting sensitivity. A player pursuing a pre-formed plan regardless of what has happened is not. Sensitivity is the difference between a conversation and a sequence of speeches. It is the precondition for feedback loops to carry genuinely independent material, and therefore the precondition for emergent fiction.

Stable state
: The position a design occupies relative to the transparency threshold once the learning curve is complete — the experience the design produces for players who have internalized its procedures. A design's stable state is its intended operating condition. Designing only for the stable state and treating the entry experience as a documentation problem is a common error: players who leave during the learning curve never reach the stable state, regardless of its quality.

Suspended outcome
: A resolution that produces a result requiring further resolution before play can continue. Designs that chain resolution steps — roll to attempt, then roll to determine degree, then consult a table to translate degree into fictional terms — are prone to this. Each additional step is a potential suspension point where the fiction waits on the procedure. A result that leaves the fiction suspended rather than transformed indicates a design problem, not a neutral state.

Trajectory
: The full arc of a design's relationship to the transparency threshold across time: its entry state, its learning curve, and its stable state. A design is not accurately described by a single threshold position; it has three. The trajectory is a property of the design-in-use across sessions, not a property of the document. Two designs with identical stable states can have dramatically different trajectories if one internalizes quickly and one does not. Designing deliberately means designing all three phases, not only the destination.

Transparency threshold
: The point at which the player's attention must shift from the fiction to the procedure in order to advance play. Below the threshold, procedure operates as background infrastructure: the player stays inside the fiction and the rules respond. Above the threshold, the player must engage the system as an object in order to produce fictional output. The threshold is not fixed: it is player-variable, shifting as procedures internalize and as facilitation distribution becomes available. In solo play, the effective threshold is lower because no facilitation distribution exists. The threshold marks a location on a continuum, not a pass/fail test. Knowing which side of it a design inhabits, and why, is more useful than claiming a generic fiction-first identity.

World engine
: The OSR and FKR design tradition, in which thin procedures serve world interaction and player problem-solving rather than fictional flow. A world engine keeps procedure light for the same reason a fiction engine does — to prevent overhead from occluding the game's primary object — but the primary object differs. In a world engine, the primary object is the world as a coherent problem space: what is actually here, what can I actually do, what are the real consequences of this action inside a world that operates according to its own logic. A ruling that halts play because the world's logic requires it is a correct ruling in a world engine. A ruling that halts the fiction is a failed ruling in a fiction engine. The procedural thinness the two traditions share does not make them the same design.
