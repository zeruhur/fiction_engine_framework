# Four Designs, One Framework

*A follow-up to "The Transparency Threshold: Abstraction, Mediation, and the Limits of Fiction Engine Design"*

## Applying the Fiction Engine Analysis in Practice

The previous essay defined the transparency threshold and placed design paradigms relative to it in broad strokes. This essay works at a finer resolution. It takes four specific games (*Freeform Universal*, *24XX*, *Breathless*, and *Hints and Hijinx*) and applies the fiction engine framework's tools to each one in turn.

The four were not chosen arbitrarily. They share enough surface properties to be grouped together under any conventional label: all four are rules-light, three are solo-capable, and all four sit closer to the fiction end of the design spectrum than PbtA, FitD, or Fate. A reader encountering all four for the first time might reasonably treat them as a family.

They are not a family. They make different tradeoffs, sit at different positions relative to the transparency threshold, and serve different design goals. Demonstrating precisely how they differ is what the framework is for. They also arrive from different design genealogies: *Freeform Universal* from the narrative indie tradition; *24XX* from the OSR and FKR lineage, pushed to the point where it touches the fiction engine paradigm from the other direction; *Breathless* as a direct hack of *24XX*; and *Hints and Hijinx* building on the Breathless SRD. That three separate traditions produce designs that cluster in similar territory is itself worth examining.

## What "Rules-Light" Conceals

Before examining the four games individually, it is worth naming the analytical problem they collectively illustrate.

"Rules-light" is as blunt a label as "narrative." It describes a quantity (fewer rules) without describing what the remaining rules do, where authority sits, what procedural demands they place on the player, or what kind of experience they are optimized to produce. A design can be rules-light and still sit above the transparency threshold. A design can be mechanically spare and still generate significant attentional overhead through the combined weight of what it does track.

The fiction engine framework replaces the quantity question with a function question. Not how many rules does this game have, but what does each rule do: does it produce a new fictional state, support the conditions for fictional play, or generate dead overhead? And the transparency threshold adds a second question: even if a rule is load-bearing, does it operate in the background or does it demand the player's conscious attention in order to advance play?

These two questions, applied consistently, produce meaningfully different assessments of games that a quantity-based approach would group together.

## 24XX: The Baseline Case

*24XX* sits closer to a pure fiction engine than any other design in this comparison, and it is worth establishing why before the other three introduce complications. It is also worth noting where it comes from: not from the narrative indie tradition but from the OSR and FKR lineage, where thin procedures serve world interaction and player problem-solving rather than fictional flow. *24XX* takes that tradition's commitment to minimal mediation and pushes it until the design touches the fiction engine paradigm from the other direction. The result is a design that looks like a fiction engine and functions like one, while arriving from a different set of values.

The core cycle is as thin as resolution gets without abandoning uncertainty entirely. Players describe what their characters do. The GM determines whether a roll is needed, and specifically, whether what they are doing is risky. Only risk triggers resolution. The roll produces one of three outcomes, each of which is immediately a fictional state: something goes wrong, something partially works, something succeeds. Play continues.

There is no classification step. There is no taxonomy of action types. There is no required translation layer between fictional intent and fictional consequence. The procedure is thin enough to become habitual almost immediately, which means it operates in the background from very early in play.

The design's most elegant single rule is the defense mechanic: say how one of your items breaks to turn a hit into a brief hindrance. The mechanical action and the fictional event are the same action. There is no administrative step between them. The player does not spend a resource and then describe a consequence; they describe a consequence and the mechanical effect follows automatically from the description. This is fiction engine design at its most precise.

The GM instructions reinforce the orientation throughout. "Describe characters in terms of behaviors, risks, and obstacles, not skill dice" is a design constraint that keeps the referee's output fictional rather than procedural. The bad luck test, where the GM rolls to see if trouble arrives, is a compact authority-distribution mechanism that produces fictional events rather than administrative prompts.

One friction point exists: advancement. After a job, each character increases a skill. The trigger ("after a job") is an administrative boundary rather than a fictional event. The advancement itself, a die step increase, is non-diegetic tracking. This is minor, and common to almost every design with any progression system, but it is the clearest point where *24XX* generates a small amount of overhead that does not immediately produce a fictional state.

What *24XX* gives up to stay below the threshold is worth naming. It provides minimal genre texture beyond what the setting supplement adds. It offers no pacing structure beyond the fictional situation itself. It generates no pressure economy. Tables that need the system to provide momentum and structure will find it sparse. These are not flaws. They are the costs of the tradeoff, and the design accepts them fully and without apology.

## Freeform Universal: The Ancestral Case

*Freeform Universal* is the design from which much of this lineage descends, and examining it against the framework reveals something interesting: the ancestor is not always the purest example of the paradigm it established. FU is an excellent fiction engine, but it carries one element that introduces a modest procedural foreground that its descendants often remove.

The core cycle is clean. Describe what you attempt, roll a d6, interpret the even/odd result as a fictional outcome on a six-point scale from yes-and to no-and, continue. The result table is so simple it fits on the character sheet and becomes habitual within minutes. No action taxonomy, no classification step, no required translation between fictional intent and fictional consequence.

The character system is as close to pure fictional description as a structured game gets. Body, Mind, Edge, and Flaw are not stat blocks; they are fictional descriptors, adjectives and short phrases that grant narrative permission and modify dice by adding bonus or penalty dice to the pool. Gear operates identically: an adjective and a noun, chosen for fictional legibility, functioning as a modifier when relevant. Conditions produced by outcomes are themselves fictional states (angry, tired, injured, trapped) that apply mechanically by adding penalty dice and fictionally by describing the character's actual situation. The mechanical state and the diegetic state are the same state, stated once. This is exemplary fiction engine design.

The Narrator guidance reinforces the orientation throughout. "Make rolls meaningful": every roll should matter regardless of result, and failure should never stop story momentum. "Say yes": players introducing fictional elements is encouraged rather than adjudicated. The explicit instruction that only players ever roll dice keeps the Narrator's attention on the fiction rather than the procedure.

FU Points are where the analysis becomes more complex. FU Points are a metacurrency: earned for good roleplaying and spectacular moments, spent for bonus dice before a roll or to reroll after. This introduces a non-diegetic resource pool that the player tracks and manages. It is not Fate-style authorial leverage (FU Points do not shape narrative directly) but they are tokens that exist outside the fiction as a mechanical buffer. The intermediate states of a FU Point pool have no mandatory diegetic expression. Three points versus one point is mechanically significant but fictionally silent until spent.

The saving grace is the earning mechanism. FU Points are generated by fictional behavior: doing something cool, making everyone laugh, playing your character authentically. The resource is non-diegetic in form but fiction-driven in generation. This is meaningfully different from a metacurrency earned by administrative milestones. It keeps the FU Point economy oriented toward the fiction even if the pool itself sits outside it.

The design explicitly acknowledges this as a variable. Starting FU Points, earning conditions, and spending options are all presented as adjustable parameters. Groups can run FU with no FU Points at all, in which case the design sits as clearly below the threshold as any game in this comparison. Groups that use FU Points heavily introduce a light metacurrency foreground. The threshold position is, by design, a dial rather than a fixed point.

What FU demonstrates as the ancestral case is that the fiction engine paradigm was established not by eliminating all non-diegetic elements but by keeping them minimal, optional, and fiction-oriented in their generation. Its descendants, including 24XX and the Breathless lineage, each made different choices about which elements to preserve, remove, or replace. Reading FU is reading the design space before those choices were made.

## Breathless: The Pressure Engine with Fiction Engine DNA

*Breathless* is the most complex case in this comparison because it is two designs in one: a fiction engine core carrying a pressure engine structure. Understanding where one ends and the other begins is where the analysis does its most useful work.

The check procedure inherits the fiction engine orientation cleanly. Narrate what your character does, roll only when things are risky, interpret the result as a fictional outcome. The GM telegraphs risk before the roll. The outcome categories (fail with complication, succeed with complication, succeed) all produce fictional states directly. No classification step. No action taxonomy. In isolation, the check procedure is a fiction engine procedure.

The die degradation mechanic is where Breathless makes its most original contribution to this design space, and it is a contribution the framework should acknowledge directly. Every roll steps the used die down one rating. The degradation is simultaneously a mechanical state and a fictional condition: the character is tiring, their resources are depleting, their capacity is diminishing. The player does not track a separate fatigue resource alongside the die rating; the die rating is the fatigue resource, expressed in the same element that drives resolution. Form and function are unified.

The catch your breath mechanic is equally precise. To reset all skill dice to their original ratings, the player asks to catch their breath. The GM immediately introduces a new complication. The administrative action, resetting die ratings, is structurally coupled to a fictional consequence: the situation changes. The player cannot restore their resources without the fiction changing. This is the cleanest expression of fiction-supporting infrastructure in this set of four designs: non-diegetic in form, fiction-producing in function, and impossible to engage without generating fictional output.

The stress track is where the friction begins. Four stress points lead to vulnerability, at which point failure on a dangerous action risks being taken out or sudden death. The threshold consequence is fictionally legible. But the intermediate stress states (one point, two points, three points) have no mandatory diegetic expression. A character at three stress is mechanically closer to danger, but the rules do not require that proximity to manifest as anything observable inside the fiction. The player must perform their own interpretive translation, which is exactly the kind of work the framework identifies as overhead accumulation: a non-diegetic state that demands the player bridge it to the fiction themselves.

The loot die adds a third degradation track alongside skill dice and the stress count. Each element is individually justifiable: skill dice represent capacity, the loot die represents scavenging fortune, stress represents accumulated damage. But the combined attentional cost of tracking all three simultaneously, plus backpack contents, which are capped at three items plus one med kit, pushes the design above the transparency threshold even though no single rule crosses it on its own. This is the fiction-supporting overhead accumulation failure mode: rules that are each load-bearing, whose combined weight shifts the player's primary attention from the situation to the resource economy.

The result is a design that sits just above the threshold by design. The tension-and-release loop (skills depleting under pressure, the risky decision of when to catch your breath, the GM complication that follows) is the experience Breathless is optimized to produce. The tracking load is the cost of that loop, and it is a cost the design pays deliberately. This does not make Breathless a poorly designed fiction engine; it makes it a well-designed pressure engine with fiction engine DNA in its core. The distinction is worth maintaining precisely because it explains both what Breathless does well and what it costs to do it.

For designers using the Breathless SRD as a base, including for solo adaptations, this analysis has a practical implication. The core check procedure and the die degradation and reset mechanics are the elements most consistent with fiction engine principles and most worth preserving. The stress track is the element most worth reconsidering or replacing, depending on whether the target design wants to maintain the pressure engine experience or move closer to the fiction engine side of the threshold.

## Hints and Hijinx: When Genre Requires the Crossing

*Hints and Hijinx* is the most instructive case in this comparison because its departure from fiction engine principles is not a design failure. It is a genre requirement, deliberately implemented, and understanding why it is necessary clarifies something the framework cannot say on its own: some genres resist fiction engine treatment at the structural level.

The Breathless core that the design inherits functions cleanly. The two-skill system, Introvert and Extrovert, is minimal and fictionally legible. The check procedure follows the established pattern: roll the skill die, interpret the result as a fictional outcome, the die degrades. The Refill Your Cup reset is the catch your breath equivalent, triggering a narrative event rather than a free administrative action. This section of the design sits below the transparency threshold.

The clue economy is where the design changes paradigm. Clues are accumulated as a count. They are carried between locations as a resource pool. They are spent in the Deduction phase to determine a die rating. This is a metacurrency system. A clue is not a fictional state in the framework's sense; it does not exist inside the fiction as a condition that characters can observe, respond to, or reason about. It is a token that exists outside the fiction as a mechanical resource, and the player tracks it, accumulates it, and spends it as such.

The Deduction Roll makes this fully explicit. The player writes a hypothesis (a fiction engine act, a narrative statement about what happened inside the fiction) but the die rating that determines whether the hypothesis is correct is calculated from clue count rather than from anything inside the fiction. The more clues used, the larger the die. The hypothesis is assessed not by its internal fictional coherence but by the size of the resource pool backing it. The fiction engine act of writing the hypothesis is coupled to a metacurrency act of spending tokens.

This is not a design flaw. Mystery investigation as a genre has structural properties that resist pure fiction engine treatment. A mystery requires accumulation: the gradual assembly of information toward a revelation. It requires deduction: a moment where the accumulated information is synthesized into a conclusion. And it requires stakes at that moment of synthesis: the deduction must feel consequential, and consequence requires that something is at risk. These structural requirements push toward a progress-tracking economy almost inevitably, because the fiction alone cannot reliably produce the sense that enough information has been gathered to justify a conclusion.

The design solves this elegantly within its constraints: the investigation phase uses fiction engine mechanics, and the deduction phase uses metacurrency mechanics. The threshold is crossed not gradually through accumulated overhead but at a specific structural moment: the transition from investigation to deduction. Below the threshold during investigation, above it during deduction. This is a deliberate architectural decision, and it works for the genre.

The design raises a question for the framework: can mystery investigation be served by a fiction engine without a clue economy? What would a pure fiction engine mystery look like? The answer is probably that it would look like a different kind of mystery, one where the revelation emerges from play rather than being unlocked by accumulated evidence. *Where the Wolves Are*, for instance, uses sealed events and observational uncertainty to produce a mystery structure without a clue economy. But that design produces a different mystery experience than *Hints and Hijinx* is trying to produce. Genre requirements and design goals are not separable.

## What the Comparison Reveals

Placing all four designs on the map the framework provides:

*Freeform Universal* sits below the threshold, with a variable margin determined by how the table uses FU Points. Without FU Points the design is as clean a fiction engine as this comparison contains. With FU Points active, a light metacurrency foreground is introduced (manageable, fiction-oriented in its generation, but present). The design presents this as a dial rather than a fixed position, which is itself a design philosophy worth naming.

*24XX* sits clearly below the threshold. Its procedural layer is thin enough to operate as background infrastructure almost immediately, and it achieves this partly by removing the FU Points economy entirely in favor of a pure risk-resolution cycle. It pays for this with minimal structural support and genre texture.

*Breathless* sits just above the threshold by design. Its die degradation and reset mechanics are exemplary fiction engine design. Its stress track and combined tracking load produce the pressure engine experience the game is optimized for. The crossing is deliberate and serves the design's goals.

*Hints and Hijinx* crosses the threshold at a specific structural moment, the transition to the deduction phase, because the mystery genre requires it. Below the threshold during investigation, above it during deduction. The crossing is architecturally contained rather than diffuse.

The genealogy reading adds something the paradigm reading alone cannot. *FU* arrives from the narrative indie tradition and establishes the fiction engine paradigm through fictional descriptors, fiction-driven outcomes, and an optional metacurrency. *24XX* arrives from a different direction entirely (OSR and FKR design values) and crosses into fiction engine territory by stripping mediation to its minimum in service of situational consequence. *Breathless* is a direct hack of *24XX*, taking its resolution core and adding a degradation economy that shifts the design toward pressure engine territory. *Hints and Hijinx* inherits that Breathless core and adds a clue economy on top, crossing the threshold at a specific structural moment. Three different genealogies, converging in the same design neighborhood while making meaningfully different choices about what stays and what goes.

That convergence is worth naming. It suggests the fiction engine paradigm is not the property of any single design tradition. It can be arrived at from narrative indie roots, from OSR minimalism, or from genre-specific adaptation. What the framework provides is a way to describe where each arrival lands and what it costs to land there, regardless of the route taken.

The comparison also reveals something the framework states abstractly: fiction engine and rules-light are not the same thing. *Breathless* is rules-light by any conventional measure, and it sits above the threshold. *FU* is more mechanically specified than *24XX* in several respects, and the FU Points economy introduces a variable the OSR-derived design deliberately shed. The quantity of rules is a poor predictor of transparency threshold position. The function and combined attentional cost of the rules is what matters.

## The Solo Dimension

Three of the four designs in this comparison are solo-capable; *Freeform Universal* is the exception, being designed for a group with a Narrator. This asymmetry is itself analytically useful.

A solo player has no facilitation distribution available. In group play, an experienced referee absorbs procedural weight: holds the result table in mind, manages the fiction's continuity, smooths attentional transitions. Other players contribute to this distribution: someone at the table who understands the system intuitively can carry load that allows others to stay inside the fiction. None of this is available to a solo player. Every procedural step is the solo player's sole responsibility.

This means the transparency threshold is effectively lower for solo design. A procedural foreground that a group of four distributes across multiple participants becomes a full cognitive load for one person. A tracking system that feels lightweight in a group game can feel heavy in solo play simply because there is no one to share it with.

FU's group design context is worth noting here precisely because its descendants were often adapted for solo play. The FU Points economy, already a light metacurrency in group play, becomes a different kind of cognitive demand when managed alone. This is one reason why *24XX*, which removes that economy, handles solo play more cleanly than FU does without adaptation. *Breathless*'s combined tracking load is more significant in solo play than the design's group context might suggest; most of its 200+ derivatives are solo designs, which makes the stress track's overhead more consequential than it might appear. *Hints and Hijinx* partially solves the problem by concentrating its above-threshold mechanics in a single structural phase rather than distributing them across the whole session.

The design question for solo fiction engines is whether the design is thin enough for one player to carry alone, without the experience becoming procedurally dominant. FU answers that question for groups. Its descendants answer it, differently, for one player at a time.

## Conclusion: The Framework as a Diagnostic Tool

The fiction engine framework offers three uses: as a design checklist, as an evaluative lens, and as a shared vocabulary. This comparison has demonstrated the second most directly.

The evaluative lens does not produce rankings. *24XX* is not better than *Breathless* because it sits further below the threshold. *Breathless* is not better than *Hints and Hijinx* because its departure from fiction engine principles is more gradual. Each design is better or worse at producing the specific experience it is optimized for, and the framework's job is to describe what each design is optimized for and what it costs to be optimized for that thing.

What the comparison reveals most clearly is that the fiction engine paradigm is a specific design commitment with specific costs, not a generic virtue that all good design shares. A design that stays below the transparency threshold accepts structural looseness, minimal genre texture, and limited pacing support in exchange for a fiction that remains primary throughout play. A design that crosses the threshold gains structural support, genre texture, and pacing economy, but at the cost of a procedural layer that demands the player's conscious attention.

Knowing which side of the threshold your design inhabits, and why, is more useful than claiming a generic fiction-forward identity. The four designs in this comparison each know what they are. *Freeform Universal* knows it is a flexible, group-oriented fiction engine with an optional metacurrency dial. *24XX* knows it is the thinnest possible version of that core. *Breathless* knows it is a pressure engine built on fiction engine foundations. *Hints and Hijinx* knows it crosses the threshold at a specific moment because its genre requires it.
