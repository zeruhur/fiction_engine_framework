# The Faction and the Fiction

*Part of the Fiction Engine companion essay series.*

## What Open Strategy Games Reveal at the Fiction Engine's Edge

The fiction engine framework describes a player making fictional choices about what their character attempts inside the diegetic layer. The player asks what their character would do, the procedure produces an outcome, the fiction changes, play continues. Everything orbits a subject: a character, a protagonist, a perspective located inside the shared imagined situation.

Open Strategy Games introduce a player making strategic choices about what their faction does inside a geopolitical fiction. The player asks what their faction should do given its leverage and objectives, the Referee adjudicates, the Report changes what everyone knows, and play continues. The fiction is present, shared, and primary. But nobody inhabits it the way a character inhabits their situation. The subject of play is a collective entity (a republic, a military force, a banking consortium) that acts in the world without anyone living inside it.

Both are fiction-primary designs. The fiction they are primary toward is not the same kind of fiction.

This essay is not asking whether OSG is a fiction engine. It is asking something more interesting: what does the fiction engine framework reveal when applied to OSG, and what does OSG reveal that the framework does not currently address? Both questions have substantive answers, and the second one has practical implications for how the framework handles referee adjudication.

## What OSG Shares with the Fiction Engine Paradigm

Before locating the divergences, the shared ground deserves precise description. OSG is not a fiction-adjacent design that happens to involve a fictional world. Its core procedures are fiction engine procedures operating at a different scale.

The action format (Action / Outcome / Leverage) requires every submission to be grounded in established fiction. Leverage is not a stat, a modifier, or a resource pool. It is a claim about the fictional world: resources, relationships, positions, and prior actions that have been established as facts in the fiction. The Referee assesses leverage as a fiction reader asking whether the established fiction supports the claim, not as a calculator running a resolution table. A player who submits an action with strong leverage has done fictional work to establish that leverage in prior turns. A player who submits an action with weak leverage is asking the fiction to support something it has not yet established. The distinction is entirely diegetic.

The RAT checklist (Reasonable, Actionable, Traceable) is quality control for fictional output, stated in wargame language. A Reasonable outcome is one proportionate to what the fiction has established. A Traceable outcome is one that emerges from prior fictional events: you can point to the decisions and conditions that led here. An Actionable outcome is one that keeps the fiction moving rather than suspending it, seeding hooks for other Actors to respond to. All three criteria are fiction engine criteria. The framework's insistence that outcomes must be continuations rather than endpoints is the same requirement Traceable and Actionable enforce together. The framework's test (does this rule produce a new fictional state?) is what Reasonable is checking.

"No Action Without Friction" maps directly onto the framework's prohibition on suspended outcomes. The explicit replacement of Hurdles with complications (replace "no" with "yes, but") is the yes-and, yes-but, no-but, no-and structure of fiction engine resolution expressed as a referee instruction rather than a dice table. Unopposed actions succeed, with the Referee determining how: the fiction advances, the Referee does not block it.

The Force of Nature mechanic (a chaotic external event introduced when both dice show the same number) is structurally equivalent to a twist mechanic: a fiction-producing event originating outside any player's control, emerging from threads already present in the fiction, creating new conditions rather than resolving old ones. The Contested Ground SRD is explicit that this is a prompt, not a requirement, and should be used only when a good Force of Nature is available. That is exactly the criterion a solo oracle should apply to a twist result.

What OSG shares with the fiction engine paradigm is the foundational commitment: procedures exist to serve the fiction, and any procedure that fails that test is a candidate for removal. The RAT checklist is, among other things, a per-outcome application of the fiction engine's central design test.

## The Transparency Threshold in OSG

OSG sits above the transparency threshold. The question worth asking is whether that position is deliberate, load-bearing, or incidental.

The action format requires conscious engagement with a three-part structure before the fiction can advance. This is a classification step: the player must identify an Action, separate it from an Outcome, and provide Leverage grounded in the fiction before the Referee can adjudicate. That step is not dead overhead; it is doing real fictional work by forcing the player to think about why their plan should work before asking the Referee to assess it. But it is procedurally foregrounded. The player is not simply stating their fictional intent; they are translating it into a structured submission format.

Private submission, Report reading, objective tracking, Turn Zero, and the management of information asymmetry across turns are all persistent attentional demands. A player in an OSG is simultaneously inside the strategic fiction, reading positions, evaluating leverage, forming alliances, and managing a procedural architecture. The dual attention is the experience the form produces.

This is closest to the FitD case described in the previous essays: procedure persistently foregrounded, not as a failure but as a design commitment that produces something fiction-transparent designs cannot. In FitD, the pressure economy creates tension. In OSG, the procedural architecture creates the information asymmetry and strategic depth that distinguish it from freeform diplomatic roleplay. Remove the action format and private submission, and you remove the mechanism that makes OSG a game rather than a collaborative fiction exercise.

The overhead is almost entirely load-bearing. The transparency threshold is crossed deliberately, and the crossing serves the design's goals. The framework holds that a rule can be intricate and still belong to a fiction engine, provided it produces fictional output rather than administrative obligation. By that logic, OSG's procedures qualify as fiction-supporting infrastructure even while they push the design above the threshold. What they produce is not dead overhead. What they produce is a layered geopolitical fiction in which position, leverage, and consequence interact over time.

## The Collective Subject Problem

The fiction engine framework assumes a diegetic layer populated by individual agents: characters, protagonists, referees, oracle outputs that describe what individual characters experience. Its player-facing guidance is built around this assumption. The relevant question is what the character would do. The diagnostic for fiction drift is whether the player is thinking about what the rules allow rather than what the character would do. The shared imagined space is a situation in which individual agents with individual perspectives are embedded.

OSG has no such agents. The subject of play is a faction: a collective entity with interests, leverage, and objectives, but no interiority in the fictional sense. The Republic of Saivorn controls deep-water ports and has no standing army. The Kingdom of Caldrath has the largest standing army and significant debt to House Meranto. These are fictional facts about collective entities, not descriptions of experiences or intentions that any individual holds. No one is inside the fiction the way a character is inside their situation. The fiction is experienced from the outside, as a map of forces and consequences, a geopolitical surface that the Actors operate across rather than inhabit.

This is a gap in the framework's vocabulary. The framework describes fiction as "the living, mutable situation that play produces and transforms moment to moment." That description fits OSG's geopolitical fiction; the situation is living, mutable, and produced by play. But the framework's description of what players do inside that situation (making informed choices about what their character attempts) does not fit. OSG players make informed choices about what their faction should do strategically given what the fiction has established. The unit of agency is collective, the register is strategic rather than experiential, and the fiction is a contested surface rather than an inhabited world.

The framework's focus on individual agents — characters with perspectives, intentions, and embedded situations — is not inadvertent narrowness. It is part of what defines the paradigm. OSG demonstrates something different: that fiction primacy as a principle extends beyond the fiction engine paradigm as a design family. A faction's leverage is as diegetic as a character's skill. A treaty obligation is as fictional as an injury. But the principle holding across both cases does not require the fiction engine framework to extend its vocabulary to cover collective agency. It requires recognizing that there is more than one design family that can hold the principle, and that the framework describes one of them with deliberate specificity.

## The Competitive Objective Structure

The fiction engine framework has no winning condition. Fiction engine design produces uncertainty and consequence as generative forces. The fiction moves because things keep changing, not because players are working toward measurable outcomes. Fictional momentum is its own justification: the situation develops, the oracle or referee or dice produce a result, and play continues into whatever new situation emerges. There is no external criterion against which that situation is assessed.

OSG introduces objectives: paired short-term and long-term goals, written into the Brief, self-assessed at game end. The Expectations Formula holds two orientations in explicit tension: "play to win, play to find out, both at once." This is not a contradiction but a design instruction. Players pursuing objectives produce the fictional pressure that makes OSG's fiction interesting. Without competitive stakes, the fiction has no structural reason to escalate, complicate, or surprise. Without fictional commitment, the competitive stakes become a resource optimization problem that collapses the fiction into a calculation.

The tension is generative. Saivorn's objective (secure a successor treaty that guarantees commercial freedom) shapes every action Saivorn takes, and those actions produce fictional consequences that other factions must respond to. The objectives are not a metagame layer on top of the fiction; they are the motivational structure that makes the fiction a contest rather than a chronicle. The fiction would still be produced without objectives, but it would be shapeless. Objectives give the fictional situation stakes, and stakes give outcomes consequence.

This reveals something the framework does not address: competitive structure can be a fiction-producing mechanism rather than a fiction-distorting one, provided the fiction remains the medium through which competition is resolved. OSG's adjudication keeps competition inside the fiction by requiring every action to be grounded in established fictional facts and every outcome to pass the RAT test. The competition is real; the resolution is always fictional. That combination is what distinguishes OSG from a wargame, where competition is resolved through calculation rather than fiction, and from pure collaborative roleplay, where there is no competition to resolve.

A useful distinction emerges from this: objective-driven play and fiction-driven resolution are not the same thing and do not conflict. OSG keeps the first while requiring the second for everything that matters. The fiction engine framework excludes winning conditions by design; that exclusion is part of the paradigm's definition, not a gap. What this comparison clarifies is that fiction-driven resolution is compatible with objective-driven play — which means designs outside the fiction engine paradigm can share its resolution logic while making different choices about goal structure. The boundary between the two runs through the question of whether play is producing situations or pursuing outcomes.

## The Layered Information Architecture

The fiction engine framework assumes a shared real-time fiction. All participants hold the same diegetic layer in common at any given moment. When the oracle produces a result, everyone hears it. When the referee describes a consequence, everyone knows it. When a character acts, the action is public to the table even if it is private within the fiction. The shared imagined space is, by assumption, shared.

OSG introduces information asymmetry as a design feature. Each Actor receives a Brief with private information that no other Actor sees. Actions are submitted privately. The Report reveals what the Referee chooses to reveal, which is not necessarily everything that happened. Different Actors hold different versions of the same fictional world at the same time. The Republic of Saivorn knows things about its own debt situation that House Meranto does not know it knows. The Conclave holds treaty clauses that have never been made public. The fiction is partially opaque, and that opacity is load-bearing.

This is a radically different relationship to the shared imagined space. In OSG, the fiction is never fully shared. It exists in layers: the public fiction established by the Report, and multiple private fictions held by individual Actors that interact with it. Intelligence, deception, and strategic uncertainty are all produced by information asymmetry built into the architecture, not improvised at the table.

The Report is the mechanism that periodically synchronizes the partially-shared fiction without collapsing the asymmetry. It is written in news-roundup style: outcomes chronicled as facts, hooks seeded for future play, private outcomes accompanied by traceable crumbs that let other Actors infer something happened without knowing what. The Report is the most sophisticated authority distribution mechanism examined in this essay series. It is simultaneously the public fiction, the shared layer all Actors hold in common, and a carefully managed partial reveal that preserves the strategic value of private information.

The fiction engine framework's concept of the shared imagined space is a design commitment, not an omission. Its oracle produces a shared output. Its referee produces a shared description. The assumption that all participants hold the same fiction in common is load-bearing: shared real-time fiction is what makes fictional consequence legible to everyone at the table simultaneously. OSG demonstrates that fiction can be primary, load-bearing, and shared while also being layered, asymmetric, and partially opaque. That is a different structural choice, not a correction. The public Report and the private Brief are both parts of the same fiction, and the game's strategic depth depends on the gap between them — a gap fiction engine play does not create because fiction engine play is not trying to produce strategic depth of that kind.

## The RAT Checklist as a General Fiction Engine Tool

Of all the elements in OSG's design, the RAT checklist deserves particular attention from fiction engine designers. It is the most exportable element in the form: a quality control mechanism for referee adjudication that applies to any fiction engine game where a referee mediates outcomes, not only to wargame-adjacent designs.

The framework currently offers the following guidance on outcomes: treat them as continuations rather than endpoints; use yes-and, yes-but, no-but, no-and to keep the fiction moving; avoid results that leave the fiction suspended rather than transformed. This guidance is sound, but it operates at the level of principle rather than practice. A referee in a fiction engine game knows they should produce fictional continuations. They do not always have a tool for checking whether a specific outcome they have just written actually does that.

The RAT checklist provides exactly that tool. Adapted from OSG's wargame context into the broader fiction engine register:

**Reasonable.** Is this outcome proportionate to what the fiction has established? Does it feel like something that would actually happen given the situation, the characters, and the conditions in play? An outcome that contradicts established fictional facts, assigns consequences out of proportion to the action, or introduces elements the fiction has not prepared the ground for fails this test. The fix is to find an outcome that the fiction already supports: one that the established situation makes plausible rather than requiring the fiction to be bent to accommodate it.

**Actionable.** Does this outcome advance the fiction rather than suspend it? Does it leave at least one participant something to respond to, investigate, exploit, or contend with? An outcome that resolves everything cleanly, eliminates a thread without replacing it, or produces a state in which nothing is at stake fails this test. The fix is not to make outcomes worse for the players but to ensure every outcome opens at least one new question, pressure, or possibility. A character who succeeds completely at their goal should find that success complicates something they had not anticipated. A character who fails should find that failure produces a new condition rather than a dead end.

**Traceable.** Can you point to earlier events in the fiction that led here? Does this outcome feel like a consequence of prior decisions rather than something that arrived from outside the fiction? An outcome that cannot be traced to established fictional facts, that depends on information or conditions not yet established in the shared fiction, fails this test. The fix is to either root the outcome in something that has already been established, or to establish the relevant conditions before producing the outcome.

The three tests work together in a specific way. Reasonable ensures the outcome is supported by the fiction that exists. Traceable ensures the outcome emerges from the fiction's history rather than arriving from outside it. Actionable ensures the outcome serves the fiction's future rather than closing it off. A referee who passes all three tests has produced an outcome that is simultaneously grounded in what the fiction is, what it has been, and what it needs to become.

What the RAT checklist adds to the framework's existing guidance is the Traceable criterion. The framework's yes-and / no-but structure ensures Actionable outcomes. The framework's Reasonable test (is this outcome proportionate and plausible?) is implicit in the concept of fictional states. But Traceable is not currently named anywhere in the framework, and it is doing distinct work. An outcome can be plausible and actionable while still feeling arbitrary if it cannot be traced to prior fictional events. Traceability is what makes an outcome feel like a consequence rather than a contrivance. It is the difference between a fiction that accumulates, where earlier decisions have weight and later events feel earned, and a fiction that resets, where each turn's outcomes are disconnected from what came before.

The practical form of the RAT checklist for a fiction engine referee or oracle interpreter might read:

Before accepting any resolution outcome, ask:

- Is this what the fiction supports? (Reasonable)
- Does this create something to respond to? (Actionable)
- Can I trace this to something that already happened? (Traceable)

If any test fails, rewrite the outcome before continuing. The fiction engine equivalent of the OSG Referee's mandate (do not publish a Report that fails the RAT check) is a referee or player who does not accept an oracle interpretation or GM ruling that fails these tests.

## Where OSG Sits

OSG is not a fiction engine. The transparency threshold is crossed structurally, the subject of play is collective rather than individual, and the competitive objective structure introduces a goal-directedness that fiction engine design does not have. A player in an OSG is managing a procedural architecture alongside the fiction in a way that the fiction engine paradigm specifically works to eliminate.

But OSG is not a pressure engine or a metacurrency engine either. It does not use codified move triggers, persistent resource economies, or explicit authorial leverage. Its fiction is primary in a way that aligns it with the fiction engine family of values while placing it outside the fiction engine paradigm as the framework defines it.

The design occupies a region adjacent to the fiction engine paradigm, sharing its foundational commitment to fiction primacy and fictional consequence while operating on a collective subject, a competitive objective structure, and a layered information architecture that the framework was not built to describe. It is neither inside the map the framework draws nor simply outside it. It is at the edge: recognizable as sharing the same values, operating through different means.

The Expectations Formula names this position as precisely as any design document can: play to win, play to find out, both at once. A fiction engine asks only the second. OSG asks both, and finds that asking both produces a different kind of fiction (a fiction of strategic forces, contested positions, and layered information) that fiction engine designs, in their commitment to individual agency and real-time shared fiction, are not designed to produce.

## What Each Reveals About the Other

The fiction engine framework applied to OSG reveals that fiction primacy is a broader principle than the framework's current language captures. A design can keep the fiction primary without keeping the subject of play individual, the fiction fully shared, or the resolution oriented toward individual consequence. The collective, the asymmetric, and the strategic are all compatible with fiction primacy, provided every procedure is tested against whether it produces fictional output rather than administrative obligation.

OSG applied to the fiction engine framework marks three edges of the paradigm. The framework's commitment to individual character agency — what the character does, experiences, and intends — is a defining feature, not an oversight; designs whose subject of play is a collective entity operate outside it by design. The framework's exclusion of winning conditions is equally deliberate; OSG demonstrates that fiction-driven resolution is compatible with objective-driven play, but the fiction engine paradigm does not combine them, and that is the point. And the framework's shared imagined space — its structural assumption that all participants hold the same fiction in common — is a load-bearing commitment, not a limitation; OSG's layered, asymmetric architecture makes different choices and produces different affordances.

These edges are not deficiencies. They are places where the framework's precision about one design space leaves adjacent design spaces undescribed — deliberately, because describing them all would dissolve the paradigm into a general theory of fiction-primary games. The fiction engine framework is a map of one region. OSG is a landmark just outside that region whose position helps clarify where the region ends.

The most practically useful exchange between the two is the RAT checklist. Developed inside OSG's wargame-adjacent context, it is a general-purpose tool for fiction engine referee adjudication that is more operationally precise than the framework's current guidance on outcomes. Reasonable, Actionable, Traceable: three tests that any referee, oracle interpreter, or player adjudicating their own solo outcomes can apply to check whether a resolution is genuinely serving the fiction. The framework's guidance says outcomes should be continuations rather than endpoints. The RAT checklist tells you how to verify that a specific outcome is.

## Conclusion: Knowing the Edge of the Map

The fiction engine essay series has moved progressively outward: from the framework itself, to the transparency threshold that marks its boundary, to specific designs within and adjacent to the paradigm, and now to a form that shares its foundational values while operating in a different register entirely.

OSG demonstrates that fiction primacy, the commitment to keeping every procedure in service of the fiction, is a principle that travels further than any single design paradigm can contain. Fiction engine designs are one family of implementations. OSG is another, working through collective subjects, layered information, and competitive stakes toward a fiction of a different kind: strategic, contested, and revealed turn by turn through a public Report that tells you what happened while preserving the asymmetries that make the next turn matter.

Knowing where the fiction engine paradigm ends and this adjacent territory begins is part of knowing what the fiction engine paradigm is. The edge of the map is not a failure of the map. It is where the map becomes useful as a boundary rather than a guide.
