# The Emergence Condition
## Why Thin Mediation Enables What Thick Procedure Suppresses

*Part of the Fiction Engine companion essay series.*
*This essay builds on "Fiction and Memory: The Structural Difference Between Accumulation and Reset." It is also readable as a standalone piece following the framework and the transparency threshold essay.*

---

The fiction engine framework ends with a claim it does not explain. "The story appears to emerge through play rather than being processed through procedure." The word "appears" is doing quiet work here. It names an experience — one that practiced players recognize immediately, the moment a session goes somewhere nobody planned, when a consequence follows from twenty minutes ago in a way that surprises everyone including the referee — without naming the mechanism that produces it. The framework is correct that emergence happens under these conditions. It does not say why.

This essay says why. The answer comes from systems thinking, but the argument is about design, and the vocabulary will be kept in its place: defined once, used precisely, dropped when it is not doing work. The practical implication of the argument is significant enough that it changes how several of the framework's design commitments should be understood. Some of what the framework presents as stylistic preference or design philosophy is, on this account, a structural condition. Remove it and you are not making a different aesthetic choice. You are breaking a mechanism.

---

## What Emergence Actually Means

Emergence is a property of systems: a behavior or characteristic that no component of the system has individually but that the system produces through the interaction of its components. A murmuration of starlings has a shape. No individual starling has a shape. The shape emerges from local interaction rules: each bird tracks its nearest neighbors and adjusts its position. No bird is flying the shape. The shape arises from the network.

This is not magic. It is a structural consequence of how components interact, and it is predictable in the following sense: if you know the interaction rules, you know what conditions allow emergence to occur. You cannot predict the specific shape of a specific murmuration. You can predict that murmurations will have shapes, that those shapes will cohere and shift, and that they will stop being shapes if the interaction rules are removed.

Applied to tabletop play: something emerges during a session when the fiction produced by the session's end could not have been predicted from the elements at its start, and when the unpredictable development is felt as arising from within the situation rather than being imposed from outside it. This is a real phenomenon. Every person who has played a fiction engine game has experienced it. The question is which interaction rules produce it and which prevent it.

## Agents, Sensitivity, and Feedback

The system that produces emergent fiction has three components worth naming.

**Agents** are participants in the play system who take inputs and produce outputs: players describing what their characters do, a referee describing the world's response, an oracle answering a question about the fiction's state, a procedure transforming fictional intent into a fictional outcome. Each agent has some model of the fiction's current state. Each acts on that model to produce the next state.

**Sensitivity** is the property by which an agent's output is shaped by the specific prior outputs of other agents, not merely by the agent's own goals. A player who responds to what another character just did — adjusting their own character's action because the situation has changed in a specific way — is exhibiting sensitivity. A player who pursues a pre-formed plan regardless of what has happened in the last twenty minutes is not. Sensitivity is the difference between a conversation and a sequence of speeches.

**Feedback loops** form when agent A produces an output that agent B uses as input, and agent B's output becomes input for agent A. The loop closes when the originating agent responds to the response to their output. In play terms: a character does something. The world responds. The character responds to the response. What the character does next is shaped by the full chain of events that preceded it, not only by their initial intent.

Now the crucial piece. Feedback loops need substance to run over. A loop between two agents whose outputs are generic — who could be responding to any situation rather than this situation — produces only nominal emergence. The surprise is shallow because the inputs were undifferentiated. Accumulated fiction, as described in the preceding essay, is what gives feedback loops their substance. When each agent is responding to the specific accumulated state of the fiction — to the particular way this situation has developed from that prior one — the feedback loops are closing over something specific and irreversible. That specificity is what makes the emergent development feel like it came from inside the fiction rather than from outside it.

The conditions for emergent fiction are not mysterious: multiple agents, sensitivity to each other's outputs, feedback loops closing over accumulated state. If those conditions are present, emergence is possible. If any of them is absent, it is not.

## What Thick Mediation Does to Feedback Loops

This is where the design argument earns its place.

When a procedural foreground is present, the player's first task upon receiving the current fictional state is not to respond to it but to classify it. Which move applies here? Which resource category is at stake? What does the system accept as valid input for this type of action? That classification step redirects the player's attention from the fiction and from the other agents to the system. While the player is classifying, the feedback loop between agents is interrupted.

This is not a criticism of the player's engagement. It is a description of what classification demands. Classification requires holding a taxonomy in mind and matching the current situation to one of its categories. The current situation must be read as a representative instance of a type rather than as a specific, accumulated state. That is the cognitive work the classification step asks for, and while that work is being done, the player is oriented toward the system rather than toward the other agents.

The interruption is brief in practiced play. An experienced PbtA player identifies the applicable move quickly, sometimes instantaneously. The transparency threshold essay makes this point: internalization reduces the attentional cost of the classification step until it operates nearly in the background. But it is structurally always there. The loop cannot close until the classification is resolved. And before internalization is complete — which takes time that varies significantly across players and groups — the interruption is real and recurring.

The second effect of thick mediation is more subtle and harder to recover through internalization. Call it the uniformity effect.

Procedural taxonomies are designed to produce consistent, legible outputs. This is one of their genuine strengths. A move structure ensures that a given type of fictional event reliably produces a given range of outcomes: when you act under fire, the game delivers tension and consequence in a recognizable form. The consistency creates dramatic rhythm. The recognizability creates strategic legibility. Players know what this kind of scene does.

The cost is that different inputs can produce identical procedural outputs. Two situations that are fictionally distinct — that differ in everything the accumulated fiction has established about the characters, their history, the stakes, the relationships in the room — can both trigger the same move and produce the same range of outcomes. The move's output range is not sensitive to the accumulated specificity of the fiction. It produces drama of a type, not drama of this situation.

The agents are responding to the move's output range rather than to the fiction's specific state. The feedback loops close, but they close over the move's output rather than over the accumulated fiction's full specificity. Something is lost at the classification step: all the ways this situation is different from any other situation that would also trigger this move. That loss is exactly the material from which unexpected, specific, unrepeatable emergent developments are made.

This is not a flaw in designs that use procedural taxonomies. It is a tradeoff. The consistent dramatic rhythm that move structures produce is a real design achievement — one that is not available to designs without that structure. Some players find the reliability more valuable than the specificity. That is a rational preference, and the paradigms built around move structures serve it well. The point is not that move structures are wrong. It is that they are optimized for something other than the kind of emergence the fiction engine paradigm is trying to produce, and the reason is structural.

In a fiction engine, the player receives the current fictional state and responds to it directly. The procedural layer is thin enough that the player's attention passes through it rather than pausing at it. There is no classification step to perform, or the classification is so obvious that it requires no conscious engagement. The feedback loop between agents is not interrupted. Each response is responding to the specific accumulated state of the fiction, all of it, not to a procedural category that the fiction instantiates. The specificity is preserved. The feedback loops close over material that is dense, particular, and irreversible. That is where emergent development comes from.

## The Social Dimension as a Systems Claim

The framework's social dimension section makes a claim that is typically read as cultural advice: "a fiction engine functions only when all participants are oriented toward the fiction as the primary object of play." The systems framing reveals this as something more precise than a recommendation about table culture. It is a description of the feedback loop condition.

When participants are oriented toward the fiction, they are oriented toward each other's outputs. The shared fictional state is the medium through which their sensitivity to each other is expressed. A player who is inside the fiction is reading the outputs of every other agent — what the referee described, what the other characters did, what the oracle produced — and responding to them as inputs. The feedback loops are running.

When a participant is oriented toward the system instead — tracking resources, consulting the procedure, managing their mechanical position — they have temporarily withdrawn from the shared fictional medium. They are still present at the table. They are not producing outputs in response to the fiction's current state. Their attention is elsewhere, and while it is elsewhere, the feedback loops weaken. The other agents are producing outputs that this participant is not receiving as inputs. When they return to the fiction, they are responding to a state that has accumulated in their absence.

This happens briefly and constantly in games above the transparency threshold. In groups with high experience and strong internalization, the interruptions are short enough that the loops recover quickly. In groups with lower experience, or in solo play where there is no distribution of attentional load, the interruptions accumulate and the conditions for emergence degrade progressively over the session.

The framework says this is a "table culture condition that design can support but not guarantee." The systems account explains exactly why design cannot guarantee it. Emergence is a property of the system-in-use, not of the design document. The design creates the interaction rules. The agents produce the interaction. A fiction engine is a design whose interaction rules preserve the feedback loop conditions. Whether those conditions produce emergent fiction depends on the agents actually running the loops; that is not something a document can compel.

## Complexity in the Fiction, Complexity in the Procedure

The framework states plainly that it is not a position against complexity. "A rule can be intricate and still belong to a fiction engine, provided it either changes the shared imagined space directly or preserves the coherence, pacing, or legibility that makes fictional change possible." The emergence argument supports this position and makes it more precise.

Complexity in the fiction does not suppress emergence. It feeds it. A setting with deep historical layering, a character with a long history of decisions and consequences, a political situation with multiple competing factions and overlapping obligations — all of this is accumulated state over which feedback loops can close. Richer accumulated state means more specific inputs and outputs, more ways the current situation differs from any other situation, more material from which unexpected development can arise. If anything, complexity of fictional state accelerates the conditions for emergence by giving agents more to be sensitive to.

What suppresses emergence is complexity in the procedure: the classification steps, the resource economies, the attentional demands that redirect agents toward the system. These reduce the time agents spend oriented toward each other and toward the fiction. The distinction is between complexity that enriches the feedback loop's content and complexity that interrupts the loop's operation. The fiction engine paradigm is committed to eliminating the second kind. It is not committed to limiting the first.

The practical implication for design: a fiction engine can support a richly specified world, a character system with many distinct descriptors, a political landscape with dozens of named factions and shifting alliances. All of that complexity lives in the fiction and feeds the feedback loops. What it cannot support, while staying below the transparency threshold, is the procedural complexity required to manage that same richness through a classification-based resolution system. The fiction holds the complexity. The procedure stays thin.

## The Failure Modes as Feedback Network Degradation

The framework names four failure modes: overhead accumulation, suspended outcomes, authority drift, and orientational divergence. They are presented as design problems — things a design can do wrong. The emergence account reveals something more specific: each failure mode is a distinct mechanism by which the feedback network can degrade.

**Overhead accumulation** adds classification demands. Each additional tracking obligation, each additional resource state that must be consulted before acting, is another moment where the agent's attention redirects from the fiction to the system. Individually, each piece of overhead is a brief interruption. Cumulatively, the interruptions accumulate and the time agents spend oriented toward the fiction decreases relative to the time they spend oriented toward the system. The feedback loops do not break; they weaken progressively, and the outputs they produce become less specific, less responsive to the accumulated fiction, more dominated by administrative state.

**Suspended outcomes** are loops that cannot close. A resolution that requires further resolution before play can continue produces an incomplete output: an agent has acted, but the system has not yet produced the fictional consequence of that action. The other agents cannot respond to an outcome that has not been produced. The feedback loop is paused mid-cycle. In a design prone to suspended outcomes, the loop is paused frequently — every time the resolution chain requires additional steps before a fictional state is produced. Agents develop habits of waiting rather than responding, and the sensitivity that the loop requires atrophies.

**Authority drift** transforms an agent in the feedback network into an obstacle within it. A referee whose rulings require procedural negotiation before producing a fictional consequence, or an oracle whose outputs require significant interpretive work before play can continue, has stopped functioning as a node in the feedback network. It is a point at which inputs enter and fictional outputs do not emerge. Other agents cannot close their feedback loops through an authority mechanism that is not producing fictional outputs. They wait, interpret, translate — and in doing so, redirect their attention from the fiction to the mechanism.

**Orientational divergence** is the most complete form of network degradation. When participants hold different models of what the game is doing, the feedback loops between them are running on different channels. A player treating the game as a tactical puzzle is producing outputs calibrated to mechanical optimization. A player treating it as character exploration is producing outputs calibrated to psychological consistency. These outputs are not legible to each other as inputs to the shared fiction: each player is responding to a different game, and no shared accumulated state develops. The fiction fragments because there is no shared medium for the feedback loops to run through.

These four failure modes are not arbitrary bad practices. They are the specific mechanisms by which the conditions for emergent fiction break down. The framework is correct to name them and correct in the remedies it implies. The emergence account explains why they matter structurally, not only pragmatically.

## The Mechanism Behind the Magic

The story does not merely "appear to emerge." It emerges through a mechanism, and the mechanism is now visible: feedback loops between agents sensitive to each other's outputs, closing over accumulated fictional state, with a procedural layer thin enough not to interrupt them. Those are the conditions. When they are present, emergent development is possible. When any of them is absent, it is not.

The emergence is real. The "magic" quality — the sensation that something is happening that nobody planned — is a genuine experiential property of the system, and it is not available to designs that interrupt the feedback loops, dissolve the accumulated specificity, or redirect the agents' attention toward the system rather than toward each other. The fiction engine paradigm's commitment to thin mediation is not a stylistic preference for a certain kind of procedural minimalism. It is a commitment to the structural condition that keeps the feedback loops intact.

This matters for how the paradigm's design commitments should be read. When the framework says outcomes should be continuations rather than endpoints, it is protecting the feedback loops from suspended-outcome interruptions. When it says fictional descriptors should do the work that numerical interfaces claim to do, it is keeping complexity in the fiction rather than in the procedure, feeding the loops rather than interrupting them. When it says the game should remain more visible than the system, it is stating the orientation condition on which the loops depend. These are not arbitrary aesthetic choices. They are structural requirements for the mechanism to run.

A designer who understands the mechanism can evaluate their own decisions against it. Not "is this rule elegant?" or "does this rule feel fiction-first?" but "does this rule interrupt the feedback loops, and if so, does what it produces justify the interruption?" Some interruptions are worth accepting; the transparency threshold essay makes this clear, and the four-designs essay demonstrates it in practice. The question is whether the designer is accepting the interruption deliberately, knowing what it costs, rather than accumulating it without accounting for the damage.

Understanding the mechanism is what allows the cost to be seen clearly.

There is one further implication the mechanism produces, which this series has not yet addressed. If what the fiction engine designer is building is a set of conditions for feedback loop behavior rather than a fixed procedural object, then those conditions change as agents use the design. Internalization moves the classification steps out of the attentional foreground. Accumulated play builds the fictional state over which the loops run. The system is modifying its own operating conditions through use. That property — a design that changes its own behavior as it is used — is the subject of the next essay.
