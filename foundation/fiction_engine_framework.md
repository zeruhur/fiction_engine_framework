# Fiction Engine: A Design Framework for Tabletop RPGs

_A note on terms: this book uses a few words more precisely than everyday speech does. Fiction means the diegetic situation of play, not narrative craft. Fictional state means a condition inside that situation, not a note in the game record. Overhead means procedural steps that produce neither fictional change nor the conditions for it, which is a narrower category than it might appear. The glossary at the back defines every term; the chapters earn them as they use them._

"Narrative game" is too broad to be analytically useful.

Games grouped under that label differ in where they place authority, how they resolve uncertainty, and how much interpretive labor they require. A game where a referee adjudicates outcomes freely against the situation does something structurally different from one where codified move triggers govern what the fiction can produce. A game where players hold authorial leverage over their story's shape through a metacurrency does something different again from one where every fictional change comes from in-world cause and effect. "Narrative" names a loose family resemblance, games that care about story in some sense, without describing what any of them does or how.

A fiction engine is a game design that treats play as a system for producing, sustaining, and transforming fiction with minimal friction. Its function is to keep the fiction moving while preserving uncertainty, agency, and consequence. The procedural layer between fictional intent and fictional change should be as thin as the situation allows. The rules are infrastructure, not experience.

## What fiction means

In this framework, fiction means the diegetic layer: the characters, places, events, and conditions that participants treat as real within the game. It is the living, mutable situation that play produces and transforms moment to moment. It is not narrative craft, literary quality, or storytelling in the writerly sense.

Not all of the fiction is known to everyone at every moment. The referee's hidden map, an NPC's undisclosed motive, a player's private knowledge: all of these are part of the fiction. What participants hold in common is the part that play has revealed so far. The distinction is not between fiction and non-fiction but between what is already known inside the diegetic layer and what is not known yet. When this book speaks of the shared fiction, it means that revealed part.

When this framework says a rule should serve the fiction, it means the rule should produce changes to the fiction rather than changes to a score, a resource pool, or an administrative record that exists outside it. When a character forces a door and it gives way noisily, alerting the guard upstairs, the fiction has changed. When a player marks off two hit points and the scene continues exactly as it would have without that mark, it has not.

## What counts as fictional state

A fictional state is a condition inside the fiction that the characters could perceive and respond to, and that the participants can therefore read, reason about, and act on. It is a property of the world, not a property of the game record.

A character's exhaustion is a fictional state when other characters can observe it and factor it into their decisions: she is pale, her movements are slow, she cannot lift the bar. It stops being a fictional state when it is only a number that modifies future rolls without changing how the situation reads inside the world.

This distinction resolves several apparent edge cases:

**Positioning.** Fictional state when it describes where a character actually is and what that means for what they can do or see. Not fictional state when it is a grid coordinate with no imagined spatial equivalent.

**Inventory depletion.** Fictional state when it changes what the character can plausibly attempt: she has no rope, so the climb is now different. Not fictional state when it is a count that has no consequence until an arbitrary threshold is crossed.

**Emotional state.** Fictional state when other characters can perceive it and it shapes what the situation will accept as plausible action. Not fictional state when it is a tag on a character sheet with no behavioral consequence inside the fiction.

**Tactical or mechanical bonuses.** Not fictional state. A +1 ongoing is a modification to a future roll. It becomes fictional state only if it reflects something that exists inside the fiction (she has the high ground, she has momentum, the crowd is watching) and would be visible to anyone present in the scene.

The test in each case is the same: remove the game record and ask whether the condition still exists inside the fiction as something the characters could respond to. Fictional states survive that removal. Administrative records do not.

Everything in this book follows from that test.

## The structural commitment

A fiction engine is the design family defined by a single structural commitment: every rule exists to serve the fiction, and any rule that fails that test is a candidate for removal.

The commitment is structural, not stylistic. It applies to every design decision: resolution procedures, character traits, pacing tools, uncertainty mechanisms. Each one either produces fictional change, supports the conditions under which fictional change can occur, or generates dead overhead that serves neither purpose. The third category is what a fiction engine works to eliminate. The test applies to each rule individually and to the system as a whole.

The distinction operates on function rather than form. A rule that is non-diegetic in form (a stress track, a pacing clock, a campaign log) can still be fiction-supporting in function if it reliably preserves the coherence, legibility, or continuity that makes fictional change possible. The question is never whether a rule looks mechanical. It is whether the rule is carrying its weight in fictional terms.

The label is a tool for precision, not a claim that this family is better than others. It names one design approach, with a specific logic and specific costs, so that the approach can be described clearly enough to reason about.

## Position and scope

This framework describes one design paradigm among several. It is not a claim that all good tabletop RPG design should follow these principles, nor that other paradigms are deficient versions of this one. Other coherent approaches exist: games built around codified move triggers, managed pressure economies, explicit metacurrency, character-world interfaces, or world-as-procedure. Each makes tradeoffs that reflect a real design philosophy, and each produces experiences a fiction engine is not built to produce: tension under managed risk, strategic identity, the ritual weight of dramatic grammar. Evaluating any of them with criteria borrowed from a different paradigm produces bad analysis, in either direction.

Fiction-first design is not identical to the fiction engine paradigm. Many games prioritize fiction while relying on formalized procedural grammars, codified move structures, explicit pacing economies, or mechanical interfaces that stay in the foreground during play. A fiction engine is the narrower subset of designs that try to minimize mediation itself, keeping the procedural layer as transparent as possible while still preserving uncertainty and consequence.

This framework does not measure the quality of a game. It measures how far a design serves fiction as the primary object of play by minimizing the mediation between fictional intent and fictional change. When a design is trying to produce something else (pressure, strategy, explicit authorship, puzzle-solving, character construction, mastery, ritual tension) the framework must recognize a change of paradigm, not treat the design as a degraded fiction engine. A foregrounded procedure is not a bad procedure. It is a procedure buying something with attention, and the relevant question is whether the experience it buys is the one the design wants. Fiction-transparency is a commitment, not a universal virtue.

A fiction engine has no winning condition. The fiction moves because the situation keeps changing, not because participants are working toward a predetermined resolution. Designs that use fiction as the medium for a competitive or goal-directed experience are built around a different structural logic. They are not worse designs, but their logic is not the fiction engine's.

The paradigm accepts, as the direct consequence of minimal mediation, that it provides less structural scaffolding than paradigms built around dramatic prescription or managed pressure. It offers no move list to generate a consistent dramatic rhythm, no metacurrency to give players authorial leverage over the story's shape, no pressure economy. Tables that need the system to supply momentum and structure will find a fiction engine sparse. That is the shape of the tradeoff, not a flaw in it.

A reader coming from a different design tradition should treat this book as a map of one region, not a map of the whole territory. Part V describes where that region ends.

## How to use this framework

The framework serves three purposes, and a reader can apply it in any of them.

**As a design checklist.** When building a game, use the criteria and failure modes in this chapter to evaluate individual rules and the system as a whole. The central test, whether a rule produces a new fictional state or overhead, applies to every design decision.

**As an evaluative lens.** When analyzing an existing game, use the definition and criteria to determine how fully it belongs to this paradigm. A game that does not meet the criteria is not necessarily worse, only built around a different engine of play. The lens is diagnostic, not a ranking device: its purpose is to identify what kind of experience a structure is optimized to produce and whether its procedures serve that aim.

**As a shared vocabulary.** The glossary gives precise terms for concepts that are often discussed imprecisely: fictional intent, fictional state, overhead, suspended outcomes, authority drift, the transparency threshold. Using them consistently reduces the ambiguity that labels like "narrative" or "story-driven" carry.

## Core operation

A fiction engine turns fictional intent into fictional change.

The player states what their character attempts within the fiction. The game produces an outcome. That outcome modifies the fiction. Play continues. This cycle should run with as little procedural overhead as the situation allows. Not with none: uncertainty and consequence both require procedure. The rules handle the friction between intent and outcome, then get out of the way.

A fiction engine does not require translation into detailed tactical subsystems, elaborate metacurrency loops, or dense narrative grammar. It does not ask players to negotiate with the rules themselves in order to advance a scene. This is not a position against complexity. It is a position against complexity that produces neither fictional output nor the conditions for it. A rule can be intricate and still belong to a fiction engine, provided it either changes the fiction directly or preserves the coherence, pacing, or legibility that makes fictional change possible. Complexity in the fiction (a deep history, many factions, a long chain of consequences) feeds play. Complexity in the procedure has to justify the attention it takes. Part III explains why.

The goal is not to simulate everything, nor to formalize everything. The goal is to maintain momentum.

## Range of application

The commitment holds across formats and scales. A fiction engine is as valid for a solo player as for a group of five, for a one-shot as for a long campaign. It is genre-neutral: horror, comedy, space opera, and intimate drama can all be built as fiction engines.

What changes across formats is the structural conditions under which the commitment operates: how authority is distributed, what sustains uncertainty, who absorbs procedural weight. A group can spread that weight across several people; a solo player carries all of it, and needs an oracle to supply what no single participant can supply alone. Solo design is therefore not group design with one player, but a different configuration of the same commitment. Part IV treats it on its own terms.

## How outcomes function

A fiction engine treats outcomes as continuations, not endpoints.

The productive question after any resolution is not simply whether an action succeeded. It is: what new fictional state does this result create, and how does play proceed from there? The range of outcomes (yes and, yes but, no but, no and) each forces the fiction to keep changing. Each opens the next move rather than closing the last one.

A result that leaves the fiction suspended rather than transformed indicates a design problem, not a neutral state. A resolution chain that produces a number, then requires another roll to interpret the number, then requires the referee to translate the interpretation into fictional terms, has three points where the fiction waits on the procedure. One is enough.

Outcomes also have to be traceable. A traceable outcome is one that can be followed back to fictional facts already established: the decisions, conditions, and events that made it possible. An outcome that arrives from outside the established fiction may be plausible and may keep play moving, yet it still feels like a contrivance rather than a consequence. Traceability is what lets each outcome add to the fiction's causal tissue, so that prior events constrain what follows and later events feel earned. Part III develops why this matters.

## Authority and procedure

Fiction engines distribute narrative authority in different ways. Some use a referee. Some use shared authorship. Some use structured uncertainty mechanisms such as oracles in solo or low-referee play. All of these are workable. What the rules must do in each case is make the distribution of authority visible and operable, rather than leaving it to convention or improvisation.

The principle across all configurations is the same: whatever mechanism handles authority must produce fictional output rather than administrative prompts. When the procedure begins to dominate the fiction, when the table spends more time tracking states, resolving subsystems, or consulting tables than engaging with the situation, the engine is no longer working as intended.

In solo play the oracle carries an extra structural role. It is the only source of fictional content the player did not generate, which makes it a second agent in play rather than a resolution convenience. Part IV develops this.

## Failure modes

A fiction engine fails in characteristic ways.

**Overhead accumulation.** Rules that generate tracking obligations, resource management, or procedural steps without producing fictional output are overhead. Individually each may seem minor. Cumulatively they shift the table's attention from the situation to the system. The engine stalls not through a single bad rule but through the weight of many marginal ones. A subtler variant involves rules that are each load-bearing but whose combined attentional cost still pushes the design above the transparency threshold.

**Suspended outcomes.** A resolution that produces a result requiring further resolution before play can continue breaks the engine's fundamental cycle. Designs that chain resolution steps (roll to attempt, then roll to determine degree, then consult a table to translate degree into fictional terms) are prone to this. Each additional step is a potential suspension point.

**Authority drift.** Any mechanism for distributing narrative authority can drift from producing fictional events toward producing administrative prompts that require significant interpretive work before the fiction can advance. In solo and low-referee play this most often takes the form of oracle outputs that read as tasks ("find a way to resolve the tension") rather than events ("the contact is already gone when you arrive"). In group play it shows up as rulings that generate procedural negotiation rather than fictional consequence. Either way, the authority mechanism has become an obstacle between fictional intent and fictional change rather than a conduit for it.

**Orientational divergence.** When participants at the same table hold different implicit models of what the game is doing (one treating it as a tactical puzzle, another as character exploration, another as improvised theater) the engine loses coherence even if every rule is working correctly. No rule can fix this, because no rule caused it. See the social dimension below.

## Examples

Three cases show where the framework's distinctions do their most useful work.

**A rule that belongs in a fiction engine.** When you force a locked door, roll. On a strong success, it opens cleanly. On a partial success, it opens but alerts someone nearby. On a failure, the lock breaks and something approaches. Every outcome is a new fictional state. The player knows at once what the situation has become and how play continues.

**A rule that generates dead overhead.** Track door integrity across five damage boxes. Once all boxes are filled, make a secondary roll to determine whether the door opens or jams. The procedure produces a count, then another roll, before the fiction can change. Neither step is a fictional event. Both are administrative steps between fictional intent and fictional consequence.

**A borderline case.** A stress track with no diegetic equivalent: it does not represent anything the other characters can observe. But as it fills, the character's actions become constrained in ways that match a deteriorating state. The track is non-diegetic in form but fiction-supporting in function. Whether it belongs in a fiction engine depends on whether its administrative cost is proportionate to the fictional coherence it maintains. That is a design judgment, not a rule violation.

The borderline case is where the framework does its most useful work, because it keeps the test from becoming a blunt instrument. The question is never whether a rule looks mechanical. It is whether the rule is carrying its weight, and in what currency.

## For designers

Every design decision can be held against a single test: does this rule produce a new fictional state, support the conditions for fictional play, or generate dead overhead?

Guidelines that follow from that test:

- Resolution should be short enough to remember without reference.
- Outcomes should advance the fiction rather than suspend it pending further resolution, and should be traceable to what the fiction has established.
- Fictional descriptors should do the work that numerical interfaces often claim to do: "she is exhausted and can barely lift her sword" carries more usable information than a stamina score of 3.
- Uncertainty should create stakes, not punishment.
- Cognitive load should be minimized everywhere it does not directly serve meaningful choice.
- The situation should remain more visible to the table than the system.

A useful diagnostic: remove a rule and observe whether the fiction still moves and remains coherent. If it does, the rule was dead overhead. If play stalls or loses legibility, the rule was load-bearing: either producing fictional change or supporting the conditions for it. Keep the load-bearing rules. Cut the rest.

## For players

The player's task inside a fiction engine is to make informed choices about what their character attempts, and to treat every outcome, including failure, as something that changes the situation rather than something that ends the scene.

Within this paradigm, the relevant question is what the character attempts from inside the situation, not how to optimize an external procedure. Consequences are part of the structure of play, not interruptions to it. When the fiction becomes unstable or surprising, the framework is working correctly.

A useful diagnostic: if you find yourself thinking about what the rules allow rather than what the character would do, the fiction has slipped. Pause, return to the situation, and restate it before continuing.

## The social dimension

A fiction engine works only when all participants are oriented toward the fiction as the primary object of play. Rules alone cannot secure this. It is a condition of the table that design can support but not guarantee.

When the orientation holds, the rules recede: participants engage with the situation, the situation transforms, and the mechanics become infrastructure they are aware of without attending to. When participants hold divergent models of what the game is doing, the fiction fragments because they are not holding it in common, however well the rules are designed.

Designers reduce this risk by making the game's orientation legible from the first page: what kind of object this is, what it asks of the people playing it, and what it will produce. Players reduce it by stating their expectations before play begins and by treating the fiction as a common object rather than a stage for individual expression. When it breaks, the diagnostic is not "which rule is wrong" but "which participant is somewhere else, and why."

A fiction engine is a social agreement before it is a mechanical system. The rules formalize that agreement, but they cannot substitute for it.

## Criteria

The core criteria for a fiction engine are procedural rather than scalar. A game belongs more fully to this framework when:

- Every rule either produces fictional change or supports the conditions for it.
- Procedures remain transparent in use: the table engages with the situation, not the system.
- Outcomes advance or complicate the fiction rather than suspending it pending further resolution, and can be traced to established fictional facts.
- It remains legible when the fiction becomes unstable or surprising.
- Players describe what happens in fictional terms rather than mechanical ones.
- The game makes its own orientation legible enough that all participants can hold the fiction as a common object rather than a personal stage.

Several qualities often accompany fiction engine design without being definitional to it. A fiction engine tends to be learnable quickly, needs little setup, and keeps cognitive load low. These are tendencies that follow from thin mediation, not requirements. A game with a rich setup procedure that establishes a deep fictional situation before play can still be a fiction engine, provided the play procedures serve the fiction once it is running.

Two qualifications keep the criteria honest. First, transparency is not a fixed property of the rules: the same procedure can demand attention in the first session and recede by the tenth. Where a design sits relative to the transparency threshold is a trajectory, not a point (Part II and Part III). Second, the criteria describe conditions, not guarantees. A design can meet every one of them and still fail to produce fiction-transparent play at a table that lacks the social agreement, has not finished learning the procedures, or resets its fiction every session. The criteria are necessary, not sufficient.

A game moves away from this framework when players spend more time talking about the system than about the situation. Moving away is not, by itself, a failure. It becomes one only when the design claims or requires fiction-transparency while its procedures consistently ask players to attend elsewhere.

## Conclusion

The fiction engine paradigm rests on a single bet: that a system transparent enough to disappear into the fiction produces better play, for the specific aims this paradigm serves, than one that makes its own operation the object of attention. Some games intentionally foreground procedure because procedure itself creates tension, ritual, thematic pressure, or strategic identity. That is a coherent choice, and it produces experiences a fiction engine is not designed to produce. Knowing what a design is optimized for is more useful than claiming universality.

The framework is most useful when it clarifies tradeoffs and least useful when it becomes a badge of identity. Its purpose is to clarify tradeoffs, not to certify purity.

A fiction engine is not an argument that stories can be mechanically controlled. It is a design approach in which the system is light enough, responsive enough, and fiction-facing enough that the story emerges through play rather than being processed through procedure. The rules are infrastructure. The fiction is the point.
