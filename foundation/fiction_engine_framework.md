# Fiction Engine: A Design Framework for Tabletop RPGs

*Version 1.0 — May 2026*

A fiction engine is a game design that treats play as a system for producing, sustaining, and transforming fiction with minimal friction. Its function is to keep the fiction moving while preserving uncertainty, agency, and consequence. The procedural layer between fictional intent and fictional change should be as thin as the situation allows. The rules are infrastructure, not experience.

## What is fiction

In this framework, fiction means the diegetic layer: the characters, places, events, and conditions that participants hold as real within the game. Some of this layer is shared by all participants at any given moment; some is held only by a subset — the referee's hidden map, an NPC's undisclosed motive, a player's private knowledge. Both are part of the fiction. The distinction is not between fiction and non-fiction but between what is currently known and what is not yet known within the diegetic layer. Fiction is not narrative craft, literary quality, or storytelling in the literary sense. It is the living, mutable situation that play produces and transforms moment to moment.

When this framework says a rule should serve the fiction, it means the rule should produce changes to the fiction rather than changes to a score, a resource pool, or an administrative record that exists outside it.

## What counts as fictional state

A fictional state is any condition that exists inside the fiction and is visible to participants as something they can see, respond to, and reason about within it. It is a property of the world, not a property of the game record.

A character's exhaustion is a fictional state when it is something other characters can observe, respond to, and factor into their decisions inside the fiction: she is pale, her movements are slow, she cannot lift the bar. It stops being a fictional state when it is only a number that modifies future rolls without producing any change to how the situation reads inside the world.

This distinction resolves several apparent edge cases:

**Positioning.** Fictional state when it describes where a character actually is and what that means for what they can do or see. Not fictional state when it is a grid coordinate with no imagined spatial equivalent.

**Inventory depletion.** Fictional state when it changes what the character can plausibly attempt inside the situation: she has no rope, so the climb is now different. Not fictional state when it is a count that has no consequence until a threshold is crossed arbitrarily.

**Emotional state.** Fictional state when it is a condition other characters can perceive and that shapes what the situation will accept as plausible action. Not fictional state when it is a tag attached to a character sheet with no behavioral consequence inside the fiction.

**Tactical or mechanical bonuses.** Not fictional state. A +1 ongoing is a modification to a future roll. It becomes fictional state only if it reflects something that exists inside the fiction — she has the high ground, she has momentum, the crowd is watching — and would be visible to anyone present in the scene.

The test in each case is the same: if you removed the game record and asked whether the condition still exists inside the fiction as something participants can see, respond to, and reason about, the answer tells you what kind of state it is. Fictional states survive that removal. Administrative records do not.

## Definition

The label "narrative game" is too broad to be analytically useful. Games grouped under that heading differ significantly in where they place authority, how they resolve uncertainty, and how much interpretive labor they require. A fiction engine is the design family defined by a single structural commitment: every rule exists to serve the fiction, and any rule that fails that test is a candidate for removal.

This commitment is structural, not stylistic. It applies to every design decision: resolution procedures, character traits, pacing tools, uncertainty mechanisms. Each one either produces fictional change, supports the conditions under which fictional change can occur, or generates dead overhead that serves neither purpose. The third category is what a fiction engine works to eliminate.

Importantly, this distinction operates on function rather than form. A rule that is non-diegetic in form — a stress track, a pacing clock, a campaign log — can still be fiction-supporting in function if it reliably preserves the coherence, legibility, or continuity that makes fictional change possible. The question is never whether a rule looks mechanical. It is whether the rule is carrying its weight in fictional terms.

## Position and scope

This framework describes one design paradigm among several. It is not a claim that all good tabletop RPG design should follow these principles, nor that other paradigms are deficient versions of this one. Other coherent approaches exist — games built around codified move triggers, managed pressure economies, explicit metacurrency, character-world interfaces, or world-as-procedure — and each makes tradeoffs that reflect a genuine design philosophy. Evaluating any of them using criteria borrowed from a different paradigm produces bad analysis.

Fiction-first design is not identical to the fiction engine paradigm. Many games prioritize fiction while still relying on highly formalized procedural grammars, codified move structures, explicit pacing economies, or persistent mechanical interfaces that remain foregrounded during play. A fiction engine describes the narrower subset of designs that attempt to minimize mediation itself, keeping the procedural layer as transparent as possible while still preserving uncertainty and consequence. That narrowness is a deliberate tradeoff, not a judgment on fiction-oriented systems that sit outside it.

This framework does not measure the quality of a game. It measures the degree to which a design serves fiction as the primary object of play by minimizing the mediation between fictional intent and fictional change. When a design is trying to produce something else (pressure, strategy, explicit authorship, puzzle-solving, character construction, mastery, ritual tension) the framework must recognize a change of paradigm, not treat the design as a degraded fiction engine.

A foregrounded procedure is not therefore a bad procedure. It is a procedure buying something with attention. The relevant question is not whether the procedure is transparent in the abstract, but whether the experience it purchases is the one the design actually wants. Fiction-transparency is a commitment, not a universal virtue.

A fiction engine has no winning condition. The fiction moves because the situation keeps changing (outcomes produce new fictional states that open into further play), not because participants are working toward a predetermined resolution. A fiction engine produces a situation that responds to what participants do, without building toward a predetermined endpoint. This is different from a design that uses fiction as the medium for a competitive or goal-directed experience, where fictional actions accumulate toward a resolution condition that exists outside the fiction itself. Designs of that kind are not worse designs; they are built around a different structural logic. But that logic is not the fiction engine's logic, and evaluating either by the other's criteria produces bad analysis. The distinction is not incidental. It is load-bearing.

What this framework offers is a precise description of a specific approach: its structural commitments, its operational logic, and its tradeoffs. The fiction engine paradigm values thin mediation, fictional momentum, and minimal procedural overhead. It accepts, as a consequence of those values, that it will provide less structural scaffolding than paradigms built around dramatic prescription or managed pressure. That is not a flaw. It is the shape of the tradeoff.

A reader coming from a different design tradition should treat this document as a map of one region, not a map of the whole territory.

## How to use this framework

This framework serves three distinct purposes, and a reader can apply it in any of them.

**As a design checklist.** When building a game, use the criteria and failure modes sections later in this document to evaluate individual rules and the overall system. The central test — does this rule produce a new fictional state or overhead? — applies to every design decision.

**As an evaluative lens.** When analyzing an existing game, use the definition and criteria sections to determine how fully it belongs to this paradigm. A game that does not meet these criteria is not necessarily a worse game, only a game built around a different engine of play. The lens should be used diagnostically, not as a ranking device. Its purpose is to identify what kind of experience a structure is optimized to produce, and whether its procedures serve that aim.

**As a shared vocabulary.** When discussing design decisions with other designers or players, this framework offers precise terms for concepts that are often discussed imprecisely: fictional intent, the fiction, the diegetic layer, overhead, suspended outcomes, authority drift. All of these are defined in the glossary. Using them consistently makes design conversations more productive by reducing the ambiguity that labels like "narrative" or "story-driven" carry.

## Core operation

A fiction engine turns fictional intent into fictional change.

The player states what their character attempts within the fiction. The game produces an outcome. That outcome modifies the fiction. Play continues. This cycle should run with as little procedural overhead as the situation allows.

A fiction engine does not require translation into detailed tactical subsystems, elaborate metacurrency loops, or dense narrative grammar. It does not ask players to negotiate with the rules themselves in order to advance a scene. This is not a position against complexity. It is a position against complexity that produces neither fictional output nor the conditions for it. A rule can be intricate and still belong to a fiction engine, provided it either changes the shared imagined space directly or preserves the coherence, pacing, or legibility that makes fictional change possible.

The goal is not to simulate everything, nor to formalize everything. The goal is to maintain momentum.

## Range of application

This framework applies across formats and scales. A fiction engine is as valid for a solo player as for a group of five. It applies to a one-shot as readily as to a multi-session campaign. It is genre-neutral: horror, comedy, space opera, and intimate drama can all be built as fiction engines without modification to the core commitment.

What changes across formats is how authority is distributed and what mechanisms sustain uncertainty. Solo play, small groups, and large tables each solve these problems differently. The framework does not prescribe those solutions. It asks only that whatever mechanism is used produces fictional output and preserves the conditions for meaningful play.

## How outcomes function

A fiction engine treats outcomes as continuations, not endpoints.

The productive question after any resolution is not simply whether an action succeeded. It is: what new fictional state does this result create, and how does play proceed from there? The range of possible outcomes — yes and, yes but, no but, no and — each forces the fiction to keep changing. Each opens the next move rather than closing the last one.

A result that leaves the fiction suspended rather than transformed indicates a design problem, not a neutral state.

## Authority and procedure

Fiction engines distribute narrative authority in different ways. Some use a referee. Some use shared authorship. Some use structured uncertainty mechanisms such as oracles in solo or low-referee play. All of these are workable configurations. What the rules must do in each case is make the distribution of authority visible and operable, rather than leaving it to convention or improvisation.

The principle that applies across all configurations is the same: whatever mechanism handles authority must produce fictional output rather than administrative prompts. When the procedure begins to dominate the fiction — when the table is spending more time tracking states, resolving subsystems, or consulting tables than engaging with the situation — the engine is no longer functioning as intended.

## Failure modes

A fiction engine can fail in characteristic ways that are worth naming directly.

**Overhead accumulation.** Rules that generate tracking obligations, resource management, or procedural steps without producing fictional output are overhead. Individually each may seem minor. Cumulatively they shift the table's attention from the situation to the system. The engine stalls not through a single bad rule but through the weight of many marginal ones.

**Suspended outcomes.** A resolution that produces a result requiring further resolution before play can continue breaks the engine's fundamental cycle. Designs that chain resolution steps — roll to attempt, then roll to determine degree, then consult a table to translate degree into fictional terms — are prone to this. Each additional step is a potential suspension point.

**Authority drift.** Any mechanism for distributing narrative authority — a referee, a shared authorship structure, an oracle, a randomized prompt system — can drift from producing fictional output toward producing administrative prompts that require significant interpretive work before the fiction can advance. In solo and low-referee play this most commonly takes the form of oracle outputs that read as tasks rather than events. In group play it manifests as referee rulings that generate procedural negotiation rather than fictional consequence. The failure mode is the same across configurations: the authority mechanism has become an obstacle between fictional intent and fictional change rather than a conduit for it.

**Orientational divergence.** When participants at the same table hold different implicit models of what the game is doing — one treating it as a tactical puzzle, another as collaborative story, another as character study — the engine loses coherence even if all the rules are functioning correctly. This failure mode is addressed in the social dimension section below.

## Examples

Three cases illustrate where the framework's distinctions do their most useful work.

**A rule that clearly belongs in a fiction engine.** When you force a locked door, roll. On a strong success, it opens cleanly. On a partial success, it opens but alerts someone nearby. On a failure, the lock breaks and something approaches. Every outcome is a new fictional state. The player knows immediately what the situation has become and how play continues.

**A rule that generates dead overhead.** Track door integrity across five damage boxes. Once all boxes are filled, make a secondary roll to determine whether the door opens or jams. The procedure produces a number, then another roll, before the fiction can change. Neither step is a fictional event. Both are administrative steps between fictional intent and fictional consequence.

**A borderline case.** A stress track that has no diegetic equivalent — it does not represent anything the character experiences inside the fiction — but reliably produces visible changes in character behavior as it fills. The track is non-diegetic in form but fiction-supporting in function. It belongs to the second category: infrastructure that preserves the conditions for meaningful play rather than changing the fiction directly. Whether it belongs in a fiction engine depends on whether its administrative cost is proportionate to the fictional coherence it maintains. That is a design judgment, not a rule violation.

The borderline case is where the framework does its most useful work, because it forces the question: is this rule carrying its weight, and in what currency?

## For designers

Every design decision can be held against a single test: does this rule produce a new fictional state, support the conditions for fictional play, or generate dead overhead?

Guidelines that follow from that test:

- Resolution should be short enough to remember without reference.
- Outcomes should advance the fiction rather than suspend it pending further resolution.
- Fictional descriptors should do the work that numerical interfaces often claim to do: "she is exhausted and can barely lift her sword" carries more usable information than a stamina score of 3.
- Uncertainty should create stakes, not punishment.
- Cognitive load should be minimized everywhere it does not directly serve meaningful choice.
- The situation should remain more visible to the table than the system.

A useful diagnostic: remove a rule and observe whether the fiction still moves and remains coherent. If it does, the rule was dead overhead. If play stalls or loses legibility, the rule was load-bearing: either producing fictional change or supporting the conditions for it. Keep the load-bearing rules. Cut the rest.

## For players

The player's task inside a fiction engine is to make informed choices about what their character attempts and to treat every outcome — including failure — as something that changes the situation rather than something that ends the scene.

Within this paradigm, the relevant question is usually what the character attempts from inside the situation, not how to optimise an external procedure. Consequences are part of the structure of play, not interruptions to it. When the fiction becomes unstable or surprising, that is the framework operating correctly.

A useful diagnostic: if you find yourself thinking about what the rules allow rather than what the character would do, the fiction has slipped. Pause, return to the situation, and restate it before continuing.

## The social dimension

A fiction engine functions only when all participants are oriented toward the fiction as the primary object of play. This is not a design problem that rules alone can solve. It is a table culture condition that design can support but not guarantee.

When participants hold divergent models of what the game is doing — one treating it as a tactical puzzle, another as character exploration, another as improvised theater — the engine loses coherence independently of whether the rules themselves are well designed. The fiction fragments because participants are not holding it in common.

Designers can reduce this risk by making the game's orientation legible from the first page: what kind of object is this, what does it ask of you, and what will it produce. Players can reduce it by stating their expectations before play begins and by treating the fiction as a genuine common object rather than a stage for individual expression.

A fiction engine is a social agreement before it is a mechanical system. The rules formalize that agreement, but they cannot substitute for it.

## Criteria

The core criteria for a fiction engine are procedural rather than scalar. A game belongs more fully to this framework when:

- Every rule either produces fictional change or supports the conditions for it.
- Procedures remain transparent in use: the table engages with the situation, not the system.
- Outcomes advance or complicate the fiction rather than suspending it pending further resolution.
- It remains legible when the fiction becomes unstable or surprising.
- Players describe what happens in fictional terms rather than mechanical ones.
- The game makes its own orientation legible enough that all participants can hold the fiction as a common object rather than a personal stage.

Several qualities often accompany fiction engine design without being definitional to it. A fiction engine tends to be learnable quickly, requires little setup, and keeps cognitive load low. These are common tendencies that follow from the commitment to thin mediation, not requirements of the paradigm itself. A game with a rich setup procedure that establishes a deep fictional situation before play begins can still be a fiction engine, provided the play procedures themselves serve the fiction once it is running.

A game moves away from this framework when players spend more time talking about the system than about the situation. Moving away from this framework is not, by itself, a failure. It becomes a failure only when the design claims or requires fiction-transparency while its procedures consistently ask players to attend elsewhere.

## Glossary

**Diegetic.** Belonging to the fiction: the layer of characters, places, events, and conditions that all participants hold in common and treat as real within the game. A diegetic element — a character's wound, a crumbling building, a political alliance — exists inside the fiction. A non-diegetic element — a hit point total, a fate point, a stress track — exists outside the fiction as part of the game's administrative layer. Fiction engines aim to keep as much as possible diegetic.

**Fictional intent.** What a player's character is attempting within the fiction. Fictional intent is the input to any resolution procedure: the player describes what they want to do inside the fiction, and the game produces a fictional outcome in response.

**Oracle.** One mechanism for distributing narrative authority in solo or low-referee play. An oracle typically takes a closed question about the fiction — does the door open? is the guard suspicious? — and produces a yes or no answer, often with gradations or complications. It functions as a fiction engine component when its outputs read as fictional events rather than prompts requiring interpretive work before play can continue. Other authority mechanisms — referees, shared authorship structures, randomized prompt systems — are subject to the same functional test.

**Overhead.** Procedural steps, tracking obligations, or resource management layers that neither produce a change to the fiction nor support the conditions under which fictional change can occur. This document distinguishes dead overhead from fiction-supporting infrastructure: safety tools, pacing mechanisms, campaign logs, and similar systems may be non-diegetic while still being load-bearing. Dead overhead is the target for removal; fiction-supporting infrastructure is not.

**Referee.** The participant whose role is to mediate between the fiction and the other players: describing the world's response to player action, adjudicating outcomes, and maintaining the consistency of the fiction. Some games distribute this role across all participants; others eliminate it in favor of procedural tools such as oracles.

## Conclusion

The fiction engine paradigm rests on a single bet: that a system transparent enough to disappear into the fiction produces better play — for the specific aims this paradigm serves — than one that makes its own operation the object of attention. This does not mean that other paradigms are wrong. Some games intentionally foreground procedure because procedure itself creates tension, ritual, thematic pressure, or strategic identity. That is a coherent design choice, and it produces experiences a fiction engine is not designed to produce. Different designs are optimized for different things, and knowing what your design is optimized for is more useful than claiming universality.

The framework is most useful when it clarifies tradeoffs, and least useful when it becomes a badge of identity. Its purpose is to clarify tradeoffs, not to certify purity.

A fiction engine is not an argument that stories can be mechanically controlled. It is a design approach in which the system is light enough, responsive enough, and fiction-facing enough that the story appears to emerge through play rather than being processed through procedure. The rules are infrastructure. The fiction is the point.
