# The Bet

_A note on terms: this collection of essays uses several concepts precisely enough that imprecision creates confusion. Fiction means the shared diegetic situation, not narrative craft. Fictional state means a condition inside the fiction visible to the characters within it, not a note in the game record. Overhead means procedural steps that produce neither fictional change nor the conditions for it — a narrower category than it might appear. These are not terms in their everyday senses. A glossary at the back defines each one; the essays will earn the terms as they use them._

"Narrative game" is too broad to be analytically useful.

Games grouped under that label differ in where they place authority, how they resolve uncertainty, and how much interpretive labor they require. A game where a referee adjudicates outcomes freely against the situation is doing something structurally different from one where codified move triggers govern what the fiction can produce. A game where players hold authorial leverage over their story's shape through a metacurrency is doing something structurally different again from one where all fictional changes emerge from in-world cause and effect. None of these differences is captured by "narrative." The label names a loose family resemblance — these games care about story in some sense — without describing what any of them actually does or how it does it.

A fiction engine is the design family defined by one structural commitment: every rule exists to serve the fiction, and any rule that fails that test is a candidate for removal. That commitment is not stylistic. It applies to every design decision — resolution procedures, character traits, pacing tools, uncertainty mechanisms — and the test applies to each one individually and to the system as a whole. The label is a tool for precision, not a claim that this family is superior to others. It names one design approach, with a specific logic and specific costs, in order to describe that approach clearly enough to reason about it.

The rest of this collection is what the commitment means in practice. But two words in it — rules and fiction — are doing more specific work than they appear to, and the argument depends on keeping them precise.

## What fiction means

In what follows, fiction does not mean literary craft, narrative quality, or storytelling in the writerly sense. It means the shared situation: the characters, places, events, and conditions that all participants hold in common during play and treat as real within the game. It is the living, mutable situation that play produces and transforms, moment to moment. When a character forces a door and it gives way noisily, alerting the guard upstairs — that is a change to the fiction. When the player marks off two hit points and the scene continues exactly as it would have without that mark — that is not.

This distinction is the load-bearing one. A fictional state is any condition inside the shared situation that the characters can see, respond to, and reason about. A character's exhaustion is a fictional state when it makes her slow, when the other characters notice, when it shapes what she can plausibly attempt. It stops being a fictional state when it is only a number on a sheet that modifies future rolls without changing how the situation reads inside the world. The test is simple: remove the game record and ask whether the condition still exists inside the fiction as something participants can respond to. Fictional states survive that removal. Administrative records do not.

Everything in this collection follows from that test.

## The single structural commitment

A fiction engine takes that test seriously and applies it to every rule.

The design family is defined by one commitment: every rule exists to serve the fiction, and any rule that fails that test is a candidate for removal. This does not mean the game must be simple, or spare, or without texture. A rule can be intricate and still pass the test, provided it produces fictional change or supports the conditions under which fictional change can occur. What the commitment refuses is a third category: rules that produce neither, that exist as administrative overhead between fictional intent and fictional consequence.

The core operation a fiction engine runs is: the player states what their character attempts within the fiction; the game produces an outcome; that outcome modifies the fiction; play continues. The procedural layer in the middle should be as thin as the situation allows. Not absent — uncertainty and consequence both require procedure. Thin. The rules handle the friction between intent and outcome, then get out of the way.

Three examples show where the distinction does its most useful work.

**A rule that belongs.** When you force a locked door, roll. On a strong success, it opens cleanly. On a partial success, it opens but alerts someone nearby. On a failure, the lock breaks and something approaches. Each result is a new fictional state. The player knows immediately what the situation has become and how play continues.

**A rule that doesn't.** Track door integrity across five damage boxes. Once all boxes are filled, make a secondary roll to determine whether the door opens or jams. The procedure produces a count, then another roll, before the fiction changes. Neither step is a fictional event. Both are administrative steps interposed between fictional intent and fictional consequence.

**The borderline case — where the framework does its most useful work.** A stress track with no direct expression inside the fiction: no condition the other characters can observe. But as it fills, the character's actions become constrained in ways that match a deteriorating state. The track is non-diegetic in form — it exists on the sheet, not in the world — but fiction-supporting in function. It is preserving the coherence of a condition the fiction needs. Whether it belongs in a fiction engine depends on whether its administrative cost is proportionate to the fictional coherence it maintains. That is a design judgment, not a rule violation.

The borderline case matters because it prevents the test from becoming a blunt instrument. The question is never whether a rule looks mechanical. It is whether the rule is carrying its weight, and in what currency.

## How outcomes work

A fiction engine treats outcomes as continuations, not endpoints.

The productive question after any resolution is not simply whether an action succeeded. It is: what new fictional state does this result create, and how does play proceed from there? The full range of outcomes — yes and, yes but, no but, no and — each forces the fiction to keep changing. Each opens the next move rather than closing the last one.

A result that leaves the fiction suspended rather than transformed is a design problem, not a neutral state. A resolution chain that produces a number, then requires another roll to interpret the number, then requires the referee to translate the interpretation into fictional terms, has three suspension points where one would be enough. Each step is a moment where the fiction waits on the procedure. The procedure should be doing the waiting instead.

## What can go wrong

A fiction engine fails in characteristic ways.

**Overhead accumulation.** Rules that generate tracking obligations without producing fictional output are overhead. Individually each may seem minor. Cumulatively, they shift the table's attention from the situation to the system. The engine stalls not through a single bad rule but through the weight of many marginal ones.

**Suspended outcomes.** Resolution that produces a result requiring further resolution before play can continue breaks the fundamental cycle. The fiction stops while the procedure runs. Run enough of those stops in a session and the procedure has become the primary object of attention.

**Authority drift.** Any mechanism that handles narrative authority — a referee, an oracle, a randomized prompt system — can drift from producing fictional events toward producing administrative prompts that require significant interpretive work before the fiction can advance. The oracle output that reads as a task ("find a way to resolve the tension") rather than an event ("the contact is already gone when you arrive") is the clearest case. The failure mode is the same across configurations: the authority mechanism has become an obstacle between fictional intent and fictional change rather than a conduit for it.

**Orientational divergence.** When participants hold different models of what the game is doing — one treating it as a tactical puzzle, another as character exploration, another as improvised theater — the engine loses coherence even when the rules are functioning correctly. The fiction fragments because participants are not holding it in common. No rule can fix this, because no rule caused it.

## The social dimension

A fiction engine is a social agreement before it is a mechanical system.

It functions only when all participants are oriented toward the fiction as the primary object of play. When that orientation holds, the rules recede: participants engage with the situation, the situation transforms, and the mechanics become infrastructure they are aware of without attending to. When it breaks — when one player is running a different game in their head — the procedures cannot compensate. The rules formalize the agreement. They cannot substitute for it.

Designers reduce this risk by making the game's orientation legible from the first page: what kind of object this is, what it asks of the people playing it, what it will produce. Players reduce it by stating their expectations before play begins and by treating the fiction as a common object. The diagnostic when it breaks is not "which rule is wrong" but "which participant is somewhere else, and why."

## One paradigm among several

Nothing in this argument implies that other design approaches are deficient.

Some games intentionally foreground procedure because procedure itself creates the experience: tension under managed risk, strategic identity, the ritual weight of dramatic grammar. These are coherent design achievements, optimized for things a fiction engine is not designed to produce. Evaluating them using criteria borrowed from a different paradigm produces bad analysis. Evaluating a fiction engine against criteria built for a pressure economy produces equally bad analysis in the other direction.

This is not a claim that all good tabletop RPG design should converge on thin mediation. It is a precise description of one design commitment, its structural logic, and the costs it accepts.

The Fiction Engine Framework does not measure the quality of a game. It measures the degree to which a structure serves fiction as the primary object of play. When a design is trying to produce something else (pressure, strategy, explicit authorship, puzzle-solving, character construction, mastery, ritual tension) the framework must recognize a change of paradigm, not treat the design as a degraded fiction engine.

A rule that foregrounds procedure is not therefore a bad rule. It is a rule buying something with attention. The relevant question is not whether the rule is transparent, but whether the experience it purchases is the one the design actually wants. Fiction-transparency is a commitment, not a universal virtue.

A fiction engine accepts, as the direct consequence of minimal overhead, that it provides less structural scaffolding than paradigms built around dramatic prescription or managed pressure. It provides no move list to generate consistent dramatic rhythm. It provides no metacurrency to give players authorial leverage over story shape. It generates no pressure economy. Those are costs, and they are real. Tables that need the system to provide momentum and structure will find a fiction engine sparse.

What it offers in return is a procedural layer thin enough to disappear into the fiction — thin enough that the story appears to emerge through play rather than being processed through procedure. That is the bet this book is built around. The chapters that follow are what the bet costs, what it produces, and how far it travels.
