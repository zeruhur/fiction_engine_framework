# **Thresholds**

*A fiction-engine tabletop RPG ruleset*

## 1. What this game is

**Thresholds** is a rules-light tabletop RPG about characters entering unstable situations and changing them through play.

The rules do not try to model the world exhaustively. They answer one question:

> **When a character tries something uncertain and consequential, what happens in the fiction next?**

There are no hit points, classes, levels, action economies, tactical grids, fate points, or victory conditions. Characters are defined by fictional facts. Harm, advantage, danger, equipment, exhaustion, fear, reputation, trust, and opportunity all matter only insofar as they exist inside the fiction and change what can plausibly happen.

The referee’s role is to keep the world coherent, responsive, and surprising. The players’ role is to say what their characters attempt from inside the situation.

---

# 2. Materials

You need:

* two six-sided dice
* paper or a shared document
* one referee and one or more players
* or, for solo/low-referee play, the oracle rules in section 14

---

# 3. The basic cycle of play

Play proceeds in a simple loop.

1. **The referee describes the situation.**
2. **A player says what their character does.**
3. **The table decides whether the outcome is obvious, impossible, or uncertain.**
4. **If obvious, it happens.**
5. **If impossible, the referee says why and what would need to change.**
6. **If uncertain and consequential, roll.**
7. **The result creates a new fictional state.**
8. **Play continues from that changed situation.**

Do not roll to see whether play can continue. Roll only when the result will change the situation.

---

# 4. Characters

A character sheet contains only fictional facts.

Each player creates a character by answering these prompts.

## Name

Who are you called?

## Place in the world

What are you, socially or practically?

Examples:

* village surgeon
* disgraced knight
* smuggler-priest
* apprentice weather witch
* widowed cartographer
* deserter from the imperial army

## Three truths

Write three things that are true about your character.

At least one should be a **capability**.
At least one should be a **connection**.
At least one should be a **vulnerability, burden, or complication**.

Examples:

* I can read old military fortifications.
* The dockworkers of Greyharbor still trust me.
* I panic when trapped underground.
* I carry my brother’s sword, though I never learned to use it properly.
* I know the songs that calm the dead.
* The magistrate believes I murdered her son.

These are not bonuses. They are facts. They determine what your character can do, what is easy, what is dangerous, who responds to them, and what consequences make sense.

## Visible condition

Begin with one visible condition that tells the table how the character currently appears.

Examples:

* road-worn and hungry
* dressed above their station
* cheerful despite recent grief
* limping from an old injury
* alert, clean, and prepared

This condition may change during play.

## Belongings

List what the character is carrying that matters.

Do not list every mundane object unless it is important. A character plausibly equipped for their role may have ordinary tools of that role unless the fiction says otherwise.

Examples:

* a surgeon’s satchel
* a patched traveling cloak
* a rusted pistol with one shot prepared
* three letters of introduction
* a mule named Candle
* lockpicks hidden in a boot heel

# 5. The character sheet

A character sheet looks like this:

```text
Name:
Place in the world:

Truths:
1.
2.
3.

Visible condition:

Belongings:

Current states:
-
-
-
```

**Current states** are fictional conditions created during play.

Examples:

* bleeding from the ribs
* trusted by the miller’s family
* carrying a stolen temple key
* soaked, freezing, and shivering
* marked by the ash-spirit
* wanted in Eastgate
* separated from the others
* holding the bridge alone

A state stays on the sheet only while it is true inside the fiction.

# 6. When to roll

Roll only when all three are true:

1. A character is attempting something specific.
2. The outcome is uncertain.
3. The outcome matters.

Do **not** roll for routine competence.
Do **not** roll for actions whose consequences are uninteresting.
Do **not** roll when the fiction already decides the answer.

A former locksmith opening a cheap lock in a quiet room opens it.
A former locksmith opening an ancient lock while guards climb the stairs rolls.
A farmer with no tools trying to open the same ancient lock may need to find another approach first.

The player always states both:

> **Intent:** what they want to accomplish.
> **Method:** how they try to do it.

“I get past the guard” is incomplete.
“I flatter the guard by pretending to recognize his military service” is playable.

---

# 7. Before the roll

Before rolling, the referee should make the stakes clear enough that the player understands the risk.

Use plain fictional language.

Good:

> “You can try to cross the rotten beam, but if it gives way you may fall into the flooded chamber below.”

Good:

> “You can threaten him, but if he does not break, he will know you are desperate.”

Bad:

> “Failing this will give you a penalty later.”

Bad:

> “This will fill two boxes on the complication track.”

The player may change their approach after hearing the risk.

---

# 8. The roll

When a roll is needed, roll **2d6**.

Read the result:

## 10 or more: **Yes, and**

The character’s intent succeeds, and the situation improves, opens, or clarifies.

Examples:

* The door opens silently, and you hear the guards before they reach the hall.
* The crowd believes you, and someone steps forward with useful information.
* You drive the beast back, and it exposes the chain around its throat.
* You cross the ravine, and the rope remains secure for the others.

## 7–9: **Yes, but / No, but**

The situation changes, but imperfectly.

Usually, the character succeeds with a cost, delay, exposure, hard choice, or partial effect.

Sometimes, especially when the opposition is strong or the method is weak, the character does not get what they wanted, but they gain something useful, avoid the worst, or reveal a new way forward.

Examples:

* The door opens, but the hinges shriek.
* The guard lets you pass, but only if you leave your weapon with him.
* You wound the beast, but it pins you beneath its weight.
* You do not convince the magistrate, but her clerk secretly believes you.
* You leap the gap, but your pack tears loose and falls.

## 6 or less: **No, and**

The character’s intent fails, or succeeds too late, and the situation worsens.

This does not stop play. It creates a new fictional state.

Examples:

* The lock breaks, and someone begins opening the door from the other side.
* The guard calls for help, and now the courtyard is waking.
* The beast knocks you down and closes its jaws around your arm.
* The magistrate names you a liar in front of the court.
* The beam snaps; you fall into the black water below.

Every result must answer:

> **What is now true in the fiction?**

---

# 9. How character truths affect play

Character truths never add numbers to the roll.

They affect the fiction directly.

A relevant truth can do any of the following:

## Make an action possible

A character who knows the songs that calm the dead can attempt to quiet a restless ghost. Another character may not be able to try without first learning, bargaining, or using some other method.

## Make an action automatic

A trained sailor ties a reliable knot in ordinary conditions. No roll.

## Change the stakes

A battlefield surgeon treating a wound under pressure still rolls, but failure may mean “the patient survives with a fever” rather than “the patient dies immediately.”

## Change the available consequences

If a character is known and loved in a village, failure in a social confrontation may create disappointment, debt, or shame rather than immediate violence.

## Invite trouble

A vulnerability is also real. If a character panics underground, then being trapped in a mine changes what they can plausibly do until someone steadies them or they regain control.

The rule is simple:

> If a trait is true, let it be true before touching the dice.

---

# 10. Advantage and disadvantage

Do not use numerical modifiers.

Instead, describe what is actually true.

If the character has a clear advantage, the referee should usually do one of these:

* allow success without a roll
* reduce the danger of failure
* make success more complete
* offer more information before the choice
* let the character attempt something otherwise impossible

If the character is clearly hindered, the referee should usually do one of these:

* require a different approach first
* make the possible result narrower
* make the danger sharper
* say the action is impossible until the fiction changes

Examples:

* High ground is not “+1.” It means enemies must climb, arrows have clearer lines, and retreat may be easier.
* Darkness is not “disadvantage.” It means you cannot see the pit, faces are hard to read, and anyone silent may vanish.
* A fine sword is not a damage bonus. It cuts mail poorly, bites flesh cleanly, impresses duelists, and may be recognized.

---

# 11. Harm

There are no hit points.

When someone is harmed, say what happened to them in the fiction.

Examples:

* cut across the forehead, blood in the eyes
* winded and unable to speak
* left hand crushed
* arrow through the thigh
* terrified and backing away
* coughing black smoke
* concussed and confused
* dying unless treated now

A harm state matters because it changes what the character can do.

A character with an arrow through the thigh cannot sprint.
A character coughing black smoke cannot hide quietly.
A character terrified of the thing in the cellar may freeze, flee, bargain, or need help before facing it.

## Severe harm

When a consequence could kill, maim, break, or permanently change a character, the referee should make that risk visible before the roll unless it is already obvious.

Good:

> “If you hold the door alone, you can buy them time, but the ogre may crush you against the frame.”

Good:

> “You can drink from the silver pool, but if the stories are true, it may change what you are.”

Death is allowed when it follows honestly from the fiction, but it should not arrive as a hidden mechanical surprise.

## Recovery

Harm changes when the fiction changes.

A wound may need pressure, stitching, rest, medicine, magic, shelter, or time.
A fear may need reassurance, distance, victory, confession, or ritual.
A broken reputation may need restitution, proof, sacrifice, or public witness.

Erase or alter a harm state only when something inside the fiction has addressed it.

---

# 12. Conflict

There is no separate combat system.

A fight is just a dangerous situation where people are trying to change the fiction by force.

Do not roll for every swing. Roll when an action could meaningfully alter the situation.

Examples of meaningful combat intents:

* I drive him away from the doorway.
* I get inside his spear reach.
* I hold the stairs until the others escape.
* I disarm her without killing her.
* I put an arrow in the lantern above them.
* I tackle the assassin before he reaches the prince.
* I kill the wolf before it drags the child into the trees.

On a hit, create a new state.
On a partial, create a new state with cost or complication.
On a miss, create a new state that worsens the situation.

Possible combat states:

* disarmed
* cornered
* bleeding heavily
* separated from allies
* shield split
* weapon lodged in bone
* pinned beneath a body
* enemy morale breaking
* reinforcements arriving
* fire spreading through the room
* hostage exposed
* dead

The referee should treat weapons according to what they are.

A knife in a crowded room is different from a spear in a doorway.
A pistol is different before and after it has fired.
A warhammer against plate armor is different from a rapier in a duel.

Do not translate weapons into damage numbers. Let them change what is plausible.

---

# 13. Groups, help, and opposition

## Helping

A character can help if they describe how their action changes the situation.

Good help:

* “I hold the lantern low so she can see the tripwire.”
* “I keep talking so the guard looks at me instead of him.”
* “I brace the door with my shoulder while she lifts the bar.”

Helping does not add a bonus. It changes what is possible, what is risky, or what success looks like.

If helping exposes the helper to danger, say so before the roll.

## Group action

When several characters act together toward one intent, make one roll for the shared attempt.

Before rolling, establish:

* who is most exposed
* what each character is contributing
* what success changes
* what failure threatens

The result affects the group according to the fiction.

## Opposition

NPCs do not roll against players by default.

When an NPC acts, the referee describes what they do and what danger or opportunity it creates. If a player resists, interrupts, exploits, or endures that action, the player may roll.

Example:

> Referee: “The captain orders two soldiers to bar the gate while she draws her pistol. If no one stops them, the gate will be sealed.”

A player might then say:

> “I rush the soldiers before the bar drops.”

Now there is a roll.

# 14. NPCs and creatures

NPCs and creatures are written as fictional profiles, not stat blocks.

Use this format:

```text
Name:
What they want:
What they can do:
What shows on the surface:
What they know:
What they fear or need:
What would change them:
```

Example:

```text
Name: Captain Ilyra Voss
What she wants: Keep the refugees outside the city until dawn.
What she can do: Command frightened soldiers; read military deception; shoot calmly at close range.
What shows on the surface: Controlled voice, sleepless eyes, hand always near pistol.
What she knows: The plague is already inside the city.
What she fears or needs: Proof that opening the gate will not start a riot.
What would change her: Public support from the temple, evidence of a worse threat outside, or someone taking responsibility before witnesses.
```

A creature may be written the same way.

```text
Name: The Bell-Stag
What it wants: To drive intruders away from the ruined chapel.
What it can do: Move silently through fog; gore a horse; ring the iron bells grown into its antlers.
What shows on the surface: Wet fur, blind white eyes, bells that sound underwater.
What it knows: The chapel crypt is open.
What it fears or needs: Fire blessed with a funeral prayer.
What would change it: Returning the stolen bell-clapper to the grave below.
```

If an NPC is injured, persuaded, frightened, trapped, exposed, or killed, write that as a fictional state.

---

# 15. Equipment and resources

Track objects, not abstract inventory points.

A thing matters if its presence or absence changes what can happen.

Examples:

* rope
* lantern oil
* court clothing
* a forged seal
* a loaded pistol
* a winter cloak
* a sacred relic
* the last clean bandage

For ordinary belongings, use common sense. A traveling healer probably has bandages. A naked prisoner does not.

For scarce things, track the actual fiction.

Good:

* one torch left
* pistol unloaded
* waterskin empty
* mule carrying the heavy chest
* rope cut and hanging from the tower

Avoid:

* Supply 3
* Ammo 2
* Gear points
* Wealth rating

Money may be recorded when the exact amount matters inside the fiction. Otherwise, describe economic position.

Examples:

* broke and owing rent
* comfortable but watched by creditors
* rich in land, poor in coin
* carrying a purse heavy enough to attract thieves

---

# 16. Magic, powers, and extraordinary abilities

Magic is treated as part of the fiction, not as a spell list unless the setting demands one.

A magical ability should answer:

```text
What can it change?
What does it require?
What does it look, sound, or feel like?
What can go wrong?
What does it cost inside the fiction?
```

Example:

```text
Ash-speech
What it can change: Lets the caster speak with the recent dead through their ashes.
What it requires: A named corpse, a flame, and a question spoken aloud.
What it looks like: The ash rises into the shape of a mouth.
What can go wrong: The wrong dead answer, the corpse remembers pain, or something nearby learns the caster’s name.
What it costs: The caster’s voice becomes smoke-rough until they sleep.
```

Do not count spell slots unless the count itself is fictional.

Acceptable:

* three blessed matches
* one oath remaining before the saint turns away
* the moon will not answer again tonight
* the demon demands a secret each time

Avoid:

* 4 mana
* 2 spell points
* once per scene
* recharge on a short rest

A magical result should always create a fictional state.

---

# 17. Social play

Social actions are resolved the same way as any other action.

The player must say what their character says, offers, hides, threatens, proves, or reveals.

Do not roll to “be persuasive” in the abstract. Roll when a specific approach meets uncertain resistance.

NPCs are not puppets. A roll cannot make someone do something that the fiction gives them no reason to do. It can create pressure, trust, fear, obligation, confusion, temptation, shame, or opportunity.

Examples:

* The duke will not abandon his city because of one speech.
* The duke might delay the execution if presented with evidence.
* The guard will not betray her sister for a bribe.
* The guard might look away if convinced the prisoner is already doomed.
* The mob will not become calm because someone rolled well.
* The mob might split, hesitate, or turn its anger toward a new target.

On a partial success, social consequences should remain social and fictional.

Examples:

* They agree, but want a witness.
* They believe you, but ask how you know.
* They let you pass, but remember your face.
* They keep your secret, but now you owe them.
* They are moved, but someone else in the room is offended.

---

# 18. Exploration and discovery

The referee describes what characters could notice from their position, senses, tools, and knowledge.

Do not hide essential information behind a failed roll. If the characters need a fact to make meaningful choices, give them the fact through the fiction.

Roll when the act of discovery is uncertain and consequential.

Examples:

* searching the room before the guards return
* reading the old map while rain destroys the ink
* following tracks through a storm
* examining a corpse without attracting the family’s attention
* listening at a door while something listens back

A discovery roll should produce discovery, danger, or both.

10+:

> You find the hidden stair, and you know it was used recently.

7–9:

> You find the hidden stair, but opening it will scrape loudly unless you take time you may not have.

6 or less:

> You find the hidden stair because something below opens it first.

Failure should not mean “you learn nothing; try somewhere else” unless that itself changes the situation.

---

# 19. Time, travel, and pressure

Do not track time unless time matters.

When time matters, track it in fictional terms.

Examples:

* dawn is one hour away
* the tide is rising around the causeway
* the wedding begins when the third bell rings
* the fever will break or kill him before morning
* the soldiers will arrive after they search two more houses

A non-diegetic clock may be used only as table infrastructure, and only if each step corresponds to a visible or knowable fictional change.

Good clock:

```text
The manor burns:
1. Smoke fills the upper hall.
2. The west stairs collapse.
3. The nursery catches.
4. The roof falls in.
```

Bad clock:

```text
Danger: 0/6
```

Travel should focus on choices, discoveries, costs, and changed circumstances. Do not roll for every day of movement unless each result changes the fiction.

---

# 20. Referee principles

The referee should follow these principles.

## Say what is true

Describe the situation honestly according to what is established, prepared, or implied.

## Keep the fiction visible

Consequences should appear as things characters can perceive, suffer, use, or respond to.

## Roll late

Let players gather information, make plans, use tools, and change the situation before dice appear.

## Resolve once

One roll should produce a playable outcome. Do not require a second roll to find out what the first roll meant.

## Make failure active

A failed roll should introduce action, pressure, revelation, loss, movement, or transformation.

## Use the characters’ truths

A character’s background, relationships, fears, injuries, tools, and promises are part of the world. Bring them into consequences.

## Do not protect plans

If the players’ choices would change the situation, let the situation change.

## Do not punish curiosity

Make investigation dangerous, costly, revealing, or transformative. Do not make it empty.

## Name consequences plainly

“The knife goes into your side” is better than “take harm.”
“The priest now knows you lied” is better than “mark suspicion.”

---

# 21. Player principles

Players should follow these principles.

## Act from the situation

Say what your character does, not which mechanic you want to trigger.

## Treat consequences as real

A failed roll is not a pause or a mistake. It is the world changing.

## Use what is true

Your belongings, wounds, relationships, memories, promises, and fears are tools and constraints.

## Ask about the fiction

Useful questions include:

* What can I see from here?
* Does the guard seem frightened or loyal?
* How far away is the door?
* What would I need to cross safely?
* Who in this room looks uncertain?
* What does my old military training tell me?

## Let the character change

If the fiction wounds, frightens, tempts, exposes, redeems, or transforms your character, write that down.

---

# 22. Advancement

Characters do not gain levels.

At the end of a session, each player may change the sheet if the fiction justifies it.

Use these prompts:

* What did you learn to do?
* Who now trusts, fears, hates, or needs you?
* What wound, fear, debt, or promise follows you?
* What belief changed?
* What object, title, secret, or responsibility did you gain?
* What part of your old life is no longer true?

A character may add, remove, or rewrite one truth when play has made the change undeniable.

Examples:

* “I served the crown faithfully” becomes “I deserted when I learned what the crown buried.”
* “The dockworkers trust me” becomes “The dockworkers think I sold them out.”
* “I cannot bear enclosed spaces” becomes “I can enter the dark if Mara is with me.”
* “I know only village medicine” becomes “I have learned the plague-signs of the old empire.”

Advancement is not increased power. It is changed fictional position.

---

# 23. Campaign record

Keep a shared record of facts that must remain stable between sessions.

Use headings like:

```text
People:
Places:
Promises:
Threats:
Open questions:
Changed states:
```

Examples:

```text
People:
- Captain Voss opened the gate after Teren took public responsibility.
- The bell-ringer child is still missing.

Places:
- The west road is flooded.
- The chapel crypt is open.

Promises:
- Mara owes the ash-spirit one secret.
- The refugees promised to speak for the characters at trial.

Threats:
- The city plague is already inside the walls.
- Something wearing Father Caldus’s face was seen near the well.

Open questions:
- Who stole the bell-clapper?
- Why does the plague spare children?
```

The campaign record is not a score sheet. It preserves continuity so the fiction can remain coherent.

---

# 24. Optional oracle for solo or low-referee play

When no referee is available, or when the referee wants to disclaim an answer, ask a closed question about the fiction.

Examples:

* Is the door guarded?
* Does she recognize me?
* Has the bridge already collapsed?
* Is the ghost angry?
* Did the letter arrive first?

Roll 2d6.

## Oracle answer

|      Roll | Answer                                                        |
| --------: | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
|       10+ | Yes, and something else is also true.                         |
|       7–9 | Yes, but there is a complication or cost.                     |
|       4–6 | No, but something useful, revealing, or dangerous is present. |
| 3 or less | No, and the situation worsens.                                |

The oracle must answer as a fictional event or fact, not as a prompt that requires another procedure.

Good:

> “Yes, the door is guarded, and the guard is arguing with someone you know.”

Good:

> “No, the bridge has not collapsed, but the supports are groaning under floodwater.”

Bad:

> “Introduce a complication.”

Bad:

> “Generate a theme.”

If the oracle answer is too vague, translate it immediately into something the characters could observe, discover, or be affected by.

---

# 25. Safety and table agreement

Before play, agree on material that should be excluded, softened, or handled off-screen.

This is table infrastructure, not part of the fiction, but it supports the conditions for play.

Use whatever safety tools the table prefers. Keep them simple and available.

Also agree on the game’s orientation:

> We are here to treat the fiction as the shared object of play.
> We will make choices from inside the situation.
> We will let consequences change the world and the characters.
> We will not treat the rules as a tactical puzzle or a way to win.

---

# 26. Running the first session

To begin quickly, the referee prepares a charged situation, not a plot.

Prepare:

```text
A place:
A present danger:
Three people who want different things:
One thing hidden from the characters:
One thing already going wrong:
Three sensory details:
A question you do not know the answer to:
```

Example:

```text
A place:
The river gate of a walled city at midnight.

A present danger:
Refugees outside the gate may carry plague; something worse follows them.

Three people who want different things:
- Captain Voss wants the gate closed until dawn.
- Sister Elian wants the sick brought inside.
- A masked merchant wants one particular child kept outside.

One thing hidden from the characters:
The plague is already inside the city.

One thing already going wrong:
The river is rising against the lower gate.

Three sensory details:
- Wet rope creaking in the dark.
- Lanterns reflected in floodwater.
- A bell ringing once, then stopping.

A question you do not know the answer to:
Who will the characters choose to save first?
```

Start with the characters present and under pressure.

Do not decide how the situation ends.

# 27. Design compliance summary

This ruleset is built to match the fiction-engine framework in the following ways:

* **Every rule points back to fictional state.** Traits, harm, equipment, relationships, magic, time, and advancement all matter because they change what is true in the imagined world.
* **Resolution is thin.** One 2d6 roll converts fictional intent into fictional change.
* **Outcomes continue play.** Every result produces a new situation rather than a dead end.
* **There are no abstract pressure economies.** No hit points, stress points, metacurrency, action points, or generic supply pools.
* **Non-diegetic tools are limited to support infrastructure.** Safety tools, campaign records, and optional clocks exist only to preserve coherence, continuity, or consent.
* **Authority is explicit.** The referee maintains coherence and consequence; players state intent and method; the oracle can replace or supplement the referee only by producing concrete fictional facts.
* **Character growth is fictional, not numerical.** Characters change because events change them.
