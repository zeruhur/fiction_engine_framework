# Above the Situation

*This essay is the fourth in a series examining what the fiction engine paradigm cannot produce and why. It builds on the limits framing essay "The Bet" and on the companion essays "Four Designs, One Framework" and "Faction and Fiction." It is readable as a standalone following the framework.*

## What Strategic Depth Requires and Why the Engine Refuses It

The first three essays in this sub-series have described three mechanisms of mismatch. The fiction engine *inverts* dread's structural requirements: where dread needs foreclosure, the engine produces continuation. It is *structurally incompatible* with mystery: where investigation needs convergence toward a fixed answer, the engine produces open succession. It *actively prevents* procedural tension by succeeding at its own core commitment: where tension needs a visible, costly system, the engine makes the system disappear.

Each of those mismatches involves the fiction engine's outputs. What it produces — outcomes as continuations, procedures that recede, fiction that stays moving — is wrong for those experiences in different ways. The mismatch with strategic optimization is different in kind. It does not concern what the fiction engine produces. It concerns where the player is positioned relative to the fiction.

A fiction engine embeds the player in the current situation. Strategic depth requires the player above it. The incompatibility is positional, and positional incompatibility cannot be addressed by revising procedures.

## The Positional Distinction

Two positions are available relative to any fictional situation.

Inside the situation: the player's primary object of attention is the current fictional state — the characters present, the conditions in play, the pressures of this moment. Decisions emerge from engagement with that state. The player asks what the character would do here, now, given what is. Planning happens from within: what would make sense to attempt, given everything established so far.

Above the situation: the player's primary object of attention is the game state as a decision tree. The current fictional moment is an input to a calculation rather than a field of engagement. The player models the available branches, evaluates their payoffs, tracks constraints that extend past the current moment. Planning happens from outside: what is the optimal move given the full state and the objective function.

These are not cognitive styles applied to the same activity. They are different activities. The player inside the situation responds; the player above it calculates. The distinction between responding and calculating is not a preference difference. It is a difference in what the player is doing and what kind of object they are doing it with.

The fiction engine assumes the first position. Its design logic follows from that assumption: thin procedure, outcomes as continuations, fictional states as the primary content of play. Everything the framework identifies as load-bearing fiction engine design — the immediate legibility of fictional states, the minimal classification step between intent and outcome, the resolution cycle that keeps the fiction moving — is optimized for embedded, responsive engagement. Strategic optimization requires the second position, and the fiction engine's design logic is constructed around the first.

## What Optimization Requires

Strategic optimization across multiple turns requires the player to hold the game state as a stable, legible object they can reason about. Three properties follow from this, and the fiction engine's logic works against each.

*State legibility.* The player must be able to read the current state precisely enough to model its future branches. Fictional descriptors convey texture; they resist calculation. "The alliance is strained" and "the city is watchful" communicate conditions the player can respond to from inside the fiction. They do not communicate quantities a player can calculate with. How strained is the alliance? At what point does watchfulness become a concrete obstacle rather than ambient pressure? Strategic optimization requires answers to these questions as quantities or at least as ordinal positions. Impressions are insufficient because they cannot be added, compared, or projected forward.

*State stability.* The player must be able to predict how the state will develop without encountering the fiction engine's characteristic commitment to transformation through surprise. Emergence — the fiction going somewhere nobody planned — is the fiction engine's goal. Predictability is the optimizer's requirement. A game state that keeps transforming in ways the player could not anticipate is not a useful object for multi-turn planning. It is a useful object for embedded, responsive play. These are opposite design commitments.

*Multi-turn visibility.* Strategic optimization requires that the player's current decision be evaluable in terms of its effects several turns forward. The fiction engine's continuation logic — each outcome opens into the next, the situation transforms — means the state beyond the next turn is genuinely uncertain in ways that resist modeling. That uncertainty is the fiction engine's feature. It is the optimizer's obstacle. A player planning six turns ahead inside a fiction engine is not planning; they are guessing, because the fiction will have transformed several times before the plan could execute.

None of these is a superficial property of a design that could be adjusted without changing the paradigm. All three follow from the same commitment: the fiction is primary, procedure is infrastructure, and the player's engagement is with the fiction as it is right now. That commitment is what makes optimization unavailable.

## The Legibility Inversion

The fiction engine's preference for fictional descriptors over numerical interfaces is explicit in the framework and treated as a strength. A fictional descriptor — "she is exhausted and can barely lift her sword" — is better than a stamina score of 3 for a player inside the current situation. It tells them what the character can plausibly attempt right now. It maintains the fiction's texture. It survives the removal of the game record because the condition exists in the world the characters inhabit.

For strategic optimization, the same preference is exactly wrong. "She is exhausted and can barely lift her sword" cannot be added, subtracted, compared across turns, or used to calculate whether resting now or pushing through produces a better state at turn 5. A stamina score of 3 can do all of those things. The numerical interface is inferior for embedded engagement because it requires translation — the player must convert the number into a fictional condition before they know what it means in play. The numerical interface is superior for strategic calculation because that translation is the overhead being paid for legibility as a calculable quantity.

The friction the fiction engine is designed to eliminate — the translation step between system state and fictional meaning — is the friction strategic optimization depends on. The translation step is where the calculation lives. Remove it and you have fiction that is immediately legible to a player inside the situation; the player inside the situation is exactly who you have it for. But you have also removed the object the strategic optimizer was working with.

This is not a coincidence or an oversight. The preference for fictional descriptors is a choice that optimizes for one kind of play by removing the preconditions for another. A designer who wants strategic depth and reaches for fictional descriptors has removed the tool that strategic depth needs.

## The OSG Architecture Revisited

The OSG companion essay identified OSG as sitting above the transparency threshold deliberately, with a procedural architecture designed to produce the information asymmetry and strategic depth the form requires. The action format — Action, Outcome, Leverage — is worth revisiting here as the clearest available case of a fiction-primary design that is also above the threshold specifically because it needs to serve strategic play.

The action format is a translation mechanism. The player plans above the fiction: they assess the strategic situation, identify the objective, evaluate the leverage available. They then express that plan in fictional terms as a submitted action: what the character attempts, what they expect to result, what established fictional fact supports the attempt. The Leverage requirement grounds the strategic move in the accumulated fiction — the plan must be traceable to prior events, to relationships established in play, to conditions the fiction has made real. Strategic thinking above the fiction; fictional expression within it. The RAT checklist evaluates the submission on the fiction engine's terms: Reasonable given what has been established, Actionable as a coherent attempt, Traceable to prior fictional facts.

This architecture does something the fiction engine paradigm cannot natively do: it maintains two positions simultaneously. The planning phase requires the player above the situation. The submission and adjudication phases require the player inside it. The design holds both by separating them into distinct structural moments — not by asking the player to occupy both simultaneously, but by sequencing them so each phase has its own positional demand.

A pure fiction engine has no concept of a planning phase above the fiction. Every engagement the framework describes is embedded: the player states what their character attempts from inside the fictional situation. There is no prior step where the player models the game state from outside it, evaluates branches, considers optimal moves. The fiction engine assumes that step does not exist and builds accordingly. The OSG architecture assumes it does exist and builds a structure to contain it.

The cost the OSG essay identified — the threshold crossing imposes procedural demands that persist above the fiction throughout play, not only at discrete resolution moments — is also the cost the fiction engine cannot absorb. The planning phase is the overhead. It is exactly the kind of step the fiction engine is designed to eliminate, and it cannot be eliminated here because it is where the strategic engagement lives.

## Strategy-as-Fiction and Strategy-as-Optimization

A fiction engine can produce the experience of a character who is a strategist: someone who plans, calculates, schemes, outmaneuvers. This is strategy-as-fiction. The player occupies the perspective of someone who thinks strategically, expressed through fictional choices and fictional outcomes. The character is clever; the player experiences what that cleverness feels like from inside.

This is not strategy-as-optimization: the player actually performing strategic calculation over a game state and making decisions based on that calculation. In strategy-as-fiction, the player is in the fiction, experiencing the character's perspective. In strategy-as-optimization, the player is above the game state, manipulating it toward an objective.

Both can describe themselves as planning. The character planning an infiltration and the player optimizing a position in a 4X game are both "planning." The object of the plan differs — the fiction versus the game state — and that object is what the fiction engine cannot bridge. A player whose character is a master tactician, playing that tactician from inside the fiction, is doing something different from a player who is a master tactician themselves, performing that mastery over the game's state. The fiction engine produces the former. The latter requires a different relationship to the game's state, a different positional orientation, and a different kind of rule that makes that orientation possible.

The distinction matters because it prevents a common misdiagnosis. A fiction engine that allows players to describe tactical decisions is not providing strategic depth. It is providing the fiction of tactical thinking. A player can describe their character devising a careful plan, and the fiction can reflect that plan's cleverness, without the player ever performing the calculation themselves. The calculation is narrated rather than executed. Strategy-as-fiction is available to any fiction engine that allows clever characters. Strategy-as-optimization requires a game state the player can actually calculate with — which means a game state legible as a decision object, not as a shared situation.

## The Wargame Limit

At the far end of this territory are wargames and 4X designs where the fiction has thinned to decoration or disappeared entirely. The optimization logic has gone to completion: strategic reasoning has shaped the entire design toward a legible, calculable state, and the fictional layer — where it remains — is aesthetic rather than functional. Unit names, faction histories, thematic art: these produce texture without serving the state as a fiction engine would define it. The fiction does not transform in response to the players' choices; it is backdrop for the moves they are making in a state space.

The colonization runs in both directions. The fiction engine's logic shapes whatever it touches toward embedded, responsive, fictional engagement — toward a player inside the situation, responding to what is present. Strategic optimization's logic shapes whatever it touches toward legible, calculable, plannable state — toward a player above the situation, modeling what is possible. When these two logics meet in a single design, one tends to dominate. Which one depends on which commitment is more fundamental to what the design is for.

OSG holds both by building an explicit architectural seam: a planning phase above the fiction, a submission phase inside it, an adjudication phase that evaluates the submission on fictional terms. The seam is visible and deliberate. It does not eliminate the tension between the two positional demands; it manages that tension by giving each demand its own structural moment.

A designer who wants strategic depth inside a fictional frame is working against both logics simultaneously, and will need to make the same kind of explicit decision: which phase of play is above the situation, which is inside it, and how does the design mark the boundary. Neither the pure fiction engine nor the pure strategy game provides the hybrid. The hybrid requires designing the seam with the same care brought to everything else — because the seam is where the incompatibility lives, and a seam ignored is a seam that breaks.
