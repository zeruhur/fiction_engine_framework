# The Same Thinness, Different Purposes
## Why OSR/FKR and Fiction Engine Designs Converge Procedurally and Diverge Structurally

*Part of the Fiction Engine companion essay series.*

---

Two traditions arrive at nearly identical procedural profiles — thin rules, situational rulings, minimal overhead, high referee discretion — from different starting points and in service of different things. At the level of play transcript, a 24XX session and an FKR session can be indistinguishable: quick resolutions, a foregrounded situation, a referee making calls rather than consulting tables. A reader encountering both for the first time has reasonable grounds to treat them as the same tradition.

They are not. This essay says precisely why, and why that matters for designers and players who operate in both neighborhoods.

---

## What Each Tradition Is Minimizing Toward

The convergence is real and not accidental. Both traditions are responding to the same diagnosis: heavy procedure gets between the player and what the game is trying to produce. What they disagree about is what the game is trying to produce.

OSR/FKR thinness is in service of world interaction. The procedure gets out of the way so the player can engage with the world as a coherent problem space: what is actually here, what can I actually do, what are the real consequences of this action inside a world that operates according to its own logic. The fiction is the medium through which that engagement happens. The world's logic — its physical and social properties, its established facts and causal structures — is the object toward which the player is oriented. A thin procedure is valuable because it does not obscure the world. The world remains the primary object of attention.

Fiction engine thinness is in service of fictional flow. The procedure gets out of the way so the fiction can keep moving: what changes, what opens, how does the situation transform into the next situation. The world's logic is a constraint on plausibility — outcomes must be consistent with what the fiction has established — but it is not the primary object. Fictional momentum is the object. A thin procedure is valuable because it does not interrupt the fiction. The fiction remains the primary object of attention.

These are not the same minimization target. Both traditions are minimizing overhead. The overhead they are minimizing is getting between the player and different things.

---

## Where They Look Identical

The procedural surface is genuinely shared. Both traditions favor rulings over rules: the referee applies judgment to the specific situation rather than consulting a codified procedure. Both favor minimal tracking: the fiction carries the relevant states without extensive administrative record. Both treat risk as the primary trigger for resolution: unopposed actions succeed, and the mechanics activate only where something genuinely hangs in the balance. Both value high referee discretion: the person mediating the fiction has authority to make calls that a design document cannot anticipate.

These are not superficial resemblances. They are structural commitments that both traditions share because they are responding to a shared problem. Codified procedure accumulates weight. That weight draws the player's attention away from the game's primary object — whether that object is the world or the fiction — and toward the system itself. Both traditions refuse that accumulation for the same reason, even if the object being protected differs.

The result is that both traditions sit below, or near, the transparency threshold. In each case the procedural layer is thin enough that the player's attention passes through it rather than pausing at it. The system does not demand conscious engagement in order for play to advance. The shared commitment to thinness produces a shared threshold position.

---

## Where They Diverge Under Pressure

The divergence becomes visible at the moment of ruling under ambiguity — where the situation is genuinely unclear and the referee must decide what happens. The question each tradition asks is different.

OSR/FKR: what would actually happen here, given the world's established logic? The ruling is an inference about the world. Getting it right means being consistent with what the world is — its physical properties, its causal history, the behavioral logic of its inhabitants. Fictional momentum is a secondary consideration; world-coherence is primary. A ruling that halts play because the world's logic requires it is a correct ruling. The player has encountered the world operating correctly.

Fiction engine: what new fictional state does this produce, and does play continue? The ruling is a transformation of the situation. Getting it right means the fiction keeps moving and the outcome is a continuation rather than a suspension. World-coherence is a constraint on plausibility — an outcome that contradicts established fictional facts fails the Reasonable criterion — but it is not a trump card. A ruling that halts play is a failed ruling regardless of its world-logic justification. The fiction has stalled, and a stalled fiction is a design problem.

Two designs with identical procedural thinness produce different rulings under the same ambiguous circumstances because they are applying different criteria for what makes a ruling correct. The thinness is the same. What it is optimizing for is not.

## The Locked Door

A concrete case that does not require knowledge of any specific game.

A locked door in an OSR design: the player engages with the door as a world-object. What is it made of? How is it locked? What tools are available, and what do those tools actually accomplish given the door's physical properties? The resolution procedure produces a result consistent with what the world would do. A crowbar applied to a heavy iron door in a stone frame produces different outcomes than the same tool applied to a wooden door with a simple latch. The player who outsmarts the door — who reads the world correctly and acts on that reading — has engaged correctly with the design.

A locked door in a fiction engine: the resolution procedure produces a new fictional state. On a strong success, the door opens cleanly. On a partial success, it opens but alerts someone nearby. On a failure, something changes — the lock breaks, someone approaches, a new condition enters the fiction. Each result is a continuation. The question is not what the door would do in strict physical terms but what the situation becomes. A complication on a partial success is not a failure of world-logic; it is the fiction producing its next moment.

Same door. Same thin procedure. The OSR referee asking "what would this door actually do?" and the fiction engine referee asking "what does this situation become?" are applying different criteria even if the mechanics they reach for are indistinguishable from the outside.

## What Each Tradition Accepts as a Cost

OSR/FKR accepts that world-logic can produce situations where the fiction stalls — where the correct answer is "no, and nothing changes yet, because the world's logic does not support an outcome here." The player has not found the solution. The world has not yielded. Play continues with the player searching for another approach, gathering more information, or accepting that this particular path is closed. That is not a design failure. It is the world operating correctly, and the experience of encountering a coherent world that resists the player is part of what the tradition is designed to produce.

A fiction engine accepts that fictional momentum can override strict world-consistency. The ruling that favors a transformation over the most rigorously logical outcome — that introduces a complication rather than a flat refusal, that finds the continuation rather than the stopping point — is making a choice the world might not strictly mandate. That is not a design failure. It is the fiction operating correctly, and the experience of a situation that keeps changing and opening is part of what the paradigm is designed to produce.

Neither tradition is wrong about what it accepts. They are optimized for different things, and the optimization produces different tolerances for the other's cost. An OSR player who finds that a fiction engine will not let the world simply resist is encountering the paradigm working as intended. A fiction engine player who finds that an OSR world stops moving until they solve a problem is encountering a different paradigm working as intended. The incompatibility is not mutual deficiency. It is different design goals producing different acceptable failure modes.

## Practical Consequences for Designers

The distinction produces specific divergences in design decisions.

**Rulings.** An OSR/FKR designer builds referee judgment toward world-coherence: the well-made ruling is the one that is consistent with the world's established logic and would not surprise a neutral observer who understood how the world works. A fiction engine designer builds referee judgment toward fictional continuation: the well-made ruling is the one that produces a new fictional state and keeps play moving, within the constraints of plausibility. The criteria for a good ruling differ, and the referee who has internalized one set of criteria will produce different calls than one who has internalized the other, even when running the same thin procedure.

**Failure states.** In OSR/FKR, failure can close options legitimately. The player who fails to pick the lock has not picked the lock; the world does not owe them an alternative path through. They must find another way, and the design does not guarantee that another way exists or is accessible. In a fiction engine, failure must open something. The framework states this plainly: a result that leaves the fiction suspended rather than transformed indicates a design problem. Failure that produces "nothing changes" is a failure of the design, not a correct expression of world-logic.

**Fictional description.** Both traditions value it, but for different reasons. In OSR/FKR, description communicates world-properties the player needs in order to engage with the problem: the texture of the door, the sounds from behind it, the visible condition of the hinges. The player is reading the world through the description in order to act on it. In a fiction engine, description is the fictional state: the output of play, the condition the situation is now in, the shared imagined space that all participants hold in common. The description is not instrumental to solving a problem; it is what the resolution produces.

**Player orientation.** A player in an OSR/FKR design is primarily asking what can I do here — reading the world in order to engage with it as a problem space. A player in a fiction engine is primarily asking what happens next — reading the situation in order to make a choice that transforms it. Both are engaged with the fiction. The direction of attention differs, and that difference shapes what a well-played session looks like in each tradition.

## Practical Consequences for Players

The incompatibility mode is the most practically significant consequence of the distinction, because the surface similarity of the two traditions makes the divergence invisible until it causes friction.

Two players at the same table: one oriented toward world-engagement, one oriented toward fictional flow, both playing a thin-procedure game that could belong to either tradition. The player oriented toward the world is making decisions based on what the world would actually support. The player oriented toward fictional flow is making decisions based on what would produce the most interesting continuation. Both are playing correctly by their own tradition's criteria. They are producing rulings — and expecting rulings — by different standards, and neither can easily name why the other player's calls feel slightly off.

The problem is not that one player is wrong. It is that the procedural thinness the design shares with both traditions has made the divergence invisible. In a heavily proceduralized game, the rules encode the tradition: a codified move list signals PbtA's approach; a detailed world-interaction resolution table signals OSR priorities. Thin procedure does not encode the tradition in the same way. The rules do not announce which object they are protecting.

This is where the distinction is most practically useful: not to adjudicate which tradition is correct but to give players and referees a way to identify which orientation a given design is asking for, and to recognize when a table is applying two different answers to that question simultaneously. A referee who knows they are running a fiction engine can say "that ruling halts the fiction, and a halted fiction is a design problem — let me find the continuation." A referee who knows they are running an OSR game can say "the world genuinely resists here, and that resistance is the experience the game is producing." Neither answer is wrong in its context. The confusion arises from not knowing which context you are in.

---

## Two Traditions, One Solution, Different Problems

The convergence at the procedural level is not a coincidence and not an error. Both traditions arrived at thinness because thinness solves a real problem: heavy procedure is an obstacle. They diagnosed the same problem and drew the same procedural conclusion. What differs is what the procedure was obstructing in each case.

In OSR/FKR, the procedure was getting between the player and the world. Removing it gave the world back its legibility as a coherent problem space. In fiction engine design, the procedure was getting between the player and the fiction's momentum. Removing it gave the fiction back its capacity to keep changing. The solution worked in both cases, for different reasons.

A designer who knows which problem their thinness is solving can make deliberate decisions when the two traditions pull toward different rulings: when the world's logic suggests stopping and the fiction's momentum suggests continuing, when description serves as information-for-problem-solving versus information-as-fictional-state, when failure should close options versus open complications.

These are not questions a design document can answer in advance for every situation. They are orientation questions — the kind that get answered in the moment of the ruling, and that get answered consistently only when the person making the ruling knows which tradition they are working inside.

Knowing which problem your thinness is for is the beginning of knowing what to do with it.
